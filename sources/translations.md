# Translations

This file records which English translation is used for each text in the corpus (`sources/corpus.md`), its copyright status, where it can be checked, and where the choice of translation materially changes the meaning.

**Version 1.0, 2026-10-13.**

## Principles

1. **Public domain first.** We prefer translations that are in the public domain, so that anyone may quote and check them freely.
2. **Freely accessible second.** Where no adequate public-domain translation exists, we use a copyrighted translation that is free to read online, and we quote it only briefly.
3. **The original language decides.** Where translations disagree, we check the original (Hebrew, Aramaic, Greek, Latin, Arabic, Sanskrit, Pali, Prakrit, Classical Chinese, Gurmukhi, Avestan, Pahlavi) and say which reading we follow.
4. **UK publication.** The King James Version is under perpetual Crown copyright in the United Kingdom. For Bible quotations we therefore prefer the World English Bible (British Edition), which is in the public domain worldwide.
5. **Copyright statuses below are our best reading.** Several could not be confirmed directly in this session; these are marked "to confirm". Anyone who finds an error should say so.

**Where translation changes meaning**
- *Dharma* is rendered "righteousness" (Ganguli), "law", "duty" or "religion". "Law" makes ethical passages sound legal.
- Bühler and other older translators render *varṇa* as "caste", merging the four classes (*varṇa*) with birth groups (*jāti*). This can make counter-evidence look clearer or blurrier than the Sanskrit warrants.
- Mahabharata numbering differs between Ganguli's edition (based on the older printed text) and the Pune critical edition. We always give both.
- **Mahabharata 13.114.8 (critical edition)**: *pratikūla* means "contrary, disagreeable, hostile". Ganguli's "injurious" narrows the rule to harm; "disagreeable" widens it to anything unwelcome. Both support Article I's floor.
- **Bhagavad Gita 6.32**: *ātmaupamyena* read ethically means "by comparison with oneself" (taking one's own pleasure and pain as the measure for others). Read through Advaita Vedānta it means "by the likeness of the Self" in all. Translators who capitalise "Self" push the verse towards metaphysics. All readings conclude that others' pain counts as one's own.

**Where translation changes meaning**
- **Hebrew *re'a*** (Leviticus 19:18) ranges from "neighbour" (JPS 1917) to "fellow" or "companion" and, in some modern renderings, "fellow Israelite". The choice silently decides the scope question, so we use a neutral word and discuss scope openly.
- **Hebrew *ger*** (Exodus 23:9; Leviticus 19:33 to 34) means "resident foreigner" or "stranger" in its plain sense; later rabbinic sources often read it as "convert". A translation that says "convert" narrows the verse.
- **Hebrew *kamokha*** ("as yourself"): Ibn Ezra and Ramban read Leviticus 19:18 as "love *for* your fellow" (wish for them the good you wish for yourself), not as a demand for equal feeling.
- **Aramaic *chavrakh*** (Shabbat 31a): "your fellow" or "your neighbour". "Your friend" would wrongly narrow a saying addressed to a non-Jew.
- Verse numbering differs between Hebrew and English Bibles in places (for example Exodus 22:20 Hebrew = 22:21 English). We give the Hebrew numbering, with the English where it differs.

**Where translation changes meaning**
- Old Avestan, the language of the Gathas, is among the hardest scriptural languages to translate. Philological translators (Mills, Insler, Humbach) and reformist translators (Taraporewala, Irani, Jafarey) often disagree on whole stanzas. Reformist versions tend to read universal ethical meanings into unclear lines. We give the philological reading as the base and note reformist readings where they differ.
- ***drəgvant*** ("follower of the Lie", variously "wicked", "deceitful", "liar"): whether it names a moral type, which anyone can become by choice, or a rival group decides the scope of Gathic ethical teaching.
- **Yasna 33.2 and 47.4.** Mills and Bartholomae commend harming the follower of the Lie (33.2) and being ill-disposed to him (47.4). Modern Zoroastrian translators divide: the Zarathushtrian Assembly (after Humbach) has "foils the wrongful" (33.2) and a statement of what one "can" do (47.4); D. J. Irani makes the object of 33.2 "Evil" but keeps rejection of the wicked at 47.4; F. Azargoshasb keeps "fighting the wicked" (33.2) and contempt (47.4). So it is not accurate to say that reformist translations as a group soften these verses.
- **Yasna 46.6.** Every translation found has "very good to" the follower of the Lie (forbidden); the difference is in how "very good to" is read (general kindness, or patronage and protection). The "shielding" reading is D. J. Irani's, not Taraporewala's.
- **Yasna 43.1** (*uštā ahmāi yahmāi uštā kahmāicīt*): reformist renderings make it a reciprocity teaching ("happiness to the one who brings happiness to others"); philological renderings read it as a blessing on whoever receives Mazda's favour, with no reciprocity.
- West's Pahlavi translations are the only freely available English. Popular quotation collections paraphrase him loosely and disagree on references (for example "94.5" or "95.5"). We cite only West's own text and numbering.

**Where translation changes meaning**
- ***Sabbe*** ("all") and ***satta*** ("beings") cover all sentient beings, animals included. Müller's "All men tremble" (Dhammapada 129) wrongly narrows the scope.
- **Dhammapada 129 to 130**, *attānaṃ upamaṃ katvā*: literally "having made oneself the comparison". Renderings include "putting oneself in the place of another" and "drawing the parallel to yourself". All keep the negative form.
- ***na haneyya na ghātaye***: the verb covers both "strike" and "kill". "Kill" limits the verse; "hurt" widens it to harm in general.
- ***Attā*** ("self") in these ethical verses is the ordinary word for oneself. Translations that write a capital "Self" create a false clash with the teaching of not-self.
- The popular English line attributed to Udānavarga 5.18 ("Hurt not others in ways that you yourself would find hurtful") is a free rendering whose source we have not traced. We do not quote it as a translation.

**Where translation changes meaning**
- The fourfold list *prāṇa, bhūta, jīva, sattva* (Jacobi: "breathing, existing, living, sentient creatures") covers all living beings. Any rendering as "men" or "people" wrongly narrows the scope.
- ***Hiṃsā*** is defined in the Tattvārtha Sūtra by careless or passion-driven action, so it covers intention and mental harm, not only killing.
- **Sūtrakṛtāṅga I.10.3 and I.11.33**: Jacobi's "as he himself would be treated" interprets a compact Prakrit phrase of self-comparison (literally closer to "regarding beings as equal to oneself"). The Golden Rule logic survives either way, but part of the English wording is Jacobi's.
- **Ācārāṅga 1.5.5** ("you are the one you intend to kill"): readers divide between "the victim is like you" and "the harm returns to you". Only the first is a statement of reciprocity in the Golden Rule sense.

**Where translation changes meaning**
- **恕 *shu*** is rendered "reciprocity" (Legge), "consideration", "empathy", "understanding" or "likening to oneself". "Reciprocity" can wrongly suggest tit-for-tat exchange. The tradition's own gloss is "measuring others by oneself" (Xing Bing: 忖己度物).
- **施 *shi*** in 勿施於人: "do not do to others" (Legge) or "do not impose on others". "Impose" is closer and slightly stronger.
- **仁 *ren***: "perfect virtue" (Legge) makes it sound like a summit; "humaneness" or "goodness" is closer to an everyday quality.
- **以直報怨** (Analects 14.34): Legge's "recompense injury with justice" can sound like retribution; 直 means a straight, fair response.
- No translation choice turns the negative rule into something else: every version keeps "what you do not want for yourself, do not impose on others".

| Text | Translation used | Status | Where to check |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tao Te Ching | James Legge, *The Tao Teh King*, SBE vol. 39 (1891) | Public domain | ctext.org/dao-de-jing (with the Chinese and manuscript variants); sacred-texts.com/tao |

**Where translation changes meaning**
- Classical Chinese is terse and has no tense, number or mood. Translators often turn descriptions of the sage into commands, or the reverse. Legge notes, for example, that chapter 63 describes the way of the Tao rather than giving an order.
- **Tao Te Ching 49**: Legge glosses 善者 as people who are "good (to me)", which frames the line as kindness that does not depend on return; read without the gloss it means simply "good people and not-good people". Either way the scope is everyone. The Mawangdui manuscript reads "the sage constantly has no mind of his own" rather than "has no fixed mind".
- **Tao Te Ching 63**, 報怨以德: "recompense injury with kindness" (Legge) or "requite resentment with virtue". A modern scholarly conjecture moves the phrase to chapter 79, where it could be read as questioned.
- **Tao Te Ching 54**, 以身觀身: Legge reads it as observing the effect of cultivated virtue in a person. Some popular modern versions ("use yourself to understand others") create a Golden Rule reading that the classical commentaries do not support. We do not use those versions.
- **Tao Te Ching 5**, 不仁: "not benevolent" or "impartial" (Legge) versus "ruthless" in some modern versions. The choice changes how strong a counter-passage it appears.
- **Zhuangzi 18**, 以己養養鳥 ("nourishing the bird with what nourishes oneself"): stable across translations.

**Where translation changes meaning**
- Greek *prohairesis* is "will" (Long), "moral purpose" (Oldfather) or "choice". This affects how strongly the Stoic claim that "nothing outside your choice can harm you" reads.
- *Kathēkon* is "duty", "appropriate action" or "proper function". "Duty" makes Stoic ethics look more rule-based than it is.
- **Seneca, Letter 47.11**: the Latin *sic cum inferiore vivas quemadmodum tecum superiorem velis vivere* means "live with your inferior as you would wish your superior to live with you". Gummere's "treat your inferiors as you would be treated by your betters" sharpens it into a familiar formula and adds a class colouring ("betters"). We quote the Latin beside the English.

- **Divine names** (Hari, Prabh, Ram, Allah, Khasam, Sahib) are often all rendered "Lord". This hides the deliberate use of Hindu and Muslim names for one God, which bears on the universal scope of the ethics.

- ***Sarbat da bhala*** in the Ardas ("the good of all") is unambiguously universal.

**Where translation changes meaning**
- All the texts are English originals, so the issue is between *versions*, not translations. The 2002 Declaration says "Humanism is ethical" (a description); the 2022 text says "Humanists strive to be ethical" (a commitment).
- Member organisations publish their own translations, which vary slightly. For example, a German humanist body's English renders "compatible with the rights of others" as "in harmony with the rights of others", which sounds more cooperative and less like a limit. We cite the Humanists International English.
- Older texts (1933, 1952) use "man" for humanity. This is period usage, not a limit on scope.
