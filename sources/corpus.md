# Source Corpus

This file fixes which texts count as evidence for each of the thirteen traditions. It confirms or refines the starting corpus in the project brief (Section 4). Every change is listed with its reason.

**Version 1.0, 2026-10-13.** The corpus can be changed later, but only openly: any change must be recorded here and in `CHANGELOG.md`, with reasons, and must never be made to rescue or sink a particular article.

## How the tiers work

- **Tier 1 (primary):** core scripture or founding texts.
- **Tier 2 (authoritative):** major traditional commentary, or texts that the tradition itself treats as carrying authority.
- **The tier rule.** A Tier 2 text may support or explain Tier 1. It may not be the *only* evidence for a tradition unless that tradition genuinely treats the text as primary. Where we judge that a Tier 2 text *is* treated as primary, this file says so in advance, with the reason, under "Treated as primary".
- **Reconciling authorities.** Some Tier 2 texts are listed only so that we can show how a tradition itself explains a hard passage (the brief's "no cherry-picking" rule). They are marked "for reconciliation". They never count as the main evidence for an article.
- **Branches.** Where branches of a tradition accept different texts, we name the branch. An article must not rest on a text that a major branch rejects, unless another text that branch accepts teaches the same thing.

## 1. Hinduism

**Tier 1**
- Vedas (śruti): the four Saṃhitās (Ṛg, Yajur, Sāma, Atharva) with their Brāhmaṇas and Āraṇyakas.
- Upanishads: principally the *mukhya* (principal) Upanishads commented on by the classical teachers (Īśā, Kena, Kaṭha, Praśna, Muṇḍaka, Māṇḍūkya, Taittirīya, Aitareya, Chāndogya, Bṛhadāraṇyaka, Śvetāśvatara). Later and minor Upanishads carry less weight.
- Bhagavad Gita (critical edition of the Mahabharata, 6.23 to 6.40).
- Brahma Sutras (Bādarāyaṇa). *Added.*

**Tier 2**
- Mahabharata (*itihāsa*), especially the Śānti and Anuśāsana Parvas, its main teaching books on right conduct.
- Ramayana of Vālmīki.
- Dharmaśāstra and Dharmasūtra literature: Manusmṛti, Yājñavalkya Smṛti, and the Dharmasūtras of Āpastamba, Gautama, Baudhāyana and Vasiṣṭha. **Flagged as contested** (see below).
- Purāṇas, especially the Viṣṇu and Bhāgavata Purāṇas. *Added.*
- Classical commentaries on Tier 1 (Śaṅkara, Rāmānuja, Madhva), for reconciliation. *Added.*
- Tamil devotional canons (Śaiva Tirumuṟai; Nālāyira Divya Prabandham), supporting only.

**Not counted for Hinduism:** the Tirukkural. Recorded as context only.

**Reasons for changes**
1. *Brahma Sutras added.* The Vedānta schools, which shape most learned Hinduism, name three foundations (*prasthāna-trayī*): the Upanishads, the Brahma Sutras and the Gita. Listing two of the three is incomplete.
2. *Principal Upanishads specified.* Over a hundred texts are called Upanishads, many late and sectarian. Naming the principal ones stops a minor text being cited as core scripture.
3. *Purāṇas added to Tier 2.* They are among the most used authoritative texts in living Hinduism, and some Vaiṣṇava traditions treat the Bhāgavata as near-primary. (The Arya Samaj rejects them.)
4. *Commentators added for reconciliation.* The method requires us to show how a tradition itself reconciles hard passages; for Hinduism these teachers, together with the rule that revealed śruti outranks remembered smṛti, are the main reconciling authorities.
5. *Dharmaśāstra widened and kept as contested.* Manusmṛti is not the only law book and was not always the most used in practice (Yājñavalkya and its commentary, the Mitākṣarā, were more influential in classical legal practice). Its authority is strongly contested within the tradition: B. R. Ambedkar publicly burned it in 1927; anti-caste movements reject it; Gandhi accepted it only where it agreed with truth and non-violence; the Arya Samaj accepts it only where it agrees with the Vedas. The tradition's own rule that smṛti yields to śruti where they conflict is recorded so that this contest can be represented fairly.
6. *Tirukkural not counted.* Its religious affiliation is genuinely disputed (Jain authorship is widely argued; Hindu, Buddhist and non-sectarian claims also exist). It is not in the Sanskrit śruti or smṛti or the Tamil Śaiva canon. Counting it for Hinduism would risk resting a Hindu verdict on a text many Hindus do not treat as their scripture, and would risk counting the same text twice.

**Note on the Mahabharata.** The Gita, a Tier 1 text, sits inside the Mahabharata, and the tradition calls *itihāsa* a "fifth Veda". We keep the Mahabharata in Tier 2, as the brief proposes, but record that its explicit teachings on right conduct carry near-primary weight in the tradition. This affects how the tier rule applies (see Article I).

## 2. Judaism

**Tier 1**
- Tanakh (Hebrew Bible, Masoretic Text): Torah, Nevi'im (Prophets), Ketuvim (Writings).

**Tier 2**
- Mishnah (including Pirkei Avot).
- Tosefta. *Added.*
- Tannaitic halakhic midrash: Mekhilta de-Rabbi Yishmael (Exodus), Sifra (Leviticus), Sifrei (Numbers, Deuteronomy). *Added.*
- Babylonian Talmud (Bavli) and Jerusalem Talmud (Yerushalmi). *Both named.*
- Targumim (Aramaic translations): Onkelos; Targum Pseudo-Jonathan as a supporting witness. *Added.*
- Aggadic midrash (for example Genesis Rabbah, Avot de-Rabbi Natan), for clarification.
- Classical commentators and codes (Rashi, Ibn Ezra, Ramban, Maimonides' Mishneh Torah, Sefer HaChinukh, Shulchan Arukh), **for reconciliation only**.

**Excluded:** the Apocrypha (for example Tobit, Ben Sira). They are not in the rabbinic canon.

**Treated as primary:** for Rabbinic Judaism (Orthodox, Conservative or Masorti in principle) the Mishnah and Talmud are the Oral Torah and are effectively primary for practice. Karaite Judaism rejects the Oral Torah and accepts only the Tanakh. Reform and Reconstructionist Judaism treat Talmud and codes as valued but not binding. For these reasons, Tier 1 evidence matters for Judaism, and an article should not rest on the Talmud alone.

**Reasons for changes**
1. Both Talmuds are authoritative, and the Jerusalem Talmud holds an independent record of the teaching on Leviticus 19:18.
2. The Sifra and other tannaitic midrashim come from the same period and carry the same authority as the Mishnah. The Sifra holds the earliest record of Rabbi Akiva's teaching that "love your fellow as yourself" is a great principle of the Torah.
3. The Targumim record the tradition's own rendering of key verses.
4. The method requires reconciliation by the tradition's own authorities; in Rabbinic Judaism these are the classical commentators and codifiers. They clarify and never stand alone.
5. Tobit 4:15 (a negative form of the Golden Rule) is not usable as Jewish evidence because Tobit is not in the Jewish canon.

## 3. Zoroastrianism

**Tier 1**
- Avesta, Old Avestan layer: the five Gathas (Yasna 28 to 34, 43 to 51) and the Yasna Haptanghaiti (Yasna 35 to 41). Accepted by every branch.
- Avesta, Younger Avestan layer: the rest of the Yasna, the Visperad, the Yashts and the Khorde Avesta. Accepted as scripture by traditionalists.
- Vendidad (Videvdad). **Flagged as contested:** traditionalists treat it as scripture; many reformists treat it as a later priestly text with no binding force.

**Tier 2**
- Zand: the Pahlavi translation and commentary of the Avesta. *Named.*
- Denkard, especially Book 6 (the main collection of ethical wisdom).
- Dadestan-i Denig.
- Shayast-ne-Shayast. *Added.*
- Andarz (advice) literature, such as the Andarz of Adurbad Mahraspandan. *Added.*
- Menog-i Khrad. *Added.*
- Supporting only, lower authority: Bundahishn, Arda Viraf Namag.

**Treated as primary? Disputed by branch.** Traditionalists (the orthodox Parsi priesthood and Iranian traditionalists) accept the whole surviving Avesta and treat the Pahlavi books, above all the Zand, as the authoritative transmitted interpretation of scripture, though not as revelation. Reformists (the "Gathas-only" movement) treat the Gathas as the only binding authority. So Pahlavi evidence counts as primary-level authority for one branch and not for the other. Articles must say which.

**Reasons for changes**
1. Splitting Tier 1 into layers shows which branch a citation binds.
2. The Zand is the tradition's own commentary layer, the closest match to "major traditional commentary".
3. Most explicit Zoroastrian ethical teaching is in the Shayast-ne-Shayast, the Andarz literature and Menog-i Khrad. Leaving them out would understate the tradition.
4. Dadestan-i Denig chapter 94 (West's numbering) is, by West's own note, quoted from the opening of Denkard Book 6, so both places should be cited.

## 4. Buddhism

**Tier 1**
- Pali Canon (Theravada Tipiṭaka): Sutta Pitaka (all five Nikāyas, including the Dhammapada, Udāna, Itivuttaka and Sutta Nipāta), Vinaya Pitaka, and Abhidhamma Pitaka. *Abhidhamma named.*
- Early-discourse parallels in other recensions (the Chinese Āgamas; the Dharmaguptaka and Mūlasarvāstivāda Vinayas), counted only where they match a Pali passage. *Added.*

**Tier 2**
- Major Mahāyāna sūtras (for example the Lotus Sutra, Vimalakīrti, Skill in Means, Mahāparinirvāṇa, the Brahmajāla bodhisattva-precept sutra). **Flagged:** these are primary scripture for Mahāyāna branches but are not accepted by Theravāda. They can establish the Mahāyāna position but cannot stand alone for Buddhism as a whole.
- Udānavarga (Sarvāstivāda verse collection, parallel to the Dhammapada; canonical in the Tibetan Kangyur).
- Theravāda commentaries (Buddhaghosa's *Aṭṭhakathā*, Visuddhimagga). *Added.*
- Milindapañha (para-canonical; printed in the Burmese canon). *Added.*
- Major Mahāyāna treatises (Śāntideva's Bodhicaryāvatāra and Śikṣāsamuccaya; Nāgārjuna's Ratnāvalī; Asaṅga's Bodhisattvabhūmi). *Added.*

**Outside the corpus but searched for counter-evidence:** historical chronicles such as the Mahāvaṃsa.

**Reasons for changes**
1. Theravāda counts all three baskets as canonical.
2. East Asian and Tibetan Buddhists receive the early teaching through Chinese and Tibetan recensions. A Pali-only Tier 1 would quietly favour one branch; using the parallels only where they match keeps the tier rule simple.
3. Commentaries and treatises are the authorities each branch actually uses to explain hard passages.

## 5. Jainism

**Tier 1**
- Śvetāmbara Āgamas: 45 texts for Mūrtipūjaka Śvetāmbaras, 32 for Sthānakavāsī and Terāpanthī Śvetāmbaras. Key texts for conduct: Ācārāṅga Sūtra, Sūtrakṛtāṅga, Upāsakadaśāḥ (lay vows), Uttarādhyayana, Daśavaikālika, Kalpa Sūtra, Āvaśyaka Sūtra. **Not accepted by Digambaras.**
- Digambara primary texts: Ṣaṭkhaṇḍāgama and Kaṣāyapāhuḍa. Kundakunda's works and the Mūlācāra are near-canonical for Digambaras and flagged as branch-specific. *Added.*
- Tattvārtha Sūtra (Umāsvāti or Umāsvāmī). **Moved from Tier 2.** Cite both recensions' numbering (for example Digambara 7.11 = Śvetāmbara 7.6).

**Tier 2**
- Commentaries on the Tattvārtha Sūtra (Śvetāmbara *Svopajña Bhāṣya*; Digambara *Sarvārthasiddhi*, *Tattvārtharājavārttika*, *Ślokavārttika*).
- Śvetāmbara exegetical layers (*Niryukti*, *Bhāṣya*, *Cūrṇi*, *Ṭīkā*), for example Śīlāṅka's commentaries. Accepted by Mūrtipūjakas; Sthānakavāsīs and Terāpanthīs accept only the 32 sūtras as binding.
- Lay-conduct manuals (*śrāvakācāra*): Ratnakaraṇḍaka Śrāvakācāra, Puruṣārthasiddhyupāya (Digambara); Hemacandra's Yogaśāstra (Śvetāmbara).
- Saman Suttam (1974): a modern anthology approved by representatives of all four main sects. Cite the underlying source where possible.

**Reasons for changes**
1. "Agamas" is ambiguous: the Śvetāmbara sub-branches accept different numbers, and Digambaras hold that the original Aṅgas were lost.
2. Without Digambara texts, one of the two main branches would have no Tier 1 text at all.
3. *Tattvārtha Sūtra moved to Tier 1.* It is the only text accepted as authoritative by every Jain branch, and Digambaras treat it as primary scripture (the *Mokṣaśāstra*). Leaving it in Tier 2 would make the one shared text unable to stand on its own.

**Branch rule for articles.** Evidence drawn only from the Śvetāmbara Āgamas does not speak for Digambaras. An article should show either a shared text (the Tattvārtha Sūtra) or a Digambara text teaching the same thing, or record the gap.

## 6. Confucianism

**Tier 1**
- Analects (*Lunyu* 論語).
- Mencius (*Mengzi* 孟子).

**Tier 2**
- Doctrine of the Mean (*Zhongyong* 中庸) and Great Learning (*Daxue* 大學). **Treated as primary:** from Zhu Xi (12th century) onward they form the Four Books with the Analects and Mencius, and were the civil-service examination standard from 1313 to 1905. Under the tier rule, evidence from them may therefore stand alone. They stay in Tier 2 because they are not the recorded words of Confucius or Mencius and their traditional authorship is doubted.
- Classic of Filial Piety (*Xiaojing* 孝經).
- Book of Rites (*Liji* 禮記) and the other Five Classics, where needed. *Added.*
- Standard commentaries: He Yan, Huang Kan and Xing Bing on the Analects (*Lunyu zhushu*); Zhao Qi on the Mencius; Zhu Xi's *Collected Commentaries on the Four Books*. For evidence of the tradition's own definitions and for reconciliation. *Added.*
- Xunzi 荀子. **Flagged as contested:** a major classical thinker, but rejected by Neo-Confucian orthodoxy. Never sole evidence. *Added.*

**Reasons for changes**
1. The method requires the tradition's own authorities for reconciliation; Zhu Xi and the older commentaries are those authorities.
2. For Han to Tang Confucianism the Five Classics were the primary canon; omitting them would misrepresent the tradition.
3. Xunzi is authoritative for some modern Confucians but was officially marginalised in 1530.

**Note on numbering.** ctext.org numbers Analects passages differently from Legge's printed edition in several books (for example ctext 15.24 is Legge XV.23). We give ctext numbers, with Legge's where they differ. The Great Learning's "commentary chapter 10" follows Zhu Xi's arrangement; Wang Yangming's school used the older text order.

## 7. Taoism

**Tier 1**
- Tao Te Ching (*Daodejing* 道德經): the received text of the Wang Bi and Heshang Gong lineages. The Mawangdui silk manuscripts (c. 168 BCE) and the Guodian bamboo slips (c. 300 BCE) are recorded as variants where they change the meaning.
- Zhuangzi 莊子 (Guo Xiang's 33-chapter edition). **The Inner Chapters (1 to 7) carry the most weight.** The Outer (8 to 22) and Miscellaneous (23 to 33) Chapters are canonical but later and by several hands, so the layer is recorded for every citation.

**Tier 2**
- Treatise on Response and Retribution (*Taishang Ganying Pian* 太上感應篇). Kept in Tier 2: it is in the Taoist Canon (*Daozang*) and hugely influential in lay practice, but it is a 12th-century morality book drawing on Confucian and Buddhist as well as Taoist teaching, and no Taoist school treats it as a founding scripture.
- Classical commentaries, for reconciliation: Heshang Gong and Wang Bi on the Tao Te Ching; Guo Xiang (with Cheng Xuanying's sub-commentary) on the Zhuangzi. *Added.*
- Liezi 列子 (honoured as a "true scripture" by the Tang court in 742). **Flagged:** its date and authenticity are disputed, and its Yang Zhu chapter is a disputed, self-centred strand. *Added.*
- Religious Taoist precept texts (for example the Lingbao Ten Precepts; the Precepts of Lord Lao). For the religious Taoist branches, whose ethics are taught mainly through ordination precepts. *Added; not yet searched.*

**Reasons for changes**
1. Only the Zhuangzi's Inner Chapters are confidently early. The sharpest attacks on conventional morality (*ren* and *yi*) are in the Outer Chapters, so the layer matters, especially for counter-evidence.
2. Early manuscripts change some key lines. For example, the Guodian version of chapter 19 reads "abandon artifice and scheming" where the received text reads "abandon benevolence, discard righteousness".
3. The method requires reconciliation by the tradition's own authorities. Heshang Gong, Wang Bi and Guo Xiang are the standard traditional readings, and Heshang Gong is itself transmitted within religious Taoism.
4. *Ganying Pian kept in Tier 2.* Promoting it would let a late, mixed text carry a finding alone.

**Branch note.** The old split between "philosophical" Taoism (*Daojia*: Laozi and Zhuangzi) and "religious" Taoism (*Daojiao*: Celestial Masters, Lingbao, Quanzhen, Zhengyi and others) is now questioned by scholars, but it still shapes which texts different readers accept. Philosophical readers often set aside the Ganying Pian and the precept texts; religious Taoists treat the Tao Te Ching as revealed scripture and also use liturgical and precept texts.

## 8. Stoicism

Stoicism is a philosophical school, not a religion. It has no closed canon and no living authority. Here "Tier 1" means the complete surviving ethical works of recognised Stoic teachers.

**Tier 1**
- Epictetus, *Discourses* (as recorded by Arrian) and *Enchiridion*.
- Marcus Aurelius, *Meditations*.
- Seneca, *Letters to Lucilius* (*Epistulae Morales*).
- Seneca, moral essays: *De Ira*, *De Clementia*, *De Beneficiis*, *De Constantia Sapientis*, *De Vita Beata*, *De Otio*, *De Tranquillitate Animi*, *De Brevitate Vitae*, *De Providentia*, and the *Consolations*. **Added.**

**Tier 2**
- Musonius Rufus, *Lectures* and fragments. (There is a fair case for Tier 1: the text is a student's record of a Stoic teacher's lectures, like Epictetus' *Discourses*. We keep it in Tier 2 because it survives only in excerpts.)
- Epictetus, *Fragments* (mainly from Stobaeus). **Flagged:** many items attributed to Epictetus are of doubtful authenticity. *Added.*
- Early Stoa through ancient reports: Diogenes Laertius, *Lives*, Book 7; Arius Didymus' summary of Stoic ethics (in Stobaeus); Hierocles; Cleanthes' *Hymn to Zeus*. *Added.*
- Cicero, *De Finibus* Book 3 and *De Officiis*. **Flagged:** Cicero was not himself a Stoic, but these books present Stoic ethics. *Added.*

**Excluded:** Seneca's tragedies (Stoic content disputed) and the forged correspondence between Seneca and Paul.

**Reasons for changes**
1. Seneca's moral essays are by the same author, from the same years and with the same authority as his letters. *De Beneficiis* is the main surviving Stoic treatment of giving and return, and *De Ira* and *De Clementia* are the main Stoic texts on anger and punishment.
2. The proposed corpus was entirely Roman Imperial. The founders survive only in reports; these must be available for support and, especially, for counter-evidence.

## 9. Christianity

**Tier 1**
- New Testament (27 books, the same in all major branches).
- Old Testament: the 39 books shared by all branches. The Hebrew Masoretic Text is the reference, with the Greek Septuagint noted where the Orthodox branch treats it as authoritative.
- Deuterocanonical books (Tobit, Judith, Wisdom, Sirach, Baruch, 1 and 2 Maccabees, Greek additions to Esther and Daniel; Orthodox Bibles also include 1 Esdras, 3 Maccabees, the Prayer of Manasseh, Psalm 151). **Tier 1 for Catholic and Orthodox Christians only.** Protestants treat them as Apocrypha. **Rule: no finding for Christianity may rest on a deuterocanonical text alone.**

**Tier 2**
- The ecumenical creeds (Apostles', Nicene). They contain almost no teaching on conduct.
- The Apostolic Fathers, especially the *Didache* (the earliest surviving Christian moral teaching manual, late 1st or early 2nd century). *Added.*
- The Church Fathers, East and West (for example Augustine, John Chrysostom, Basil). *Widened from "only where needed".*
- Branch-specific authorities, each speaking only for its own branch: Catholic (Thomas Aquinas, *Summa Theologiae*; the *Catechism of the Catholic Church*); Orthodox (the Fathers and the canons of the ecumenical councils); Protestant (Luther's Catechisms, the Heidelberg Catechism, the Westminster Confession, the Thirty-Nine Articles). *Added.*

**Reasons for changes**
1. The deuterocanon is scripture for Catholics and Orthodox but not Protestants. Tobit 4:15, the only word-for-word negative Golden Rule in any Christian Bible, is deuterocanonical, so its status must be explicit.
2. The creeds contain little on conduct. The tradition's own reconciliations (on war, punishment and the scope of "neighbour") are found in Augustine, Aquinas and the confessions.

## 10. Islam

**Tier 1**
- Qur'an (accepted by all branches; recitation variants do not affect the passages cited so far).

**Tier 2**
- Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim (Sunni; near-consensus acceptance among Sunni scholars).
- The Forty Hadith of an-Nawawī. **Treated as an anthology:** cite the underlying collection and its grading beside the Forty number (for example Forty no. 13 = Bukhārī 13 = Muslim 45).
- Nahj al-Balāgha (sermons, letters and sayings attributed to ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib; compiled by al-Sharīf al-Raḍī, d. 1015). Shia; highly revered; not treated as authoritative by most Sunni scholars. **Added.**
- Al-Kāfī of al-Kulaynī (Twelver Shia; the foremost of the Four Books, though Shia scholars do not treat every report in it as sound). **Added.**
- Classical commentaries, **for reconciliation only**: Qur'an commentary (*tafsīr*) of al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr; hadith commentaries of al-Nawawī, Ibn Ḥajar and Ibn Rajab. *Added.*
- Optional leads: the other canonical Sunni collections (Abū Dāwūd, al-Tirmidhī, al-Nasāʾī, Ibn Mājah) and the Musnad of Aḥmad, used hadith by hadith with their grading.

**Treated as primary:** both Sunni and Shia legal theory name the Qur'an and the Sunnah (the Prophet's authenticated teaching and example) as the two primary sources. So, under the tier rule, an authentic (*ṣaḥīḥ*) hadith may count even where the Qur'an is silent. The collections differ by branch, and a small Qur'an-only minority rejects hadith altogether, so articles should show Qur'anic evidence wherever it exists.

**Reasons for changes**
1. The proposed Tier 2 was Sunni only. Twelver Shia Muslims do not use Bukhārī and Muslim as their hadith canon; they rely on the Four Books and revere the Nahj al-Balāgha. Without these, Islam would be tested through one branch only.
2. An-Nawawī's collection draws its authority from its sources; some of its hadiths are graded *ḥasan* (good) rather than *ṣaḥīḥ* (sound).
3. The method requires reconciliation by the tradition's own authorities: in Islam, the classical commentators.

## 11. Sikhism

**Tier 1**
- Guru Granth Sahib (Adi Granth), standard 1,430-page (*ang*) recension. This includes the compositions of six Sikh Gurus, the Bhagats (for example Kabir, Sheikh Farid, Namdev, Ravidas) and the Bhatts. **Sikhs treat all of it equally as Gurbani, so the Bhagats' verses count as full Tier 1.**

**Tier 2**
- Sikh Rehat Maryada (SGPC; approved 1945, published 1950), the official code of conduct, including the standard text of the Ardas.
- Vaaran Bhai Gurdas. The Rehat Maryada itself permits them to be sung in the gurdwara as explanation of Gurbani. *Added.*
- Dasam Granth. **Flagged as contested.** Only the compositions prescribed for daily prayer and initiation (Jaap, the Ten Swayyas, Benti Chaupai) have near-universal acceptance. The rest (including the Zafarnama) is cited for context and counter-evidence only, never as sole evidence. *Added.*

**Excluded as evidence:** the Janamsakhis and the 18th-century Rahitnamas (many, mutually inconsistent, and superseded by the Rehat Maryada).

## 12. Bahá'í Faith

**Tier 1**
- All authenticated writings of Bahá'u'lláh, including: the Kitáb-i-Aqdas (cited by paragraph of the 1992 authorised translation); the Hidden Words (Arabic and Persian); the Kitáb-i-Íqán; **Gleanings from the Writings of Bahá'u'lláh** (moved from Tier 2); the Tablets of Bahá'u'lláh revealed after the Kitáb-i-Aqdas; the Epistle to the Son of the Wolf; the Summons of the Lord of Hosts; Prayers and Meditations.
- Writings of the Báb. **Flagged:** Bahá'ís regard them as revealed, but many of the Báb's laws were changed by Bahá'u'lláh, so on conduct they are supporting, not decisive.

**Tier 2**
- Authenticated writings of ʿAbdu'l-Bahá (for example *Selections*, *Some Answered Questions*, *The Secret of Divine Civilization*, the *Will and Testament*). **Flagged as near-primary:** the tradition treats these as part of its sacred text and as authorised interpretation.
- Writings of Shoghi Effendi (authorised interpreter).
- Elucidations and legislation of the Universal House of Justice, including the Notes to the Kitáb-i-Aqdas.
- Talks of ʿAbdu'l-Bahá recorded by others (*Paris Talks*, *Promulgation of Universal Peace*): clarifying only, because many transcripts were not authenticated.

**Excluded:** pilgrims' notes and unofficial provisional translations, which the tradition itself says carry no authority.

**Reasons for changes**
1. *Gleanings moved to Tier 1.* Every passage in it is Bahá'u'lláh's own text, selected and translated by Shoghi Effendi. Only the selection is editorial. Leaving it in Tier 2 would downgrade scripture.
2. Many central ethical teachings appear only in the later Tablets, which the tradition also treats as scripture.
3. "Other authorised writings" was vague; the tradition itself ranks these sources.

**Branch note.** Small groups that reject the authority of the present Universal House of Justice accept Bahá'u'lláh's and ʿAbdu'l-Bahá's writings, so Tier 1 is undisputed.

## 13. Secular humanism

Humanism has no scripture. Its founding texts are consensus declarations adopted by humanist bodies. Each describes itself as a statement of what humanists hold at the time, not a creed, and later versions replace earlier ones.

**Tier 1**
- Declaration of Modern Humanism (Amsterdam Declaration 2022), Humanists International. The current international statement; it replaced the 2002 text.
- Amsterdam Declaration 2002 and Amsterdam Declaration 1952 (historical founding versions). *Added.*
- Humanist Manifesto I (1933), **flagged as historical** and as "religious humanism"; Humanist Manifesto II (1973), **flagged as historical** (the American Humanist Association has declared Manifestos I and II historic and superseded by Manifesto III; flag added 2026-10-13 after the independent check of Article I); Humanist Manifesto III, *Humanism and Its Aspirations* (2003), American Humanist Association.
- IHEU Minimum Statement on Humanism (1996), the formal definition adopted by the international body. *Added.*
- A Secular Humanist Declaration (1980), drafted by Paul Kurtz for the Council for Democratic and Secular Humanism (now the Council for Secular Humanism). **Moved from Tier 2.**

**Tier 2**
- Humanist Manifesto 2000: *A Call for a New Planetary Humanism* (Kurtz; not adopted by the AHA or Humanists International). *Added.*
- Paul Kurtz's writings on humanist ethics (for example *Forbidden Fruit*, 1988), as the interpretive authority for the Council for Secular Humanism branch. *Added.*
- Explanatory material published by member organisations (for example Humanists UK's *Understanding Humanism*). **Explanatory only; never sufficient on its own.** *Added.*

**Reasons for changes**
1. The 1952 and 2002 versions show whether a teaching is long-standing or new.
2. The 1996 Minimum Statement is as authoritative as the declarations.
3. *Secular Humanist Declaration moved to Tier 1.* The tradition under test is named "secular humanism", and this Declaration is the founding document of organised secular humanism under that name. Leaving it in Tier 2 would mean the branch that treats it as foundational could never rely on it alone.
4. Humanist Manifesto 2000 and Kurtz's books are single-author or single-branch statements, so they are Tier 2.

**A principle recorded in advance.** Because humanism has no scripture, its member organisations' educational material often states humanist ethics more fully than the declarations do. We keep such material in Tier 2 and do not let it carry a verdict alone. We record this rule *before* applying it to any article, so that it is not adjusted to suit a result.
