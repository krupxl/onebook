## Axiomatic residue

Foundational premises that have been isolated and stated precisely but not resolved.

*None confirmed.* Six candidates have been isolated and are not yet tested:

1. **Grading treatment by allegiance** (Q-001, Part 8). Whether courtesy, honour and company, and in the Gathas' base translation (Mills, SBE 31; the Bartholomae English on avesta.org agrees) even harm (Yasna 33.2, 47.4), may rightly differ according to a person's faith, allegiance or moral standing. Several traditions' texts grade such treatment; secular humanism rejects all prejudice.
2. **One's own wishes as the measure** (Q-001, Part 9). Whether what I would want is a proper test for how to treat others. Zhuangzi and humanist explanatory material question it.
3. **A fixed order of rank within the household** (Q-002, Part 29). Whether the authority of master over slave, husband over wife, and parent or teacher over child, with a rightful power to correct, is itself a value. Many primary texts accept these relationships while limiting them; premises of equal standing (the Sikh "one Light", the Bahá'í "no one should exalt himself above another", humanist dignity and autonomy) point the other way, and many traditions hold both.
4. **Freedom as a status** (Q-002, Part 30). Whether freedom from being owned belongs to every person or only to those with a certain status (for example Leviticus 25:42 to 46; Seneca's view that slavery reaches only the body).
5. **Where the wrong in a blow lies** (Q-002, Part 24). Whether harm is defined by the act or by the anger or passion behind it. Many texts fault striking in anger rather than striking as such; Jain teaching defines harm by the passion behind the act.
6. **What later authority can do** (Q-002, Part 17). Whether a permission in a revealed or founding text can be withdrawn by later authority, or only regulated. This is a premise about sources rather than about conduct, but it decides how far the Logical parts can go.

## Current reading of the evidence

**C01, the Golden Rule.** No tradition's texts teach the opposite of the rule in general. But testing it has shown that agreement is narrower than it first looked, and the households study has found the first wide gap between what the primary texts permit and what the rule could require when applied to households.

- **The harm floor for personal conduct** ("do not harm anyone in a way you would not want to be harmed yourself"): twelve traditions meet it in texts that count (nine through the test itself, though for Digambara Jains only through a wider duty not to harm; Sikhism and secular humanism through a wider duty not to harm, which the project lead has confirmed counts, and Taoism on borderline evidence). Zoroastrianism meets it only in Tier 2 texts that the traditionalist branch accepts. The project lead left the tier and branch rules to the project's judgement and they were kept as written; a re-test of the Avesta found nothing above the Implied level in the texts every branch accepts, and in the base translation (Mills, SBE 31, with the Bartholomae English on avesta.org) two Gathic verses (Yasna 33.2 and 47.4) point the other way for the "follower of the Lie".
- **The full form** ("do not treat anyone in *any* way you would not want"): unchanged. Eight traditions clearly teach it (Register Q-001).
- **Households and slavery** (decision D2: the floor includes them; under the project lead's delegation the article's household wording is *no cruelty*, and the rest is Register entry [Q-002](register/q-002-households-and-slavery.md)). Three wordings were tested in all thirteen traditions (annex to Article I), under nine written labelling rules applied to every tradition alike. *No cruelty* is taught in seven traditions and contradicted by counted texts in four (Hinduism, Judaism, Stoicism, Christianity), with two unclear (Islam, Zoroastrianism). *No one owned* is taught in counted texts by two traditions and contradicted in five, and in one branch of two more. *No hitting* is clearly taught by none and contradicted in five, and in one branch of a sixth. A lower floor ("do not kill, maim, starve or torment anyone in your power") still fails on the evidence already gathered; this step was made without new searches and has not yet been checked (Q-002, Part 7). Q-002 breaks the disagreement into thirty parts and records two narrower floors that may already be shared but are untested: "do not punish anyone in your power in anger, or out of proportion to the fault" (texts in at least seven traditions fault anger or excess in punishment) and "do not seize or sell a free person".

**What this means for the hypothesis so far.** Still one candidate, so still too few to judge, and no irreducible difference has been confirmed. But the households study is the first strong test, and the result is mixed:

1. On a plain reading, many primary texts accept slavery and physical correction that the rule, applied to people in one's power, could forbid. Even the narrowest wording, *no cruelty*, chosen on 2026-10-13, is contradicted by counted texts in four traditions. If the project counted only what the primary texts say, these would be real differences between the texts and the floor.
2. Yet the same traditions' own principles point the other way, and many of their later authorities have drawn that conclusion (for example on slavery: the Catechism of the Catholic Church, a 2014 open letter of Muslim scholars, and Shoghi Effendi for the Bahá'í Faith). In the Register's terms, this is a candidate for a **Logical** resolution: whether each tradition's own stated principles imply the wording. It is now settled (`METHOD.md`, section 9, decision 7) that such later conclusions never outweigh a contradicting counted text, but are the main evidence for the Logical parts. So a Logical resolution can show that a tradition's principles imply the wording; it cannot by itself admit an article while the contradicting text stands. Whether a tradition can withdraw a permission in its own primary text is itself a candidate axiom (Q-002, Part 17).
3. The narrowest wording, *no cruelty*, is the nearest to agreement: its contradictions are confined to a few named texts (Bṛhadāraṇyaka 6.4.7; Exodus 21:20 to 21; Seneca, *De Ira* 3.32; Sirach 33 and 42:5 for Catholics and Orthodox), each with a textual or logical question attached. This is where reduction looks most achievable. But it depends on reading "injure" as harm beyond moderate correction, the reading adopted with the wording; if any physical punishment counted as injury, *no cruelty* would fare little better than *no hitting*.

A pattern to watch, now seen twice: where the primary texts are short or survive mainly as liturgy (the humanist declarations; the Avesta), traditions can fail on silence rather than disagreement, and on L2 and L3 silence fails just as contradiction does. The rules are now written down and apply to every tradition: silence never teaches a wording (annex, rule 8), and a general clause reaches a practice only "by plain reference" (`METHOD.md`, section 9, decision 9). What remains is to test whether the silent traditions' own principles imply the wording, which Q-002 records as Logical parts.
