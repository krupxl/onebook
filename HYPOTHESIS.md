## Axiomatic residue

Foundational premises that have been isolated and stated precisely but not resolved.

*None confirmed.* Three candidates have been isolated and are not yet tested:

1. **Grading treatment by allegiance** (Q-001, Part 8). Whether courtesy, honour and company, and in the Gathas' base translation (Mills, SBE 31; the Bartholomae English on avesta.org agrees) even harm (Yasna 33.2, 47.4), may rightly differ according to a person's faith, allegiance or moral standing. Several traditions' texts grade such treatment; secular humanism rejects all prejudice.
2. **One's own wishes as the measure** (Q-001, Part 9). Whether what I would want is a proper test for how to treat others. Zhuangzi and humanist explanatory material question it.
3. **A fixed order of rank within the household** (proposed Q-002). Whether the authority of master over slave, husband over wife, and parent or teacher over child is itself a value, so that physical correction and holding people as property are right within limits. Many primary texts accept these relationships while limiting them; the traditions' later authorities have moved away from them to different degrees. Not yet decomposed.

## Current reading of the evidence

**C01, the Golden Rule.** No tradition's texts teach the opposite of the rule in general. But testing it has shown that agreement is narrower than it first looked, and the households study has found the first wide gap between what the primary texts permit and what the rule could require when applied to households.

- **The harm floor for personal conduct** ("do not harm anyone in a way you would not want to be harmed yourself"): twelve traditions meet it in texts that count (nine through the test itself, though for Digambara Jains only through a wider duty not to harm; Sikhism and secular humanism through a wider duty not to harm, which the project lead has confirmed counts, and Taoism on borderline evidence). Zoroastrianism meets it only in Tier 2 texts that the traditionalist branch accepts. The project lead left the tier and branch rules to the project's judgement and they were kept as written; a re-test of the Avesta found nothing above the Implied level in the texts every branch accepts, and in the base translation (Mills, SBE 31, with the Bartholomae English on avesta.org) two Gathic verses (Yasna 33.2 and 47.4) point the other way for the "follower of the Lie".
- **The full form** ("do not treat anyone in *any* way you would not want"): unchanged. Eight traditions clearly teach it (Register Q-001).
- **Households and slavery** (decision D2: the floor now includes them, wording to be chosen). Three wordings were tested in all thirteen traditions (annex to Article I), under nine written labelling rules applied to every tradition alike. *No cruelty* is taught in seven traditions and contradicted by counted texts in four (Hinduism, Judaism, Stoicism, Christianity), with two unclear (Islam, Zoroastrianism). *No one owned* is taught in counted texts by two traditions and contradicted in five, and in one branch of two more. *No hitting* is clearly taught by none and contradicted in five, and in one branch of a sixth. A lower floor ("do not kill, maim, starve or torment anyone in your power") still fails on the evidence already gathered; this step was made without new searches and has not yet been checked.

**What this means for the hypothesis so far.** Still one candidate, so still too few to judge, and no irreducible difference has been confirmed. But the households study is the first strong test, and the result is mixed:

1. On a plain reading, many primary texts accept slavery and physical correction that the rule, applied to people in one's power, could forbid, depending on the wording the project lead chooses. If the project counted only what the primary texts say, these would be real differences between the texts and the floor.
2. Yet the same traditions' own principles point the other way, and many of their later authorities have drawn that conclusion (for example on slavery: the Catechism of the Catholic Church, a 2014 open letter of Muslim scholars, and Shoghi Effendi for the Bahá'í Faith). In the Register's terms, this is a candidate for a **Logical** resolution: whether each tradition's own stated principles imply the wording. The project has not yet decided how much weight such later conclusions carry when the primary texts themselves are not withdrawn, and that question will recur.
3. The narrowest wording, *no cruelty*, is the nearest to agreement: its contradictions are confined to a few named texts (Bṛhadāraṇyaka 6.4.7; Exodus 21:20 to 21; Seneca, *De Ira* 3.32; Sirach 33 and 42:5 for Catholics and Orthodox), each with a textual or logical question attached. This is where reduction looks most achievable. But it depends on reading "injure" as harm beyond moderate correction; if any physical punishment counts as injury, *no cruelty* fares little better than *no hitting*.

A pattern to watch, now seen twice: where the primary texts are short or survive mainly as liturgy (the humanist declarations; the Avesta), traditions can fail on silence rather than disagreement, and on L2 and L3 silence fails just as contradiction does. The project may need a settled rule, decided in advance and for all traditions, on how silence and later authorities are treated.
