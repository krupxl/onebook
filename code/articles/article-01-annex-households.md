# Article I, Annex: Households and Slavery

> **What this annex is for.** It sets out the evidence for decision D2 on [Article I](article-01-golden-rule.md). On 13 October 2026 the project lead decided that the article's floor must cover slavery and authority within households, and that the exact wording will be decided after reading what each tradition's texts say. This annex gives those texts, tradition by tradition. It does not choose the wording.

## The three wordings tested

Read plainly, "Do not harm anyone in a way you would not want to be harmed yourself", applied to families, households and people in your power, rules out three different things. The traditions' texts treat them differently, so each was tested on its own:

- **L1, no cruelty:** do not injure, torment, starve, overwork or humiliate anyone in your household or in your power.
- **L2, no one owned:** no one may be held or traded as property.
- **L3, no hitting:** no physical punishment of children, spouses, servants or pupils.

## How the evidence was gathered and counted

- **Research and checking.** For each tradition, one researcher searched the texts, and then an independent verifier, who had not done the research, was asked to find what was wrong with it. The verifier's corrections are used here. All checking was done through web search, so citations are marked "VERIFIED (search)" or "UNVERIFIED" as defined in [`METHOD.md`](../../METHOD.md), section 3. An unverified citation does not count either way.
- **Counted texts.** A text counts if it is Tier 1, or a Tier 2 text that [`sources/corpus.md`](../../sources/corpus.md) says the tradition treats as primary. Other texts are shown because they limit, explain or reconcile the counted ones, or because adherents would expect to see them, but they do not decide anything on their own.
- **What the results mean.** "Contradicted" means a counted text permits or commands what the wording forbids. "Partly contradicted" means it does so for some relationships, some people or some branches only. "Not contradicted, silent" means no counted text was found either way; silence is not contradiction. "Not contradicted, taught" means a counted text teaches the wording, directly or through a wider rule.
- **Fairness.** Each section gives the tradition's own reconciliation of its hard texts, with how strong that reconciliation is (a primary text, a classical authority, or a modern body). Nothing here judges which tradition is right, and nothing describes what adherents do today: the test is what the texts teach (the project's settled decision 2).

## What the summary shows

- **Teaching.** Every tradition teaches L1 (no cruelty) at least through a wider duty not to harm that reaches everyone, though for Zoroastrianism only in texts the traditionalist branch accepts (the same gap that holds the article's main floor; see decision D1). L2 (no one owned) is taught in counted texts only by the Bahá'í Faith, explicitly, and by secular humanism, by principle. No tradition's counted texts clearly teach L3 (no hitting); the Bahá'í texts come nearest.
- **Why the wider rule settles L1 but not L2 or L3.** Under the project's counting rule, "harm no one" teaches any narrower floor it includes. Cruelty is plainly harm, so the wider rule carries L1. It cannot by itself carry L2 or L3, because whether holding a person as property, or moderate physical correction, counts as harm is exactly what the texts dispute: several traditions teach the wider rule and, in the same body of texts, accept slavery or physical correction. So, for L2 and L3, a tradition whose counted texts are silent does not yet teach the wording.
- **Contradiction.** L1: contradicted by a counted text in one tradition (Hinduism) and partly in two (Judaism and Christianity, through the shared Exodus 21:20 to 21, and for Catholics and Orthodox also Sirach 33); unclear in two (Stoicism and Islam). L2: contradicted in seven (Hinduism, Judaism, Buddhism, Jainism, Stoicism, Christianity, Islam), unclear in one and silent in three. L3: contradicted in seven (Hinduism, Judaism, Buddhism, Stoicism, Christianity, Islam, and partly Jainism), unclear in one and silent in five.
- **The traditions' own reconciliations.** Most traditions' later authorities limit these texts, and several reject slavery outright (for example, the Catechism of the Catholic Church 2414; an open letter of more than 120 Muslim scholars in 2014; Shoghi Effendi for the Bahá'í Faith). But no reconciliation found for a contradicting counted text is itself a counted text, and for L1 the reconciliations are partial: Rashi and the Mekhilta make killing a slave a capital crime but do not reach a beating the slave survives; the Christian teaching that the Old Testament's judicial laws have lapsed was not verified and does not reach Sirach; and no reconciliation of Bṛhadāraṇyaka 6.4.7 was verified.
- **Consequence.** Under the project's rules (every tradition must clearly teach the floor, and nothing may contradict its core), none of the three wordings can be admitted as the texts stand. L2 and L3 fail in most traditions, through silence as well as contradiction. L1 comes nearest: it is taught everywhere, and the contradictions are confined to a few named texts. The choice is the project lead's (decision D2 in the article).


## The evidence, tradition by tradition

### 1. Hinduism

**Summary.** No counted (Tier 1) text commands slavery, but a Ṛg Veda hymn praises a patron's gift of a hundred slaves listed alongside asses and sheep, and a verse of the Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad, in a rite for begetting a child, tells a husband to hit a wife who refuses him. No verified Tier 1 text speaks either way about hitting children, pupils or servants, and Tier 1 teaches non-harm towards people in one's household only through general rules that reach every being (Bhagavad Gita 6.32). The law books (Tier 2, flagged as contested in corpus.md) treat slavery as a legal institution and permit corrective beating of a son, pupil, wife or slave within limits of instrument and body part; other law books provide ways out of slavery, forbid selling children and say that as a rule a pupil is not to be hit. Branches differ mainly over the law books: Vedānta ranks revealed scripture above them where they conflict, the Arya Samaj accepts Manu only where it agrees with the Vedas, and anti-caste movements reject it; the rule that revealed scripture outranks the law books cannot reach the two Tier 1 texts, which are themselves revealed scripture.

**How the tradition reconciles these texts.** Hinduism has no single body that can rule for the whole tradition, so each reconciliation binds only the schools that accept it. The main classical rule is that remembered texts, including the law books, give way to revealed scripture where they conflict; it is verified here as Mīmāṃsā Sūtra 1.3.3 cited by Śaṅkara (a classical commentator, HI-13) and recorded in corpus.md, while Manusmṛti 2.13, which states the same rule, was not re-checked. That rule can limit or suspend law-book rules, but it cannot reach Bṛhadāraṇyaka 6.4.7 or Ṛg Veda 8.56.3, which are themselves revealed scripture, and no classical authority was verified that limits or rereads either verse (Śaṅkara's gloss on 6.4.7, HI-5, could not be confirmed). Within the law books, classical texts limit rather than forbid: Nārada provides ways out of slavery (HI-9), Āpastamba bars the sale of children (HI-10), Gautama and the commentator Medhātithi restrict how and when a pupil or son may be struck (HI-12), and Yājñavalkya 2.182 (freeing those enslaved by force) and Medhātithi's reading of Manu 8.414 as rhetoric rather than a binding rule are reported but unverified. Manusmṛti's own authority is contested (corpus.md): the Arya Samaj accepts it only where it agrees with the Vedas (and is reported, unverified, to rank the Upanishads below the four Saṃhitās), Gandhi accepted it only where it agreed with truth and non-violence, and anti-caste movements reject it (B. R. Ambedkar burned it publicly in 1927). Modern repudiation of slavery comes from individual teachers, such as Mata Amritanandamayi's 2014 signature (HI-14), not from a tradition-wide ruling.

**Result for each wording.**
- L1 (no cruelty): contradicted. Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad 6.4.7 (HI-4, Tier 1, verified) tells a husband to hit and overcome a wife who refuses him, with a formula that takes away her glory; Tier 1 teaches the level only through the general rule of Gita 6.32 (HI-6), with Mahabharata 13.114.8 (HI-7, Tier 2) in support.
- L2 (no one owned): contradicted. Ṛg Veda 8.56.3 (HI-1, Tier 1, verified) praises a gift of a hundred slaves listed with animals, by approving description rather than command and in a Vālakhilya hymn; the law books state the institution outright (HI-8), and no counted text teaches that no one may be owned (Gita 5.18 and 6.32 at most imply it, and 5.18 is unverified).
- L3 (no hitting): contradicted. For spouses, Bṛhadāraṇyaka 6.4.7 (HI-4, Tier 1) directs hitting; for children, pupils and servants no verified Tier 1 text was found either way, while the law books permit limited corrective beating (HI-11) and none forbids it outright (HI-12).

**Gaps.** Not verified: Ṛg Veda 8.19.36, Aitareya Brāhmaṇa 7.29, Śaṅkara on Bṛhadāraṇyaka 6.4.7, Gita 16.1 to 3 and 5.18, Yājñavalkya 2.182, Medhātithi on Manu 8.414, Manu 2.13, 4.179 to 185 (bearing offences from wife, children and slaves without resentment) and 10.63 (non-injury in the summary of the law for all classes), and the effect of the Vālakhilya hymns' standing on their authority. Not searched: other Tier 1 passages recalled as listing slaves among possessions (Chāndogya Upaniṣad 7.24.2, Bṛhadāraṇyaka 6.2.7), Rāmānuja and Madhva on 6.4.7, the Purāṇas and the Mahabharata on servants and dependants, and any modern Hindu body's statement on hitting children, wives or pupils, so silence there is a research gap, not a finding.

### 2. Judaism

**Summary.** The Tanakh allows slaves from other nations to be bought and held as permanent, inheritable property (Leviticus 25:44 to 46), calls a slave his master's "money" (Exodus 21:21), and regulates a father's sale of a young daughter into service. It also limits a master's power: a Hebrew servant must not be ruled harshly, slaves share the weekly rest, a maimed slave goes free, kidnapping is a capital crime, an escaped slave is not handed back, hired workers are owed prompt wages, and Job grounds a servant's claims in the same Maker. Proverbs commends the rod for a child, and the Mishnah and Talmud treat a father's or teacher's physical discipline as lawful while limiting it; for wives, no Tanakh or Talmud text was found that permits striking, the Talmud asks a husband to love and honour his wife, and the later codes divide. Rabbinic Judaism (Orthodox, and in principle Conservative or Masorti) reads the Tanakh through the Mishnah and Talmud, which add both limits and a ruling against freeing non-Israelite slaves; Karaites accept only the Tanakh, and Reform and Reconstructionist Judaism treat the rabbinic texts as valued but not binding, though no modern body's statements were checked.

**How the tradition reconciles these texts.** In Rabbinic Judaism the Mishnah, the tannaitic midrash and the Talmud (treated as primary) and, below them, the classical commentators and codes reconcile the hard texts, and they limit rather than repeal. On slavery, the Mekhilta and the Talmud, followed by Rashi, make killing a slave a capital crime, and Rashi extends the release for maiming to the non-Israelite slave (JU-4); Arakhin 29a, as cited by Sefer HaChinukh, suspends Hebrew servitude while the Jubilee is not in force but leaves the holding of non-Israelite slaves untouched (JU-8); and Maimonides keeps the legal permission to work such a slave harshly while asking mercy of the master as the way of piety (JU-9). The same rabbinic authorities also narrow some Tier 1 protections, on kidnapping and the escaped slave (JU-5). On children, Sanhedrin 71a treats the rebellious-son law as never applied, with one dissent (JU-12), and the Talmud limits striking (no grown son, no injurious blows to pupils) without forbidding it (JU-13). On wives the classical codes divide: Maimonides permits compulsion, which the Raavad disputes, while the Rema's Ashkenazi gloss forbids striking a wife (JU-15, both unverified). No primary text or classical authority was found that withdraws the permission for non-Israelite slavery or forbids the physical discipline of children; modern bodies of every branch, and Karaite authorities, were not searched.

**Result for each wording.**
- L1 (no cruelty): partly contradicted. Exodus 21:20 to 21 (Tier 1, verified) leaves unpunished a beating that a slave survives because the slave is his master's "money", and Leviticus 25:43 to 46 states the ban on harsh rule only for Israelites (which Maimonides reads as allowing harsh work, and work without food, for a non-Israelite slave, after Gittin 12a), while Job 31:13 to 15, Deuteronomy 5:14, Exodus 21:26 to 27 and Leviticus 25:43 clearly teach the core of L1 for servants.
- L2 (no one owned): contradicted. Leviticus 25:44 to 46, Exodus 21:21 and 21:32 treat non-Israelite slaves as property and Exodus 21:2 to 11 regulates the sale of a Hebrew servant and of a daughter (all Tier 1, verified), Gittin 38b adds for Rabbinic Judaism that freeing such a slave breaks a command, and no limiting text (Exodus 21:16, Deuteronomy 23:16 to 17, Arakhin 29a) reaches the holding of a lawfully bought non-Israelite slave.
- L3 (no hitting): contradicted. Proverbs 23:13 to 14 and 13:24 (Tier 1, verified) commend the rod for a child, Exodus 21:20 to 21 leaves a master's beating of a slave unpunished if the slave survives, and Mishnah Makkot 2:2 and Bava Batra 21a treat a father's and a teacher's striking as lawful, while for wives no counted text permitting it was found, Yevamot 62b implies it is excluded, and the codes divide (Maimonides against the Rema).

No modern body of any branch (Orthodox, Conservative or Masorti, Reform, Reconstructionist, Israeli Chief Rabbinate) was checked, which is the decisive gap for L2, and Karaite positions were not researched.

### 3. Zoroastrianism

**Summary.** The Gathas, the only texts every branch accepts, were not found to mention slavery, servants, or the physical discipline of children, spouses or pupils. Their one household passage (Yasna 53.4 to 53.5) has a bride promise to honour her father and her husband, and asks husband and wife to treat each other with righteousness (*asha*). Traditionalists also accept the Vendidad, which punishes striking any person with evil intent while itself using flogging as a court penalty, and they treat the Pahlavi books as authoritative, where a general maxim forbids doing to another what is not good for oneself. No counted text was found that permits or forbids owning people, but Encyclopaedia Iranica reports Sasanian slave law in Denkard Book 8, a Pahlavi text traditionalists treat as authoritative (the passage has not yet been found); outside the corpus, the Sasanian law book treats slaves as property and the later Persian Rivayats treat household slavery as permitted and regulated. Reformists ("Gathas-only") are bound by none of the Vendidad, Pahlavi or legal texts, so for them the Gathas alone decide, and on these questions the Gathas appear silent.

**How the tradition reconciles these texts.** No Zoroastrian authority was found that limits, suspends or repudiates slavery or physical discipline in the household, and no modern authoritative statement on either was searched for. The main difference within the tradition is one of corpus rather than interpretation. The reformist ("Gathas-only") movement, a modern movement, treats only the Gathas as binding (many reformists regard the Vendidad as a later priestly text with no binding force), so for that branch the Pahlavi and legal texts that bear on slavery carry no binding authority; no reformist statement on slavery itself was found. Traditionalists accept the whole Avesta, including the Vendidad, and treat the Pahlavi books as the authoritative interpretation of scripture (a classical authority, though not revelation); for them the self-as-measure maxim (ZO-4, ZO-5) is stated as a rule for all conduct, but no classical or modern traditionalist authority was found applying it to slaves or calling for their release. Within the Sasanian law itself (outside the corpus), slaves had some standing in court and could testify in cases concerning them, which limits an owner's power without ending ownership.

**Result for each wording.**
- L1 (no cruelty): not contradicted. No counted text permits injuring, tormenting, starving, overworking or humiliating anyone in one's household or power; traditionalists are taught it as a wider rule by the Vendidad's assault rules (ZO-3) and the Pahlavi maxim (ZO-4, ZO-5), naming injury only, but the Gathas, which every branch accepts, reach only Implied (ZO-1, ZO-2), so it is not yet clearly taught for the whole tradition.
- L2 (no one owned): unclear. The Gathas appear silent and no counted text teaches that no one may be owned, while Encyclopaedia Iranica reports slave law in Denkard Book 8 (ZO-9), which would contradict L2 for traditionalists if found, and the texts actually seen accepting slavery (ZO-10, ZO-11) are outside the corpus and do not count on their own.
- L3 (no hitting): not contradicted (silent). No counted text permits or forbids physical punishment of children, spouses, servants or pupils; the Vendidad's floggings (ZO-3) are court penalties, which Article I leaves aside, and its rule against striking with evil intent does not say whether correction is covered.

### 4. Buddhism

**Summary.** The Pali monastic code (Vinaya), which is Tier 1, treats slavery as a lawful status: it bars slaves from becoming monks out of deference to their owners, and it asks every candidate for full ordination whether he is a free man. Trading in people is forbidden to lay followers (AN 5.177) and, for Mahāyāna Buddhists, to bodhisattvas (Brahmajāla precept 12), but no counted text found tells lay people not to own slaves. The Sigālovāda Sutta (DN 31) requires masters to give their slaves or servants and workers fitting work, food, wages, care in sickness and time off, and requires husbands to honour their wives; general verses such as Dhammapada 129 to 130 tell everyone not to strike others, taking themselves as the measure. On pupils, a canonical Jātaka verse (Ja 252) calls restraint with a stick instruction, not hostility, and its commentary story approves a teacher who had his pupil beaten, while the monastic rules forbid a monk to hit or raise a hand against another in anger; no counted text was found on physically punishing children, and none permits or forbids striking a wife. The contrary texts are Pali (Theravāda canon); the other Vinayas and the texts used by East Asian and Tibetan Buddhists were not checked on these points.

### 5. Jainism

**Summary.** The one text every Jain branch accepts, the Tattvārtha Sūtra, lists binding, beating, mutilating, overloading and starving living beings as lapses of the householder's vow of non-injury. It also lists female and male dāsas among the holdings a householder must keep within a self-set limit, without forbidding them. Both translators checked render dāsa as 'servants', but the word can also mean slaves. The Śvetāmbara canon, which Digambaras do not accept, explicitly condemns the man who punishes his wife, children and other relatives, or his slave or hired servant, harshly for small faults. The same canon takes a teacher's blows for granted in training a monastic pupil, and Śvetāmbara Mūrtipūjaka commentators read the lapse of 'beating' as cruel beating only, allowing restrained whipping, ear-pulling or slapping of a person in one's care. No verified text in either branch says that no one may be owned, and no Digambara-specific text on households or physical correction was verified.

**How the tradition reconciles these texts.** On holdings, the shared Tattvārtha Sūtra supplies its own frame (a primary text): possession is attachment (7.12 Śv. = 7.17 Dig.), householders limit it and ascetics give it up entirely (7.2). The Digambara Sarvārthasiddhi of Pūjyapāda (a classical commentary) confirms this reading: it glosses dāsī-dāsa as servants and places the fault only in exceeding one's limit out of greed. It limits the practice and does not repudiate it. On beating, the shared sūtra's definition of harm as careless or passion-driven injury (7.8 Śv. = 7.13 Dig.) is the basis for reading the lapses as faults of passion. The Śvetāmbara commentators Siddhasena Gaṇin, Haribhadra and Devendra are classical authorities, binding for Mūrtipūjakas but not for Sthānakavāsīs and Terāpanthīs, and are known here only through a modern summary. They limit the lapse to cruel or merciless beating and expressly allow restrained whipping, ear-pulling and slapping of those in one's care. No classical authority or modern Jain body was found that repudiates the holding of dāsas or urges freeing them. No Digambara authority was found on restrained correction, either way.

(JA-1) explicitly lists binding, beating, mutilating, overloading and starving living beings as lapses, and for Śvetāmbaras Sūtrakṛtāṅga II.2 (JA-3, JA-4) explicitly condemns harsh punishment of slaves, servants, wife and children; 'humiliate' is not named in any verified text, and the Digambara case rests on the shared sūtra alone.
- L2 (no one owned): contradicted. The shared Tattvārtha Sūtra 7.24 Śv. = 7.29 Dig. (JA-7) counts female and male dāsas among the holdings a householder limits but may keep, and the Digambara Sarvārthasiddhi (JA-9) confirms that only greed-driven excess is a fault; whether dāsa means slave or servant here is an open textual question, but on either reading persons are counted among holdings and no text forbids it.
- L3 (no hitting): partly contradicted. For Śvetāmbaras, Uttarādhyayana 1.37 to 39 (JA-10, Tier 1, implied) treats a teacher's blows as part of good instruction, and the Mūrtipūjaka commentaries (JA-11, which do not count on their own) expressly allow restrained whipping, ear-pulling and slapping of those in one's care; no Digambara text was found either way, and no text in any branch teaches L3 as a rule.

**Gaps.** Several texts were not checked: the Digambara Sarvārthasiddhi and Tattvārtharājavārttika on 7.25, the Ratnakaraṇḍaka Śrāvakācāra (JA-6), the Śvetāmbara Svopajña Bhāṣya, Hemacandra's Yogaśāstra, Daśavaikālika 9 and the classical commentaries on Uttarādhyayana 1, and the commentaries in JA-11 are known only through a modern summary. No text on physical discipline of children or a spouse was found, for or against. No Jain authority condemning the holding of dāsas was found, which does not show that none exists. Whether the list of forbidden trades covers trade in people is unverified. The listed translations of the Tattvārtha Sūtra (J. L. Jaini 1920; N. Tatia) were not checked on dāsī-dāsa, and the translations actually used (Doshi; Vijay K. Jain) are not yet in sources/translations.md.

### 6. Confucianism

**Summary.** The counted texts (the Analects, the Mencius, the Great Learning and the Doctrine of the Mean) apply the rule "do not do to others what you would not want done to you" to people in your power, and they condemn particular abuses by rulers. But they never rule on whether people may be owned, and they never forbid or command physical punishment. The Mencius mentions the daily beating of a boy only in passing, without judging it. The Book of Rites, the Classic of Documents and the Classic of Filial Piety were held as classics by every historical branch, but corpus.md keeps them in Tier 2. They accept buying a concubine, a household with bondservants ranked lowest and rods for pupils, and they tell a child to bear a beating that draws blood without resentment. The same texts also tell masters not to slight even their servants. No text was found that permits striking a wife: the Book of Rites answers an unfilial daughter-in-law with teaching, then reproof and, at worst, divorce. The branches differ in emphasis. Cheng-Zhu orthodoxy, the examination standard from 1313 to 1905, put the Four Books first, while Han to Tang classical learning treated the Five Classics as primary. Both held the Five Classics canonical, so where the counted texts are silent, the wider canon is not.

**How the tradition reconciles these texts.** Confucianism has no council or body with authority to issue binding rulings, and no modern authoritative statement on slavery or physical punishment was found. Two primary texts limit harm. Mencius 1B.5 (Tier 1, glossed the same way by Zhu Xi) keeps punishment to the offender alone, which answers the battle oaths. Mencius 4A.18 (Tier 1) hands the correction of sons to other teachers, so that a father's anger does not harm the bond; neither text forbids beating. Classical authorities also limit harm in the household, without rejecting the way it is organised. The Classic of Filial Piety (Tier 2) bars slighting even servants. Zhu Xi's commentary, the examination standard from 1313, makes dignity and kindness towards servants a duty while accepting their service. The Zeng Shen story (outside the corpus, authenticity disputed) tells a son to flee a stick that could kill but to bear the small rod. On slavery, Han Confucian voices cited the line that human beings are the most precious, and their measures limited slavery: Dong Zhongshu's proposal, Wang Mang's ban on sale (lifted again after three years) and Guangwu's edicts against killing and branding slaves. None of these suspended or rejected slavery as the tradition's settled position. As historical context only: slavery and physical punishment in the household stayed lawful under later governments that made Confucian learning their orthodoxy.

**Result for each wording.**
- L1 (no cruelty): not contradicted, and taught. The Great Learning, commentary ch. 10 (CO-1), applies the self-as-measure rule to those below you, supported by Analects 12.2 and 20.2 and Mencius 1B.5 (CO-2, CO-3). The texts that cut against it (the Nei Ze beating, CO-9, and the battle oaths, CO-4) are in Tier 2 classics that do not count on their own.
- L2 (no one owned): not contradicted (the counted texts are silent). No counted text rules on owning people; Mencius 1B.5 bears on it only by implication. The Tier 2 classics accept buying a concubine and holding bondservants (CO-5, CO-7), so the silence belongs to the counted texts, not to the canon that every branch held.
- L3 (no hitting): not contradicted (the counted texts are silent). The counted texts neither forbid nor command hitting, and Mencius 3B.6 mentions daily beating of a boy without judging it (CO-13). The Tier 2 classics accept rods for pupils and a child bearing a bloody beating (CO-9, CO-12), and no text inside or outside the corpus was found that forbids physical punishment.

**Gaps.** Legge's English was not shown for most rows, so the paraphrases are made from the Chinese and only one short quotation from Legge is given. Several leads are unverified: the captives rule (CO-5), Ban Zhao's Lessons for Women (outside the corpus; it treats a beating as destroying a marriage while blaming the wife's insolence), the Rites of Zhou on penal enslavement of offenders' families (its place in the corpus is borderline), Analects 18.1 (a good man enslaved by a tyrant, not judged) and several passages of the Mencius. Not searched: Xunzi, the Zuo Zhuan and Gongyang Zhuan, Zheng Xuan's glosses on the school rods, Neo-Confucian family instructions on servants and beating, and modern Confucian statements. The finding on spouses rests on no targeted search.

### 7. Taoism

**Summary.** The counted texts (the Tao Te Ching and the Zhuangzi) say nothing directly about slavery, servants, wives, or the discipline of children or pupils; their teaching on power is addressed to rulers. They say that the sage-ruler does not harm the people and, in Legge's reading, treats them as his children; they blame rulers whose taxes starve the people; and they use a cruel horse tamer as the image of coercive government, while also accepting state punishment and warning rulers against misusing it. Household teaching appears only in Tier 2, chiefly the Treatise on Response and Retribution (Ganying Pian), which is widely used in religious and lay practice: it lists cruelty to those beneath you and misconduct towards wife and children as wicked deeds, and condemns forcing free people into servile status, while assuming that a servile class exists and that a wife should be yielding. Philosophical readers generally accept only the Tao Te Ching and the Zhuangzi (often also the Liezi); the religious branches (Celestial Masters or Zhengyi, Lingbao, Quanzhen) also teach through precept texts and, for Quanzhen, monastic codes, which may include physical penalties for monastics (unconfirmed).

Tao Te Ching 74 (a primary text, verified) and Zhuangzi 6 (an Inner Chapter, primary, unverified) accept state punishment but temper it: the first warns rulers against killing in the place of the one who presides over killing, and the second asks for leniency in putting people to death; both limit punishment rather than suspend or repudiate it, and both concern the state, not the household. The classical commentaries that corpus.md names for reconciliation (Heshang Gong and Wang Bi on the Tao Te Ching, Guo Xiang on the Zhuangzi) were not checked, so no traditional reading is recorded of whether the sage's not harming people reaches household discipline. No Taoist authority was found commenting on servile status; the Ganying Pian (Tier 2, a classical morality book) itself limits slavery by condemning the enslavement of free people while leaving existing servile status in place. No statement by a modern Taoist body on slavery or physical punishment was searched for, and no authority was found that reconciles the possible Quanzhen penalties.

**Result for each wording.**
- **L1 (no cruelty):** not contradicted (taught, borderline). Tao Te Ching 60, 49 and 75 and Zhuangzi 9 (TA-1 to TA-4, all verified) teach that those with power must not harm, starve or overburden those under them, reaching households only by extension, with explicit household support only in Tier 2 (Ganying Pian, TA-8 and TA-9); Tao Te Ching 74 (TA-5) accepts state punishment, which bears on how L1 is worded but does not contradict a household floor.
- **L2 (no one owned):** not contradicted (silent). No counted text accepts or condemns holding or trading people (the lines on producing without possessing, 生而不有, in Tao Te Ching 2, 10 and 51 are Implied at most and unverified), and the clearest text found, the Ganying Pian's condemnation of forcing the free into servile status (TA-11), is Tier 2 and limits enslavement without meeting L2.
- **L3 (no hitting):** not contradicted (silent). No counted text addresses physical punishment of children, spouses, servants or pupils; Zhuangzi 9 and 4 (TA-4, TA-7) bear on it only by implication, and the possible Quanzhen monastic penalties (TA-14) are unconfirmed and outside the corpus as written.

**Gaps.** Beyond the verified Tier 1 lines and five Ganying Pian lines, the household and servant evidence is unverified: the Hundred and Eighty Precepts and the Lingbao Ten Precepts (the most likely home of religious Taoist servant ethics) were not seen, the Liezi story, the Quanzhen penalties and the Zhuangzi 4 and 6 passages need checking, and Legge's English for the Ganying Pian lines was not checked. The classical commentaries, modern Taoist bodies, the Ganying Pian line on harming children (損子) and the Taiping Jing (outside the corpus; reportedly condemns killing baby girls and discusses slaves) were not searched.

### 8. Stoicism

**Summary.** The counted texts (Seneca's letters and moral essays, Epictetus and Marcus Aurelius) accept slavery as a legal institution and regulate it. Masters must not starve, torment or humiliate slaves. They should live with those below them as they would want those above them to live with them, and should ask what justice allows rather than what the law permits. The texts say legal slavery touches only the body, and no counted text calls for slaves to be freed. Physical punishment is limited, not forbidden: Seneca names occasional blows as part of good parents' correction and treats beating a slave as a proper punishment when it is done calmly, while he condemns lethal beating and constant beating for small faults. Seneca also calls drowning weak or deformed newborns an act of reason. On pupils, one unverified passage prefers correction by advice and shame to torment. On spouses, no counted text was found; only Musonius Rufus (Tier 2, unverified) teaches one standard for husband and wife and a marriage of mutual care. Stoicism has no branches and no living authority, so the differences are between ancient teachers, most sharply between Seneca and Musonius on whether every newborn must be raised.

**How the tradition reconciles these texts.** Stoicism has no later council, office or living body that can settle a conflict between its texts, and modern Stoic writers and groups are not authorities under corpus.md. Its own account of slavery is in a primary text, Seneca, *De Beneficiis* 3.20 (Tier 1), backed by the early Stoic doctrine reported in Diogenes Laertius 7.121 to 7.122 (Tier 2): legal slavery touches only the body, and true slavery is a condition of the soul. This explains why the institution was accepted and grounds limits on how slaves are treated, but it does not remove ownership; Diogenes Laertius 7.122 may also call an owner's lordship bad, but it is Tier 2 and its meaning is debated. Chrysippus' rule that a slave is a hired worker for life (reported in *De Beneficiis* 3.22.1; applied by Cicero, *De Officiis* 1.41, Tier 2 and flagged) limits treatment by requiring that slaves be given their due, and does not reject the institution. For physical punishment, the limits come from Seneca's own primary texts: do not punish in anger, wait, prefer words, keep it in proportion and do not beat constantly for small faults (*De Ira* 3.12 and 3.32; *De Clementia* 1.16; Letter 47.19). These limit hitting and do not forbid it. On newborns, the only opposing Stoic voice is Musonius Rufus, Lecture 15 (Tier 2, unverified), and no authority decides between him and Seneca.

**Result for each wording.**
- L1 (no cruelty): unclear. Counted texts teach it explicitly for slaves and the household (*De Clementia* 1.18.1 to 1.18.3; Letter 47.2 to 47.5 and 47.11; *Meditations* 5.31), but *De Ira* 1.15.2 calls drowning weak or deformed newborns an act of reason and *De Ira* 3.32 treats flogging and leg-breaking as within a master's power. L1 is met only if newborns are left to candidate C02 (as Article I does) and "injure" is read as harm beyond proportionate correction, leaving beating to L3; on a plain reading of "injure", *De Ira* 3.32 cuts against L1 for slaves.
- L2 (no one owned): contradicted. *De Beneficiis* 3.20.1 to 3.20.2 says slaves' bodies are subject and assigned to their masters, and *De Clementia* 1.18 and Letter 47.17 to 47.18 take purchase and ownership for granted; support for L2 is implied only (*Discourses* 1.13; Diogenes Laertius 7.122, Tier 2).
- L3 (no hitting): contradicted. *De Clementia* 1.14.1 makes occasional blows part of good parents' correction, and *De Ira* 3.12 and 3.32 treat beating a slave as a proper punishment when it is done without anger; Letter 47.19 and *De Clementia* 1.16 limit hitting but do not forbid it.

**Gaps.** No Stoic text was found on striking a spouse, either way; Hierocles' household fragments (Tier 2), the Greek of *Meditations* 5.31 (whether it names a wife) and duties of care to children (for example *Discourses* 1.11) were not searched. Not verified: all of Musonius Rufus, *De Clementia* 1.16.3 (pupils), Cicero, *De Officiis* 2.24, and several leads left out of the table (Letter 47.1 and 47.10 on slaves as fellow human beings, *De Ira* 3.24 on whips and fetters for small faults, *De Beneficiis* 3.22.3 on an official who hears slaves' complaints, and an Epictetus fragment of doubtful authenticity against enslaving others); several section numbers rest on modern numbering, not on search.

### 9. Christianity

**Summary.** The Bible shared by all branches contains laws that let foreign slaves be bought and kept as inherited property, leave a master unpunished if a slave he beats survives a day or two, and commend the rod for children, and the New Testament tells slaves, including those of Christian masters, to obey and honour their masters without asking for their release. The same scriptures also limit household power: masters must give up threats and give slaves what is just and fair, fathers must not provoke their children, husbands must love their wives as their own bodies, and no counted text permits striking a wife, while pupils are addressed only through the rule that a church overseer must not be a striker. Catholics and Orthodox also count Sirach, which prescribes torments and fetters for a disobedient servant and frequent beating of sons; Protestants do not count it. Christians hold that the Old Testament judicial (civil) laws no longer bind them, and the Catholic Catechism forbids enslavement outright, but the main classical authorities (Augustine, Aquinas) accept moderate physical correction of children and slaves.

**How the tradition reconciles these texts.** The main reconciling teaching is that the judicial laws of the Old Testament, including the slave and household laws of Exodus 21, Leviticus 25 and Deuteronomy 21, ended with Christ and bind Christians only where general fairness requires (Aquinas, a classical Catholic authority; the Westminster Confession 19.4, a Reformed Protestant confession; both UNVERIFIED here). This suspends those laws rather than condemning them, and it does not reach the wisdom books: Proverbs for every branch, and Sirach for Catholics and Orthodox, which is limited only by its own words ("be not excessive", treat a servant "as thyself"). For the New Testament, the classical authorities Augustine and Aquinas (Tier 2, verified) read the limits on masters and fathers as bans on excess and threats, not on moderate correction, while binding the head of a household to rule for the good of those under him. On owning people, outright repudiation comes from Tier 2 and later authorities and differs by branch: Gregory of Nyssa (a Father, verified through scholarship only, whom scholars describe as alone among the Fathers in this) condemns slaveholding as such; Augustine explains slavery as a result of sin without rejecting it; for Catholics, Catechism 2414 (verified) forbids enslavement and the trade in people, and Vatican II and later popes (outside the corpus as listed) call slavery an infamy. No Protestant or Orthodox doctrinal text in the corpus repudiating slaveholding was verified, and no authority of any rank was found that forbids the physical correction of children or servants.

**Result for each wording.**
- L1 (no cruelty): partly contradicted. The New Testament clearly teaches it for every household relationship (Ephesians 6:9, Colossians 4:1, Ephesians 5:28 to 29, Colossians 3:21, with Romans 13:10), but Exodus 21:20 to 21 and Deuteronomy 21:18 to 21 cut against it (held lapsed as judicial law, a reading UNVERIFIED here), and for Catholics and Orthodox Sirach 33 prescribes torments and fetters with no reconciling authority found beyond its own limits; whether the Proverbs rod texts also count against L1 depends on whether "injure" is worded to include moderate physical correction.
- L2 (no one owned): contradicted. Leviticus 25:44 to 46 and Exodus 21:2 to 6 allow people to be held as inherited property, and 1 Timothy 6:1 to 2, 1 Peter 2:18 and the other household codes accept slaveholding among Christians, while the counted texts explicitly forbid only enslaving by kidnap (1 Timothy 1:10), at most imply more (Philemon 16, Galatians 3:28), and leave the outright repudiation to Tier 2 or later authority (Gregory of Nyssa; Catechism 2414 for Catholics).
- L3 (no hitting): contradicted for children and servants, not for spouses. Proverbs 23:13 to 14 commends striking a child with the rod and Exodus 21:20 to 21 regulates rather than forbids striking a slave, the classical authorities Aquinas (II-II q.65 a.2) and Augustine (XIX.16) affirm moderate corrective striking, and no reconciling authority of any rank was found, while for spouses no counted text permits striking and Ephesians 5:28 to 29 teaches cherishing, and pupils are not addressed.

**Gaps.** Not verified: the reconciling texts on the Old Testament judicial laws (Aquinas I-II q.104 a.3; Westminster Confession 19.4), Ephesians 6:5 to 8, Colossians 3:22 to 25, Philemon 16, Sirach 30, 42:5 and 7:20 to 21 (numbering differs between editions), Chrysostom and the later papal texts, and Gregory of Nyssa was checked only through secondary scholarship. Not searched: any modern church statement forbidding all physical punishment of children, Orthodox and Protestant doctrinal teaching on slavery and on violence in marriage, and Protestant repudiations of earlier defences of slavery (for example the Southern Baptist Convention's 1995 resolution, a known lead).

### 10. Islam

**Summary.** The Qur'an and the sound (*ṣaḥīḥ*) hadith treat slavery as lawful. A man may have sexual relations with women his right hand possesses, including married women taken captive, and the Prophet himself sold a slave. Other counted texts command kindness to slaves, encourage freeing them and forbid enslaving a free person. Those with power over others are told not to torment people and not to insult or overburden servants. They must feed and clothe slaves as they feed and clothe themselves, free a slave they have slapped or beaten without cause, and pay workers. Qur'an 4:34 allows a husband, as the last of three steps, to strike a wife whose ill conduct he fears, and hadith limit this to a strike that is not severe. A hadith graded *ḥasan* (good) tells parents to strike children of ten who neglect prayer. The Prophet is reported never to have struck a woman or a servant, to have taught without striking, and to have said that men who beat their wives are not the best, but no counted text withdraws the permission. Sunni and Twelver Shia Muslims share the Qur'anic texts. All the verified hadith here are Sunni (Shia collections were not checked), and the small Qur'an-only minority is bound by the Qur'anic texts alone.

**How the tradition reconciles these texts.** On striking wives, the first limits come from the primary texts themselves. Qur'an 4:34 ends the steps once the wife obeys, and the Farewell Sermon hadith (Muslim 1218a, Ibn Mājah 1851) restrict the strike to one that is not severe. The Prophet's example (Muslim 2328a), his saying that men who beat their wives are not the best (Abū Dāwūd 2146) and Bukhārī's chapter disapproving of beating women express a clear preference for not striking. These texts limit and discourage the permission but do not suspend it. The classical commentator Ibn Kathīr, a Sunni authority used for reconciliation, adds that the strike must not be violent and must not touch the face. The gloss attributed to al-Ṭabarī that would make the strike symbolic is unverified, and modern fatwa bodies and reformist readings were not checked. On slavery, the primary texts limit abuse (no beating without an offence, no slapping, no forced prostitution, no enslaving the free) and encourage freeing slaves, but none forbids ownership, and classical law treated ownership as lawful. The tradition's repudiation of slavery comes from a modern source, the 2014 open letter of more than 120 scholars, which declares slavery abolished by universal consensus and forbids its reintroduction. This rests on consensus, not on a primary text. Apart from that modern consensus, no reconciling authority was found for the permission of sexual relations with captive and slave women.

**Result for each wording.**
- L1 (no cruelty): unclear. The counted texts teach it explicitly (Muslim 2613, Bukhārī 30, Muslim 1657 to 1659, Qur'an 4:19 and 4:36), but Qur'an 23:5 to 6 and 4:24, read with Muslim 1456, permit sexual relations with captive and slave women without mentioning consent, and Qur'an 4:34 permits a strike limited to "not severe". The texts checked do not settle whether either counts as injuring or humiliating.
- L2 (no one owned): contradicted. Qur'an 23:5 to 6 and 4:24 (Tier 1), with Muslim 1456 and the Prophet's own sale of a slave in Bukhārī 2141, treat slaves as lawfully held and traded. No counted text forbids ownership, and its repudiation rests on modern consensus (IS-6).
- L3 (no hitting): contradicted. Qur'an 4:34 (Tier 1) permits a husband to strike his wife as the last step, and Muslim 1218a repeats this with the limit "not severe". Abū Dāwūd 495 (*ḥasan*) commands striking children of ten over prayer, and Muslim 1657 leaves the punishment of a slave for a real offence open. No counted text was found permitting the striking of pupils.

**Gaps.** Several texts are unverified: al-Ṭabarī's glosses on 4:34, the Qur'anic verses on freeing slaves (IS-5), and further texts left out of the table (Qur'an 2:231, 4:3, 6:151, 17:31, 70:29 to 30; Bukhārī 6038 and 893; Nahj al-Balāgha Letter 53). These were not searched: Shia hadith (al-Kāfī), classical rulings on a captive's consent and on limits to striking children, modern fatwa bodies on striking wives and children, and the Cairo Declaration (1990), Article 11. Whether a *ḥasan* hadith from an optional-lead collection (IS-9) counts is a corpus question for the project lead.

### 11. Sikhism

**Summary.** For Sikhism, only the Guru Granth Sahib counts, and no passage found in it permits owning people, hitting dependants or treating them cruelly. It uses slavery mainly as a picture of devotion to God, and it grieves over women led into captivity in Babur's invasion, but it never states that no one may be held as property. It gives no rule addressed to masters of servants or workers, or to husbands, parents or teachers. Its support comes through wider teachings: no harsh word to anyone, no bullying of the poor and weak, no contempt for women, and no religious merit in widow-burning. On children and pupils, only two texts were found, and both are unverified: a father who teaches, scolds and then embraces his child, and the story of Prahlad, whose father threatens him in a rage when his teachers report him and is destroyed as a tyrant. Neither is a rule about hitting. All Sikh groups accept the Guru Granth Sahib, so these findings hold across branches. The Sikh Rehat Maryada gives explicit household rules (do not kill daughters, take no money for a child's marriage), but it is Tier 2 and is the mainstream Khalsa code, which some groups may not follow in full (general knowledge, not verified).

**How the tradition reconciles these texts.** No counted text was found that goes against these three wordings, so the tradition has nothing here to limit, suspend or repudiate. Its reconciling work is about interpretation. Within the tradition, the scripture's slave imagery (SI-6, SI-7) is understood as describing the devotee's bond with God, not as permission for one person to own another. Sikh readers take the Babarvani (SI-5) as a protest against tyranny, not as resigned acceptance. Commentary (for example gurbani.org, a secondary source) reads ang 787 (SI-9) as a critique of widow-burning and says that Guru Amar Das campaigned against it. The Sikh Rehat Maryada, the code of conduct approved by the SGPC in 1945 and published in 1950, is a modern body's authority. It turns the scripture's wider teaching into explicit rules for the household (SI-13, SI-14). It is the official code for mainstream Khalsa Sikhs, but it is Tier 2 and does not decide anything on its own. No statement by the Akal Takht or the SGPC on slavery, domestic violence or the physical punishment of children was found.

**Result for each wording.**
- L1 (no cruelty): not contradicted, and taught. No counted text permits cruelty to dependants. Clear teaching through the wider rule comes from ang 1384 (no harsh word to anyone, SI-1) and ang 199 (no bullying of the poor and weak, SI-3), with support from ang 1427, 473 and 787, although no text names starving or overworking dependants.
- L2 (no one owned): not contradicted, but not taught to the counting standard. No counted text permits holding or trading people. The nearest texts are only Implied: the Babarvani lament at ang 417 (unverified), the common origin of all people at ang 1349, and the Rehat Maryada's ban on taking money for a child's marriage (Tier 2). The "purchased slave" image at ang 396 is devotional context.
- L3 (no hitting): not contradicted, but not taught to the counting standard. No counted text permits or commands physical punishment of children, spouses, servants or pupils. The texts that point against it do so only by argument: ang 1378 (non-retaliation), ang 1384 (no harsh word) and the father image at ang 624 (unverified).

**Gaps.** The Vaaran Bhai Gurdas, the contested Dasam Granth (including the Charitropakhyan) and any Akal Takht or SGPC statement were not searched on these topics. The Guru Granth Sahib was searched only through targeted queries, not read in full, so a passage on beating children, wives or servants could have been missed. Kabir's harsher verses on women, which bear on the "humiliate" part of L1, were not checked to the project's standard. SI-5, SI-10 and SI-11 are unverified, and SI-4, SI-6 and SI-7 rest on the researcher's search alone. The article numbers in the Rehat Maryada are unconfirmed.

### 12. Bahá'í Faith

**Summary.** Bahá'u'lláh's Kitáb-i-Aqdas forbids trading in slaves and buying another person. His answer on household service treats a maid as a free, paid worker who may marry when she chooses. Tier 1 does not separately mention a slave someone already holds, but Shoghi Effendi's authorised summary reads the Aqdas as prohibiting slavery itself. Tier 1 also forbids striking another person, and any act that saddens hearts, with no exception for the household. It also tells people not to wish for others what they would not wish for themselves. 'Abdu'l-Bahá, whose writings the tradition treats as near-primary, says it is not permissible to strike or verbally abuse a child, and a 1993 letter on behalf of the Universal House of Justice says a husband must never beat his wife or treat her cruelly. Later authorised letters read 'Abdu'l-Bahá's ban as aimed at beating, condemn abuse of children and leave gentle physical discipline to parents' discretion, though two of the three were not verified here. Every Bahá'í group accepts the writings of Bahá'u'lláh and 'Abdu'l-Bahá. Small groups that reject the present House of Justice would not be bound by its letters, and their positions were not researched. No text on pupils was found, and the treatment of hired workers was not searched.

**How the tradition reconciles these texts.** The tradition reads its texts through a fixed line of authority. Bahá'u'lláh's writings are scripture. 'Abdu'l-Bahá is the authorised interpreter, and his writings are treated as near-primary. Shoghi Effendi is the next authorised interpreter, and the Universal House of Justice is the elected body that explains and legislates where the texts leave matters open. On slavery, Shoghi Effendi's summary (BA-4, verified) extends ¶72 from the trade to slavery itself. The House of Justice's Synopsis (BA-5, unverified) is reported to keep the narrower "slave trading", and neither allows anyone to be held. On children, a letter written on behalf of Shoghi Effendi (BA-11, unverified) limits 'Abdu'l-Bahá's ban (BA-9) to beating. It says the ban does not necessarily cover every form of corporal punishment and leaves the matter, for now, to parents' wise discretion. A verified 1939 letter (BA-10) calls some discipline, possibly including physical discipline, indispensable. A 1996 House of Justice letter (BA-12, unverified, from a modern body binding the mainstream branch) calls gentle, patient physical discipline acceptable and condemns abuse. These letters limit how far the counted ban reaches; they do not repudiate it, and they keep the ban on beating and abuse. On spouses, the House of Justice's 1993 letter (BA-13) turns the general ban on striking (¶148) into an explicit rule against wife-beating. For small groups that reject the present House, this rule would rest on ¶148 and on the equality of women and men. Because letters on behalf of Shoghi Effendi carry the authority of the Guardian, the narrowing in BA-11 would bind every group that accepts him, not only the mainstream.

This is Clear teaching rather than Explicit because Tier 1 has no household-specific rule.
- L2 (no one owned): not contradicted, taught. Kitáb-i-Aqdas ¶72, the Tablet to Queen Victoria and Questions and Answers no. 30 (Tier 1) explicitly forbid trading in or buying people. That no one may be held rests on Shoghi Effendi's authorised summary (BA-4, Tier 2), because Tier 1 does not separately address holding.
- L3 (no hitting): unclear. On their face the counted texts teach it: ¶148 forbids striking another, and Selections no. 95 says it is not permissible to strike a child. However, letters at the level of the authorised interpreter and the House of Justice (BA-10 to BA-12; BA-11 and BA-12 are unverified) read that ban as aimed at beating and accept gentle physical discipline of children. If those letters are verified, the result becomes contradicted for children only. Spouses are covered by BA-13 (mainstream branch). For servants and pupils, only the general rule in ¶148 exists.

### 13. Secular humanism

**Summary.** Humanism has no scripture. Its counted texts are short consensus declarations by modern humanist bodies, written after slavery was legally abolished, and none of them names slavery, servants, spouses, or the physical punishment of children or pupils. Instead they set out wider rules that cover every person: "helping and not harming", the inherent worth and dignity of each person, a world free of cruelty, and universal human rights with the greatest possible freedom for every human (Declaration of Modern Humanism 2022; Humanist Manifesto III). Explicit opposition to slavery and to the corporal punishment of children appears only in Humanists International policy and UN statements (outside the corpus) and in Humanists UK material (Tier 2). Nothing specific was found on spouses or on servants and workers in any tier. No humanist text found in any tier permits slavery, cruelty to dependants or physical punishment, and no difference between branches was found, but the founding text of the Council for Secular Humanism branch (1980) has not been read in full for these topics.

**How the tradition reconciles these texts.** No humanist text found in any tier permits slavery, cruelty to dependants or physical punishment, so on these points there is nothing for the tradition to reconcile. Humanism has no scripture and no classical commentary. Its texts are revisable consensus statements by modern bodies, and later versions replace earlier ones: the 2022 Declaration replaced the 2002 text, and the American Humanist Association has declared Manifestos I and II historic and superseded by Manifesto III. The bodies that apply the declarations' general principles to named practices are modern ones: the Humanists International General Assembly (the 2008 policy against corporal punishment of children, still listed as pending review, and the 2009 UN statement against slavery) and Humanists UK (campaign releases and UN statements). These do not limit, suspend or repudiate any counted text; they state explicitly, for particular practices, what the declarations put in general terms. Under sources/corpus.md they are outside the corpus or Tier 2, so they may explain but cannot decide a verdict on their own. The 1996 Minimum Statement is the one text every member organisation of Humanists International must accept, which makes it the broadest common text in the tradition, but it speaks only in general terms.

**Result for each wording.**
- L1 (no cruelty): not contradicted, taught; the 2022 Declaration's "helping and not harming" (HU-1) and Manifesto III's inherent worth and dignity of each person and hope for a world free of cruelty (HU-2) are Clear teaching, but only as a wider rule under decision D3, since no counted text names households, servants or people in one's power.
- L2 (no one owned): not contradicted, taught by principle (borderline); the 2022 Declaration's "universal legal human rights" (HU-5) and Manifesto III's equal enjoyment of human rights (HU-6) uphold instruments that ban slavery by name, but no counted text uses the word slavery, so if the lead requires the practice to be named this falls to Implied, and the explicit condemnations (HU-9, HU-10) are outside the corpus or Tier 2.
- L3 (no hitting): not contradicted, but silent on the counted texts (Implied only); Manifesto III's hope that differences be resolved "without resorting to violence" (HU-2) does not address discipline, the explicit positions are the Humanists International 2008 policy (HU-11, outside the corpus) and Humanists UK (HU-12, Tier 2), both covering children (and, through teachers, pupils) only, and nothing was found in any tier on spouses or servants.

**Gaps.** The 1980 Secular Humanist Declaration (Tier 1 for the Council for Secular Humanism branch), Humanist Manifesto I (1933) and the 2002 Amsterdam Declaration were not read in full for these topics, and two further items remain UNVERIFIED and are left out of the table (Manifesto II's passages on abused children and on equal rights for women and men, recalled from memory; the 2002 Declaration's duty of care, seen only in secondary copies). Humanist Manifesto 2000 was searched but its text was not returned, Kurtz's writings and any Humanists International resolutions on violence against women were not searched, the current standing of the 2008 policy was not checked, and the finding that no humanist text permits slavery or hitting rests on 22 searches, so it is a negative result from limited searching, not proof of absence.

## Gaps that apply to every section

- **How the checks were made.** Every citation was checked through web search results, not by reading the source page in full, because the reference sites could not be opened from the working environment. Some rows rest on a single search result or on a scholarly description of a text rather than the text itself; the Verified column says so.
- **Modern authoritative bodies** were searched unevenly. For several traditions (for example Judaism, Orthodox and Protestant Christianity, Zoroastrianism and Sikhism) no statement by a present-day authoritative body on slavery, domestic violence or the physical punishment of children was checked. Their absence here is a gap, not evidence that none exists.
- **Hard texts that need reading in the original with the tradition's own commentators** before any verdict is final: Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad 6.4.7 with Śaṅkara; Ṛg Veda 8.56.3 and the standing of the Vālakhilya hymns; Exodus 21:20 to 21 with the Mekhilta; Babylonian Talmud Gittin 38b; the Vinaya ordination rules and Jātaka 252 in other Buddhist canons; Tattvārtha Sūtra 7.24 (Śvetāmbara) = 7.29 (Digambara) on the meaning of *dāsa*; Seneca, *De Ira* 3.32; Qur'an 4:34 and 23:5 to 6 with the classical commentators.
- **Branches searched lightly or not at all:** Karaite Judaism; Digambara texts other than the Tattvārtha Sūtra; the Chinese and Tibetan Vinayas; Shia hadith collections; Sikh codes other than the Sikh Rehat Maryada; reformist Zoroastrian statements on slavery.
