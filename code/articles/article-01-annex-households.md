# Article I, Annex: Households and Slavery

> **What this annex is for.** It sets out the evidence for decision D2 on [Article I](article-01-golden-rule.md). On 13 October 2026 the project lead decided that the article's floor must cover slavery and authority within households, and that the exact wording will be decided after reading what each tradition's texts say. This annex gives those texts, tradition by tradition. On the same day the project lead left the remaining decisions to the project's judgement, and the wording was then chosen: the article asks for *no cruelty* (L1), the lowest of the three, and the other two go to the Register as [Q-002](../../register/q-002-households-and-slavery.md). The reasons are in [`METHOD.md`](../../METHOD.md), section 9.

## The three wordings tested

Applied to families, households and people in your power, "Do not harm anyone in a way you would not want to be harmed yourself" could be read as ruling out up to three different things. The traditions' texts treat them differently, so each was tested on its own:

- **L1, no cruelty:** do not injure, torment, starve, overwork or humiliate anyone in your household or in your power.
- **L2, no one owned:** no one may be held or traded as property.
- **L3, no hitting:** no physical punishment of children, spouses, servants or pupils.

## How the evidence was gathered and counted

- **Research and checking.** For each tradition, one researcher searched the texts, and then an independent verifier, who had not done the research, was asked to find what was wrong with it. The verifier's corrections are used here. All checking was done through web search, so citations are marked "VERIFIED (search)" or "UNVERIFIED" as defined in [`METHOD.md`](../../METHOD.md), section 3. An unverified citation does not count either way.
- **Counted texts.** A text counts if it is Tier 1, or a Tier 2 text that [`sources/corpus.md`](../../sources/corpus.md) says the tradition treats as primary. Other texts are shown because they limit, explain or reconcile the counted ones, or because adherents would expect to see them, but they do not decide anything on their own.
- **Fairness.** Each section gives the tradition's own reconciliation of its hard texts, with how strong that reconciliation is (a primary text, a classical authority, or a modern body). Nothing here judges which tradition is right, and nothing describes what adherents do today: the test is what the texts teach (the project's settled decision 2).

### How each result is labelled

The same rules are applied to every tradition. They were written after the second independent check found that the first draft of this annex had labelled similar texts differently.

1. **Only counted texts decide a label.** A text that is not counted, or is unverified, never decides a label on its own; it is reported beside the label. The one exception is the narrowing reading allowed by rule 6.
2. **Contradicted.** A counted text accepted by every major branch permits, commands or approves what the wording forbids, for at least one relationship (the floor covers all of them, so failing for one is failing). This includes a law that leaves a harm unpunished because of the victim's status, and a text that treats a harm as within a master's or parent's proper power while faulting only how it is done (in haste, in anger, to excess).
3. **Contradicted in one branch.** The same, but only in texts that one major branch accepts. The branch is named, and so is whether the other branches' texts were found silent or were not checked.
4. **Presupposing is not permitting.** A text that only presupposes a practice, describes it without approval, uses it as an image or parable, or tells the person who suffers it to bear it patiently, is Implied evidence and does not decide a label, unless it also says that the practice itself is right.
5. **Courts and war are outside the floor** (Article I, counter-evidence section A), even when a member of the household sets the procedure going. Texts about courts, rulers' punishments and war are reported as context only.
6. **Unclear.** A counted text bears directly on the wording, but its meaning or reach is unresolved: a disputed word, a reading that the tradition's own counted texts, its classical commentators, or a verified ruling of the body that corpus.md lists as authorised to elucidate its texts (in this annex, only the Universal House of Justice for the Bahá'í Faith; `METHOD.md`, section 9, decision 8) narrow, or a hard case that the wording does not plainly cover (for example, sexual relations without stated consent).
7. **Not contradicted, taught.** A counted text teaches the wording at the Explicit or Clear teaching level. For L1, a wider duty not to harm that reaches everyone also counts (METHOD.md, section 3), because cruelty is harm on every tradition's own terms. For L2 and L3 a wider duty does not count on its own, because whether holding a person as property, or moderate physical correction, is a harm is exactly what the texts dispute; several traditions teach the wider duty and accept these practices in the same body of texts. So for L2 and L3 the counted texts must address the practice itself, directly or by plain reference (a clause reaches a practice by plain reference when the practice, by definition, denies what the clause guarantees and the tradition's counted texts nowhere accept the practice; `METHOD.md`, section 9, decision 9). **Taught in part** means that a counted text covers only part of the wording (for example, trading in people but not holding them).
8. **Not contradicted, silent.** No counted text was found either way. Silence does not teach the wording.
9. **What "injure" means in L1.** "Injure" is read as harm beyond moderate physical correction; moderate correction is tested under L3. The Summary shows what changes if "injure" is read to include any physical punishment.

- **No cruelty (L1)** is taught, directly or through a wider duty not to harm, in seven traditions, and contradicted by a counted text in four. In Hinduism the contradicting text is Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad 6.4.7 (a husband told to hit a wife who refuses him, which Śaṅkara takes as written). In Judaism and Christianity it is Exodus 21:20 to 21 (a master left unpunished if a beaten slave survives a day or two, and in the rabbinic reading even if the slave then dies), with Sirach 33 and 42:5 for Catholics and Orthodox. In Stoicism it is Seneca's *De Ira* 3.32 (the lash and the breaking of a slave's legs treated as within a master's power, faulting only haste and anger). It is unclear in Islam, because counted texts permit sexual relations with captive and slave women without stating consent (Qur'an 23:5 to 6 and 4:24; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 1456; Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 4138), and the Torah's captive-woman law (Deuteronomy 21:10 to 14) raises the same question for Judaism and Christianity; and in Zoroastrianism, because the Gathas commend harming, or opposing, the follower of the Lie (Yasna 33.2, 47.4) and teach L1 itself only at the Implied level in texts every branch accepts.
- **No one owned (L2)** is taught in counted texts by two traditions: the Bahá'í Faith (Kitáb-i-Aqdas ¶72) and secular humanism (by principle, through the declarations' clauses on every person's freedom and autonomy; slavery is not named). It is contradicted in five traditions and in one branch of two more. For Hinduism the contradiction rests on a single verse in a Vālakhilya hymn of disputed standing (Ṛg Veda 8.56.3). It is unclear for Jainism, where the one text every branch accepts lists *dāsas* among a householder's holdings and translators read the word as "servants".
- **No hitting (L3)** is not clearly taught by any tradition's counted texts. The Bahá'í and Jain texts come nearest: Kitáb-i-Aqdas ¶148 and 'Abdu'l-Bahá forbid striking (though the Universal House of Justice accepts gentle physical discipline of children), and the Tattvārtha Sūtra lists beating as a lapse of the vow of non-injury (though commentators narrow it to cruel beating). It is contradicted in five traditions and in one branch of a sixth.
- **What "injure" means.** If "injure" in L1 is read to include any physical punishment, every counted text against L3 also counts against L1. Then L1 is contradicted in five traditions (Islam is added, through Qur'an 4:34) and in one branch of Buddhism (Jātaka 252), unclear in three (Zoroastrianism, Jainism, Bahá'í Faith), and taught only in Confucianism, Taoism, Sikhism and secular humanism. L1 is then hardly nearer to admission than L3.
- **A lower floor.** The method asks for a lower floor to be tried before a question goes to the Register. The lowest household floor that still means something is "do not kill, maim, starve or torment anyone in your power". On the evidence already in this annex (no further searching was done for this step), it would still fail: it is contradicted by Seneca's *De Ira* 3.32 (breaking a slave's legs), by Exodus 21:20 to 21 in the rabbinic reading (no death penalty for a master whose beaten slave dies after a day), and, for Catholics and Orthodox, by Sirach 33, the passage on servants ("tortures and torments for an evil servant"; verse number not confirmed, CH-9); it is unclear for Hinduism (whether Bṛhadāraṇyaka 6.4.7 is "torment"), Islam (the consent question) and Zoroastrianism (Yasna 33.2). This step should itself be checked before it is relied on.
- **The traditions' own reconciliations.** Some contradicting texts have reconciliations that are themselves counted texts: in Rabbinic Judaism the Mekhilta and the Talmud limit Exodus 21:20 (a master who kills his slave outright is liable to death, though not if the slave lingers a day before dying); in Islam the Prophet's farewell sermon (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 1218a) limits the strike in Qur'an 4:34 to one that is not severe. These limit the texts; none removes the contradiction. Many traditions' later authorities go further and reject slavery outright (for example the Catechism of the Catholic Church 2414; Shoghi Effendi for the Bahá'í Faith; an open letter of more than 120 Muslim scholars in 2014, which rests on a claim of consensus (*ijmāʿ*), a recognised source of Islamic law; and individual Hindu, Jewish, Buddhist, Christian and Muslim signatories of a 2014 joint declaration against modern slavery), but these are not counted texts. No reconciliation of Bṛhadāraṇyaka 6.4.7 or of Seneca's *De Ira* 3.32 was found.
- **A pattern to note.** The two traditions whose counted texts teach L2 are the two whose founding texts were written after the abolition of slavery in law had begun in many countries (the Kitáb-i-Aqdas in 1873, the humanist declarations from 1952), though it was not yet abolished everywhere. This is recorded as part of the historical setting of the question, not as a verdict.
- **General questions this annex raised, and how they were decided** (13 October 2026, under the project lead's delegation; reasons in [`METHOD.md`](../../METHOD.md), section 9). Historical schools with no living community, such as Han to Tang classical learning, are not branches, so the Five Classics stay uncounted for Confucianism. A hadith from the other Sunni collections counts for Sunni Islam when graded sound or good, so Abū Dāwūd 495 now counts (Islam's L3 result was already *contradicted* through Qur'an 4:34). Karaite Judaism is a branch. Later authorities never outweigh a contradicting counted text in this test, but are the main evidence for the Logical parts of the Register. A verified ruling of the Universal House of Justice may make a Bahá'í result *unclear* (rule 6). A Tier 2 text is "treated as primary" only where the tradition places it with its primary scripture, so ʿAbdu'l-Bahá's writings count and the Mahabharata does not. A clause guaranteeing every person's legal freedom and autonomy reaches slavery "by plain reference" (rule 7). None of these decisions changed a label in this annex.


## The evidence, tradition by tradition

### 1. Hinduism

**Summary.** No counted (Tier 1) text commands slavery, but a Ṛg Veda verse in a Vālakhilya hymn (a group of hymns whose standing is disputed) praises a patron's gift of a hundred slaves listed alongside asses and sheep, and a verse of the Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad, in a rite for begetting a child, tells a husband to hit a wife who refuses him. Śaṅkara's classical commentary on that verse follows its plain reading and does not limit the striking. Under the labelling rules these two verses between them contradict all three wordings, and the L2 result rests on the single Vālakhilya verse. No verified Tier 1 text speaks either way about hitting children, pupils or servants, no counted text teaches L2 or L3, and Tier 1 teaches non-harm towards people in one's household only through general rules that reach every being (Bhagavad Gita 6.32, rated Clear teaching, though the rating is borderline). The law books (Tier 2, flagged as contested in corpus.md, and not counted) treat slavery as a legal institution and permit corrective beating of a son, pupil, wife or slave within limits of instrument and body part; other law books provide ways out of slavery, forbid selling children and say that as a rule a pupil is not to be hit. Branches differ mainly over the law books: Vedānta ranks revealed scripture above them where they conflict, the Arya Samaj is reported to accept Manu only where it agrees with the Vedas, and anti-caste movements are reported to reject it (neither report is verified here); the rule that revealed scripture outranks the law books cannot reach the two Tier 1 texts, which are themselves revealed scripture.

**How the tradition reconciles these texts.** Hinduism has no single body that can rule for the whole tradition, so each reconciliation binds only the schools that accept it. The main classical rule is that remembered texts, including the law books, give way to revealed scripture where they conflict; it is verified here as Mīmāṃsā Sūtra 1.3.3 cited by Śaṅkara (a classical commentator, HI-13) and recorded in corpus.md, while Manusmṛti 2.13, which states the same rule, was not re-checked. That rule can limit or suspend law-book rules, but it cannot reach Bṛhadāraṇyaka 6.4.7 or Ṛg Veda 8.56.3, which are themselves revealed scripture. No classical authority was found that limits or rereads either verse. Śaṅkara's commentary on Bṛhadāraṇyaka 6.4.7 (HI-5, verified) follows the plain reading: in Mādhavānanda's translation the husband is to strike a wife who is still unyielding, and Śaṅkara explains the curse as making her known as barren and unfortunate, so it offers no reconciliation; Rāmānuja and Madhva on the verse were not searched. Within the law books, classical texts limit rather than forbid: Nārada provides ways out of slavery (HI-9), Āpastamba bars the sale of children (HI-10), Gautama and the commentator Medhātithi restrict how and when a pupil or son may be struck (HI-12), and Yājñavalkya 2.182 (freeing those enslaved by force) and Medhātithi's reading of Manu 8.414 as rhetoric rather than a binding rule are reported but unverified. Manusmṛti's own authority is contested (corpus.md): the Arya Samaj accepts it only where it agrees with the Vedas (not verified here; it is also reported, unverified, to rank the Upanishads below the four Saṃhitās), Gandhi accepted it only where it agreed with truth and non-violence (not verified here), and anti-caste movements reject it, as B. R. Ambedkar did by burning it publicly in 1927 (not verified here). Modern repudiation of slavery comes from individual teachers, such as Mata Amritanandamayi's 2014 signature (HI-14, verified, outside the corpus), not from a tradition-wide ruling.

**Result for each wording.**
- L1 (no cruelty): **Contradicted**. Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad 6.4.7 (HI-4, Tier 1, verified) tells a husband to strike and overcome a wife who refuses him, with a formula that takes away her glory, which is force against a stated refusal rather than moderate correction (rules 2 and 9), and the label is not Unclear because Śaṅkara (HI-5) follows the plain reading and the only dispute found over the verb comes from modern writers outside the corpus (rule 6). Tier 1 otherwise teaches L1 only through the wider duty of Gita 6.32 (HI-6, Clear teaching, borderline), which cannot outweigh a contradiction for one relationship (rule 2).
- L2 (no one owned): **Contradicted**. Ṛg Veda 8.56.3 (HI-1, Tier 1, verified) praises a patron's gift of a hundred slaves counted with asses and sheep, which approves the practice rather than only presupposing it (rules 2 and 4), but this rests on one verse in a Vālakhilya hymn whose standing is disputed. If those hymns were set aside, L2 would rest only on the law books (HI-8, Tier 2, not counted) and would become Not contradicted, silent in counted texts, unless the unverified leads Chāndogya Upaniṣad 7.24.2 or Ṛg Veda 10.62.10 were confirmed.
- L3 (no hitting): **Contradicted**. For spouses, Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad 6.4.7 (HI-4, Tier 1, verified) directs a husband to strike a refusing wife with a stick or with his hand, and Śaṅkara (HI-5) does not limit it. For children, pupils and servants no verified Tier 1 text was found either way; the law books permit limited corrective beating of them (HI-11) but are Tier 2 and do not count on their own (rule 1).

**Gaps.** Unverified leads on L2: Chāndogya Upaniṣad 7.24.2 (recalled as listing slaves among possessions) and Ṛg Veda 10.62.10. If confirmed, either could keep L2 contradicted even if the Vālakhilya hymns were set aside, depending on whether it approves the practice or only presupposes it (rule 4). Not verified: Ṛg Veda 8.19.36, Aitareya Brāhmaṇa 7.29, Gita 16.1 to 3 (which would give HI-6 a firmer rating) and 5.18, Yājñavalkya 2.182, Medhātithi on Manu 8.414, Manu 2.13, 4.179 to 185 (bearing offences from wife, children and slaves without resentment) and 10.63 (non-injury in the summary of the law for all classes), the Arya Samaj's, Gandhi's and Ambedkar's positions on Manusmṛti, and the effect of the Vālakhilya hymns' standing on their authority. Śaṅkara on Bṛhadāraṇyaka 6.4.7 (HI-5) was verified on wisdomlib, which is not on the project's list of verification sites. Not searched: Bṛhadāraṇyaka 6.2.7 (also recalled as listing slaves among possessions), Rāmānuja and Madhva on 6.4.7, the Purāṇas and the Mahabharata on servants and dependants, and any modern Hindu body's statement on hitting children, wives or pupils, so silence there is a research gap, not a finding.

### 2. Judaism

**Summary.** The Tanakh allows slaves from other nations to be bought and held as permanent, inheritable property (Leviticus 25:44 to 46) and calls a slave his master's "money" (Exodus 21:21). It leaves a master unpunished if a slave he beats with a rod survives a day or two (Exodus 21:20 to 21), which Rashi and the Mekhilta read to include a slave who lingers a full day and then dies. It lets women and children captured in war be kept as spoil (Numbers 31:18; Deuteronomy 20:14), lets a soldier take a captive woman as his wife without mentioning her consent (Deuteronomy 21:10 to 14), and regulates a father's sale of a young daughter into service. It also limits a master's power: Israelites are not to be sold as bondmen or ruled harshly, a released Hebrew servant goes out with provisions, slaves share the weekly rest, a maimed slave goes free, kidnapping is a capital crime, an escaped slave is not handed back, a captive wife who is sent away may not be sold, and Job grounds a servant's claims in the same Maker. Proverbs commends the rod for a child, and the Mishnah and Talmud treat a father's or teacher's physical discipline as lawful while limiting it, the Talmud counting a father's striking of a son he is teaching as the fulfilment of a commandment; for wives, no Tanakh or Talmud text was found that permits striking, the Talmud asks a husband to love and honour his wife, and the later codes divide (unverified). Rabbinic Judaism (Orthodox, and in principle Conservative or Masorti) reads the Tanakh through the Mishnah and Talmud, which add both limits and a ruling against freeing non-Israelite slaves; Karaites accept only the Tanakh, and Reform and Reconstructionist Judaism treat the rabbinic texts as valued but not binding. No modern body's statements were checked; two rabbis signed a 2014 interfaith declaration against modern slavery as individuals.

**How the tradition reconciles these texts.** In Rabbinic Judaism the Mishnah, the tannaitic midrash and the Talmud (treated as primary) and, below them, the classical commentators and codes reconcile the hard texts, and they limit rather than repeal. ### 3. Zoroastrianism

**Summary.** The Gathas, the only texts every branch accepts, were not found to mention slavery, servants, or the physical discipline of children, spouses or pupils. Their one household passage (Yasna 53.4 to 53.5) has a bride promise to honour her father and her husband, and asks husband and wife to treat each other with righteousness (*asha*). Two Gathic stanzas, Yasna 33.2 and 47.4, commend harming, or setting oneself against, the follower of the Lie, who could be someone in one's power; whether that contradicts L1 is unresolved, as in Article I, decision D1. Traditionalists also accept the Vendidad, which punishes striking any person with evil intent while itself using flogging as a court penalty, and they treat the Pahlavi books as authoritative: the Shayast-ne-Shayast carries the Vendidad's grades of assault into its scale of sins, and a general maxim forbids doing to another what is not good for oneself. For traditionalists, a Pahlavi text also contradicts L2: Denkard Book 8, summarising a lost Avestan legal book (the Hūspāram Nask), lists among its contents rules about selling slaves along with beasts of burden, cattle and other property. Outside the corpus, the Sasanian law book treats slaves as property and the later Persian Rivayats answer questions about household slaves. Reformists ("Gathas-only") are bound by none of the Vendidad, Pahlavi or legal texts, so for them the Gathas alone decide, and on slavery and physical punishment the Gathas appear silent.

### 4. Buddhism

**Summary.** The Pali monastic code (Vinaya), which is Tier 1 and part of the Theravāda branch's canon, treats slavery as a lawful status: it bars slaves from becoming monks out of deference to their owners' claim, and it asks every candidate for full ordination whether he is a free man. Trading in people is forbidden to lay followers (AN 5.177) and, for East Asian Mahāyāna, to bodhisattvas (Brahmajāla precept 12, whose text is still to be re-checked), but no counted text found tells lay people not to own slaves. The Sigālovāda Sutta (DN 31) requires masters to give their slaves or servants and workers fitting work, food, wages, care in sickness and time off, and requires husbands to honour their wives; general verses such as Dhammapada 129 to 130 tell everyone not to strike others, taking themselves as the measure; and SN 3.9 describes a king's slaves and workers preparing, under threat of punishment and in tears, a sacrifice that the Buddha rejects. On pupils, a canonical Jātaka verse (Ja 252) calls restraint with a stick instruction, not hostility, and its commentary story approves a teacher who had his pupil beaten. The monastic rules forbid a monk to hit or raise a hand against another in anger, and allow a novice who harms the monks to be punished only by an off-limits order confined to his own lodging, not by withholding food. No counted text was found on physically punishing children, and none permits or forbids striking a wife (one praises a wife who bears her husband's threat of the rod patiently, which does not decide anything under the labelling rules). The contrary texts are Pali (Theravāda canon); the Dharmaguptaka and Mūlasarvāstivāda Vinayas, which are the monastic codes of the East Asian and Tibetan branches, and those branches' other texts were not checked on these points.

**How the tradition reconciles these texts.** Within the counted texts, two primary rules limit slavery without ending it: AN 5.177 (Tier 1) forbids lay followers to trade in beings, which the Theravāda commentary, as reported at second hand, reads as people, and Brahmajāla light precept 12 (primary scripture for East Asian Mahāyāna; its text is still to be re-checked on CBETA) forbids bodhisattvas to sell free people or slaves. DN 31 (Tier 1) limits a master's power by setting duties of care towards slaves and workers. On correction, the Vinaya makes it an offence for a monk to hit or raise a hand against another in anger (Pācittiya 74 and 75), and allows a novice who harms the monks to be punished only by an off-limits order confined to his own lodging, not by withholding food (Mahāvagga I.57); these rules bind monks only. No counted text, classical commentary or treatise was found that repudiates the holding of slaves by lay people; only monastics renounce accepting them (DN 1, unverified). The one repudiation found is modern: the 2014 Joint Declaration, signed by two senior Buddhist teachers as individuals, calls modern slavery a crime against humanity, but Buddhism has no body that speaks for all Buddhists, so it binds no one else. On physical correction, the Jātaka commentary itself frames Ja 252 as a lesson for a monk quick to anger to accept correction without resentment; no classical or modern authority was found that reconciles the verse with Dhammapada 129 to 130 or with Mahāvagga I.57, and none was searched for (see Gaps).

**Result for each wording.**
- L1 (no cruelty): **Not contradicted, taught**. DN 31 (Tier 1) directly requires masters to give their slaves or servants and workers fitting work, food, wages, care in sickness and time off, and husbands to honour their wives (BU-6, BU-8), Mahāvagga I.57 (BU-15) makes it an offence for monks to withhold food from a novice as a punishment, and Dhammapada 129 to 130 adds the wider duty not to strike anyone. No counted text was found that permits cruelty to dependants; Jātaka 252 is tested under L3 (rule 9), and would bear on L1 in the Theravāda canon only if "injure" were read to include any physical punishment.
- L2 (no one owned): **Contradicted in one branch (Theravāda)**. The Pali Vinaya ruling at Mahāvagga I.47 (BU-1, Tier 1, Theravāda canon) bars slaves from ordination to protect their owners' claim, so it accepts the holding of people as property; the Dharmaguptaka and Mūlasarvāstivāda Vinayas of the East Asian and Tibetan branches were not checked, and the ordination question (BU-2) and DN 31 (BU-6) only presuppose slavery (rule 4). The trading half is taught, by AN 5.177 for lay followers and, for East Asian Mahāyāna, by Brahmajāla precept 12 (to be re-checked), but no counted text forbids owning people.
- L3 (no hitting): **Contradicted in one branch (Theravāda)**. The closing verse of Jātaka 252 (BU-10, Tier 1 in the Theravāda canon) calls restraint with a stick instruction, not hostility, which approves it (rule 2); the other branches' canons were not checked. Mahāvagga I.57 (BU-15) limits how monks may punish novices, and Pācittiya 74 and 75 (BU-12) forbid a monk to strike another monk in anger or to raise a hand in anger against anyone (whether striking a novice in anger is itself an offence was not checked), but they bind monks only and leave the verse's approval in place for everyone else; Dhammapada 129 to 130 is a wider duty that does not count for L3 (rule 7), and no counted text was found on children or spouses (AN 7.63 is Implied, rule 4).

**Gaps.** Not verified: the Pārājika 2 definition of a 'living being' that can be stolen (BU-3), the DN 31 section on parents and children, DN 1's rule that monks do not accept slaves, the DN 5 passage on a model king whose slaves and workers laboured without the rod or fear, and Brahmajāla light precept 31 on ransoming monastics sold into servitude. The text of Brahmajāla light precept 12 (BU-5) could not be reconfirmed by the second check and should be re-checked on CBETA (T1484) before it is relied on. Rows BU-7, BU-9, BU-13 and BU-14 rest on earlier searches the verifier did not repeat. Not checked: whether the Dharmaguptaka and Mūlasarvāstivāda Vinayas also ask candidates for ordination whether they are free and bar slaves (on which depends whether L2 is contradicted in more than one branch), and whether Pācittiya 74 also makes it an offence for a monk to hit a novice in anger (BU-12 records only the rule on raising a hand against novices). Not searched: the Chinese Āgama parallels and the other canonical texts of the East Asian and Tibetan branches (so those branches may be silent rather than contrary on L2 and L3), Mahāyāna and Tibetan treatises on dependants, the Aśokan edicts, and any authority reconciling Jātaka 252 with Dhammapada 129 to 130 or with Mahāvagga I.57.

### 5. Jainism

**Summary.** The one text every Jain branch accepts, the Tattvārtha Sūtra, lists binding, beating, mutilating, overloading and starving living beings as lapses of the householder's vow of non-injury. So, for every branch, beating is named among the faults, though the sūtra's own definition of harm and the Śvetāmbara commentators narrow how far the word reaches. The same sūtra lists female and male dāsas among the holdings a householder must keep within a self-set limit, without forbidding them. Both translators checked, and the Digambara commentator Pūjyapāda, read dāsa here as 'servants', but the word can also mean slaves, and which sense applies is an open textual question. The Śvetāmbara canon, which Digambaras do not accept, explicitly condemns the man who punishes his wife, children and other relatives, or his slave or hired servant, harshly for small faults. The same canon presupposes a teacher's blows in training a monastic pupil, without commanding them. Śvetāmbara Mūrtipūjaka commentators, known here only through a modern summary, read the lapse of 'beating' as cruel beating only, allowing restrained whipping, ear-pulling or slapping of a person in one's care. No verified text in either branch says that no one may be owned. Beyond the shared sūtra, no Digambara text on households or physical correction was verified.

**How the tradition reconciles these texts.** On holdings, the shared Tattvārtha Sūtra supplies its own frame (a primary text): possession is attachment (7.12 Śv. = 7.17 Dig.), householders limit it and ascetics give it up entirely (7.2). So dāsas, like land and cattle, are to be limited and ideally renounced. This limits the holding but does not forbid it. The Digambara Sarvārthasiddhi of Pūjyapāda (a classical commentary) reads dāsī-dāsa as servants, not slaves, and places the fault only in exceeding one's limit out of greed. It limits the practice and does not repudiate it. On beating, the shared sūtra names beating (vadha) among the lapses of the non-injury vow for every branch, and its own definition of harm as careless or passion-driven injury (7.8 Śv. = 7.13 Dig.) is the basis for reading the lapses as faults of passion. The Śvetāmbara commentators Siddhasena Gaṇin, Haribhadra and Devendra are classical authorities, binding for Mūrtipūjakas but not for Sthānakavāsīs and Terāpanthīs, and are known here only through a modern summary. They limit the lapse to cruel or merciless beating and expressly allow restrained whipping, ear-pulling and slapping of those in one's care. No classical authority or modern Jain body was found that repudiates the holding of dāsas or urges freeing them. No Digambara commentary on whether the lapse of beating reaches restrained correction was checked.

**Result for each wording.**
- L1 (no cruelty): **Not contradicted, taught**. For every branch, the shared Tattvārtha Sūtra 7.20 Śv. = 7.25 Dig. (JA-1) explicitly lists binding, beating, mutilating, overloading and starving living beings as lapses, and for Śvetāmbaras Ācārāṅga I.4.1 (JA-2) and Sūtrakṛtāṅga II.2 (JA-3, JA-4) add the wider duty and condemn harsh punishment of slaves, servants, wife and children. 'Humiliate' is not named in any verified text, the Digambara case rests on the shared sūtra alone, and the restrained correction that the Śvetāmbara commentators allow (JA-11) is tested under L3 (rule 9).
- L2 (no one owned): **Unclear**. The shared Tattvārtha Sūtra 7.24 Śv. = 7.29 Dig. (JA-7) lists female and male dāsas among the holdings a householder limits but may keep, and the same sūtra defines possession (parigraha) as attachment (JA-8); but both translators checked, and the Digambara commentator Pūjyapāda (JA-9), read dāsa here as 'servants', and whether it means slave is open (rule 6, a disputed word). On either reading persons are listed among holdings to be limited, no counted text forbids owning people, and Sūtrakṛtāṅga II.2 (JA-3) only presupposes slaves in households (rule 4).
- L3 (no hitting): **Unclear**. The shared Tattvārtha Sūtra 7.20 Śv. = 7.25 Dig. (JA-1) lists beating (vadha) among the lapses of the non-injury vow, which on its face bears against beating any being, for every branch; but its reach is unresolved (rule 6), because the sūtra's own definition of harm as careless or passion-driven injury (JA-12) grounds reading the lapses as faults of passion or carelessness, one translator renders vadha as 'fatal hurting', and the Śvetāmbara commentators reported in modern scholarship (JA-11, Tier 2, not counted on their own) read it as cruel beating only and allow restrained correction. No text that names moderate physical correction of children or a spouse was found, for or against: JA-4 condemns only severe punishment for small offences, and JA-1 names the beating of living beings in general. No Jain authority condemning the holding of dāsas was found, which does not show that none exists. Three further leads bear on L2 and are unverified, so they do not count: Upāsakadaśā 1 (Śvetāmbara) is reported to list, among the lapses of the householder's vow limiting possessions, exceeding one's limit on bipeds and quadrupeds (dvipada-catuṣpada); the forbidden trade kesa-vāṇijja is reported to be glossed as trade in people and animals, with Hemacandra's Yogaśāstra (Tier 2, Śvetāmbara) as the usual authority; and Uttarādhyayana 3.17 (Śvetāmbara) is reported to list slaves and servants among the goods of a fortunate family. The listed translations of the Tattvārtha Sūtra (J. L. Jaini 1920; N. Tatia) were not checked on dāsī-dāsa, and the translations actually used (Doshi; Vijay K. Jain) are not yet in sources/translations.md.

### 6. Confucianism

**Summary.** The counted texts (the Analects, the Mencius, the Great Learning and the Doctrine of the Mean) apply the rule "do not do to others what you would not want done to you" to people in your power. They also condemn particular abuses in rulers' punishments, which are reported as context only, because courts and war are outside the floor. But they never rule on whether people may be owned, and they never forbid or approve physical punishment of children, spouses, servants or pupils. The Mencius mentions the daily beating of a boy only in passing, without judging it, and the Analects records Confucius tapping an old acquaintance on the shin with his staff, outside those relationships. The Five Classics (among them the Book of Rites and the Classic of Documents) and the Classic of Filial Piety were held as classics by every historical branch, but corpus.md keeps them in Tier 2, so they do not count. They accept buying a concubine, presenting war captives (a line not yet verified on ctext) and rods for pupils, take for granted a household with bondservants ranked lowest, and tell a child to bear a beating that draws blood without resentment. The same texts also tell masters not to slight even their servants. No text was found that permits striking a wife, in a search not aimed at spouses: the Book of Rites answers an unfilial daughter-in-law with teaching, then reproof and, at worst, divorce. The branches differ in emphasis. Cheng-Zhu orthodoxy, the examination standard from 1313 to 1905, put the Four Books first, while Han to Tang classical learning treated the Five Classics as its primary canon. Both held the Five Classics canonical, so where the counted texts are silent, the wider canon is not. The silent results for L2 and L3 therefore depend on those classics staying uncounted, which is a corpus question for the project lead.

**How the tradition reconciles these texts.** Confucianism has no council or body with authority to issue binding rulings, and no modern authoritative statement on slavery or physical punishment was found. Mencius 4A.18 (Tier 1) hands the correction of sons to other teachers, so that a father's anger does not harm the bond; neither text forbids beating. Classical authorities also limit harm in the household, without rejecting the way it is organised. The Classic of Filial Piety (Tier 2) bars slighting even servants. Zhu Xi's commentary, the examination standard from 1313, makes dignity and kindness towards servants a duty while accepting their service. The Zeng Shen story (outside the corpus, authenticity disputed) tells a son to flee a stick that could kill but to bear the small rod. On slavery, Han Confucian voices cited the line that human beings are the most precious, and their measures limited slavery: Dong Zhongshu's proposal, Wang Mang's ban on sale (lifted again after three years) and Guangwu's edicts against killing and branding slaves. ### 7. Taoism

**Summary.** The counted texts (the Tao Te Ching and the Zhuangzi) say nothing directly about slavery, servants, wives, or the discipline of children or pupils; their teaching on power is addressed to rulers. They say that the sage-ruler does not harm the people and, in Legge's reading, treats them as his children; they blame rulers whose taxes starve the people; and they use a cruel horse tamer as the image of coercive government. They also accept state punishment while warning rulers against misusing it, but texts on courts and rulers' punishments are context only (rule 5). So, under the labelling rules, the counted texts teach L1 through a wider rule addressed to rulers (a borderline case) and are silent on L2 and L3. Household teaching appears only in Tier 2, chiefly the Treatise on Response and Retribution (Ganying Pian), which is not a counted text: it lists cruelty to those beneath you and misconduct towards wife and children as wicked deeds, and condemns forcing free people into servile status, while assuming that a servile class exists and that a wife should be yielding. Philosophical readers generally accept only the Tao Te Ching and the Zhuangzi (often also the Liezi); the religious branches (Celestial Masters or Zhengyi, Lingbao, Quanzhen) also teach through precept texts and, for Quanzhen, monastic codes, which may include physical penalties for monastics (unconfirmed).

**How the tradition reconciles these texts.** No counted text was found that permits what any of the three wordings forbids, so there is no contradicting counted text for the tradition to reconcile. The counted texts do carry their own limits on punishment, but only on the state's: Tao Te Ching 74 (a primary text, verified) and Zhuangzi 6 (an Inner Chapter, primary, unverified) accept state punishment and temper it. The first warns rulers against killing in the place of the one who presides over killing, and the second asks for leniency in putting people to death; both limit punishment rather than suspend or repudiate it, and both concern the state, not the household, so they are reported as context only (rule 5). The classical commentaries that corpus.md names for reconciliation (Heshang Gong and Wang Bi on the Tao Te Ching, Guo Xiang on the Zhuangzi) were not checked, so no traditional reading is recorded of whether the sage-ruler's duty not to harm the people reaches the household; that open question, and the fact that the rule is addressed to rulers, are why the L1 result is borderline. No Taoist authority was found commenting on servile status; the Ganying Pian (Tier 2, a classical morality book, not counted) itself limits slavery by condemning the enslavement of free people while leaving existing servile status in place. No statement by a modern Taoist body on slavery or physical punishment was searched for, and no authority was found that reconciles the possible Quanzhen penalties.

**Result for each wording.**
- L1 (no cruelty): **Not contradicted, taught (borderline)**. Tao Te Ching 60, 49 and 75 and Zhuangzi 9 (TA-1 to TA-4, counted and verified) teach that those in power must not harm, starve or overburden the people, a wider duty that counts for L1 under rule 7 but is addressed to rulers and reaches households only by extension, which is why the label is borderline. No counted text permits cruelty: Tao Te Ching 74 (TA-5) is context only (rule 5), and the Ganying Pian's household lines (TA-8, TA-9) agree but are Tier 2 and not counted.
- L2 (no one owned): **Not contradicted, silent**. No counted text accepts or condemns holding or trading people (the lines on producing without possessing, 生而不有, in Tao Te Ching 2, 10 and 51 are Implied at most and unverified), and silence does not teach the wording. The clearest text found, the Ganying Pian's condemnation of forcing the free into servile status (TA-11), is Tier 2 and not counted, and even if it counted it would teach L2 only in part.
- L3 (no hitting): **Not contradicted, silent**. No counted text addresses physical punishment of children, spouses, servants or pupils: Zhuangzi 9 and 4 (TA-4, TA-7) bear on it only through images (rule 4), and Tao Te Ching 74 and Zhuangzi 6 (TA-5, TA-6) concern state punishment (rule 5). The possible Quanzhen monastic penalties (TA-14) are unverified and outside the corpus as written, so they do not decide the label (rule 1).

**Gaps.** Beyond the verified Tier 1 lines and five Ganying Pian lines, the household and servant evidence is unverified: the Hundred and Eighty Precepts and the Lingbao Ten Precepts (the most likely home of religious Taoist servant ethics) were not seen, the Liezi story, the Quanzhen penalties and the Zhuangzi 4 and 6 passages need checking (for Zhuangzi 6, also whether editors treat the passage as a later insertion), and Legge's English for the Ganying Pian lines was not checked. Tao Te Ching 60 (TA-1) has been confirmed only on sites not on the project's list and should be re-checked on ctext.org. Tao Te Ching 81 (天之道，利而不害: roughly, the Way of Heaven benefits and does not harm) is an unverified lead: if confirmed, it would give L1 a wider Tier 1 rule that is not limited to rulers. Zhuangzi 8 mentions in passing Zang and Gu, two servant boys who lose their sheep, without approving or condemning their status (Implied at most, rule 4); no verified reference is recorded for it here. The classical commentaries, modern Taoist bodies, the Ganying Pian line on harming children (損子) and the Taiping Jing (outside the corpus; reportedly condemns killing baby girls and discusses slaves) were not searched.

### 8. Stoicism

**Summary.** The counted texts (Seneca's letters and moral essays, Epictetus and Marcus Aurelius) accept slavery as a legal institution and regulate it. Masters must not starve, torment or humiliate slaves. They should live with those below them as they would want those above them to live with them, and should ask what justice allows rather than what the law permits. The texts say legal slavery touches only the body, Seneca praises ruling slaves with moderation, and no counted text calls for slaves to be freed. Physical punishment is limited, not forbidden: Seneca names occasional blows as part of good parents' correction and treats beating a slave as a proper punishment when it is done calmly, while he condemns lethal beating and constant beating for small faults. In *De Ira* 3.32 he also treats the lash and the breaking of a slave's legs as within a master's power, faulting only haste and anger. He calls drowning weak or deformed newborns an act of reason; as in Article I, newborns are left to candidate C02. On pupils, one unverified passage prefers correction by advice and shame to torment. On spouses, no counted text was found; only Musonius Rufus (Tier 2, unverified) teaches one standard for husband and wife and a marriage of mutual care. Stoicism has no branches and no living authority, so the differences are between ancient teachers, most sharply between Seneca and Musonius on whether every newborn must be raised.

**How the tradition reconciles these texts.** Stoicism has no later council, office or living body that can settle a conflict between its texts, and modern Stoic writers and groups are not authorities under corpus.md. Its own account of slavery is in a primary text, Seneca, *De Beneficiis* 3.20 (Tier 1), backed by the early Stoic doctrine reported in Diogenes Laertius 7.121 to 7.122 (Tier 2): legal slavery touches only the body, and true slavery is a condition of the soul. This explains why the institution was accepted and grounds limits on how slaves are treated, but it does not remove ownership, and *De Clementia* 1.18 praises a master who rules slaves with moderation rather than asking him to give them up; Diogenes Laertius 7.122 may also call an owner's lordship bad, but it is Tier 2 and its meaning is debated. Chrysippus' rule that a slave is a hired worker for life (reported in *De Beneficiis* 3.22.1; applied by Cicero, *De Officiis* 1.41, Tier 2 and flagged) limits treatment by requiring that slaves be given their due, and does not reject the institution. For physical punishment, the limits come from Seneca's own primary texts: do not punish in anger, wait, prefer words, keep it in proportion and do not beat constantly for small faults (*De Ira* 3.12 and 3.32; *De Clementia* 1.16; Letter 47.19). These limit hitting and do not forbid it. Nor do they remove the breaking of a slave's legs in *De Ira* 3.32: the passage asks only that the master wait until anger has passed, and no text in this section limits it further. On newborns, the only opposing Stoic voice found is Musonius Rufus, Lecture 15 (Tier 2, unverified), and no authority decides between him and Seneca; this annex, like Article I, leaves newborns to candidate C02.

**Result for each wording.**
- L1 (no cruelty): **Contradicted**. *De Ira* 3.32.1 to 3.32.2 treats the lash and the breaking of a slave's legs as within a master's power and faults only haste and anger (rule 2); breaking legs is harm beyond moderate correction, so the label holds however the lash alone is read (rule 9). Counted texts teach L1 for slaves and the household (Letter 47.2 to 47.5 and 47.11; *De Clementia* 1.18.1 to 1.18.3; *Meditations* 5.31) but do not remove that passage, and *De Ira* 1.15.2 on newborns is left to candidate C02, as in Article I.
- L2 (no one owned): **Contradicted**. *De Clementia* 1.18.1 praises ruling slaves with moderation, approving a master's rule and faulting only excess (rule 2), and *De Beneficiis* 3.20.1 to 3.20.2 teaches that a slave's body is subject and assigned to a master. Letter 47.17 to 47.18 only presupposes ownership (rule 4), and support for L2 is Implied only (*Discourses* 1.13; Diogenes Laertius 7.122, Tier 2).
- L3 (no hitting): **Contradicted**. *De Clementia* 1.14.1 holds up parents who sometimes correct their children with blows as good parents, which approves the practice itself (rule 4 does not apply, and the ruler it addresses is context only under rule 5), and *De Ira* 3.12 and 3.32 treat beating a slave as a proper punishment, faulting only anger and haste (rule 2). Letter 47.19 and *De Clementia* 1.16 limit hitting but do not forbid it.

**Gaps.** No Stoic text was found on striking a spouse, either way; Hierocles' household fragments (Tier 2), the Greek of *Meditations* 5.31 (whether it names a wife) and duties of care to children (for example *Discourses* 1.11) were not searched. The second check could not display *De Ira* 3.12 or 3.32 and did not reconfirm the *De Clementia* passages in ST-9, so their status rests on the earlier search; the chapter of *De Ira* 1.15.2 was not confirmed. Whether the lash alone counts as moderate correction under rule 9 is not settled here; the L1 label does not depend on it. ### 9. Christianity

**Summary.** The Bible shared by all branches contains laws that let foreign slaves be bought and kept as inherited property, leave a master unpunished if a slave he beats survives a day or two, and commend the rod for children. The New Testament tells slaves, including those of Christian masters, to obey and honour their masters without asking for their release, and a parable uses a master's beating of his servants as an image of judgement; these New Testament texts presuppose slaveholding but do not say that it is right. The same scriptures also limit household power: an escaped slave must not be handed back, masters must give up threats and give slaves what is just and fair, fathers must not provoke their children, husbands must love their wives as their own bodies, and no counted text permits striking a wife, while pupils are addressed only through the rule that a church overseer must not be a striker. Catholics and Orthodox also count Sirach, which prescribes torments and fetters for a disobedient servant and lists "much correction of children" and making an evil servant's side bleed among things not to be ashamed of (and, in a passage not verified here, frequent beating of sons); Protestants do not count it. The law of the rebellious son (a court procedure) and the killing and capture of people in war are reported as context only; as in section 2, keeping war captives afterwards counts against L2, and the captive-woman law (Deuteronomy 21:10 to 14), which does not mention her consent, is a hard case for L1 (rule 6). The main Catholic and Reformed authorities hold that the Old Testament judicial laws no longer bind as such (Aquinas, verified; the Westminster Confession 19.4, apart from their "general equity", not verified here), and the Catholic Catechism forbids enslavement outright, but the main classical authorities (Augustine, Aquinas) accept moderate physical correction of children and slaves.

**How the tradition reconciles these texts.** The main reconciling teaching on the Old Testament laws is that their judicial precepts, including the slave and household laws of Exodus 21 and Leviticus 25, no longer bind as such, and the two main authorities put it differently. Aquinas (a classical Catholic authority, verified) holds that they were annulled by the coming of Christ, though they are not deadly to observe; the Westminster Confession 19.4 (a Reformed Protestant confession, UNVERIFIED here) holds that they expired with the state of Israel and bind only so far as their "general equity" requires. Either way this ends their binding force without condemning them, and it does not reach the wisdom books: Proverbs for every branch, and Sirach for Catholics and Orthodox, which is limited only by its own words ("be not excessive", treat a servant "as thyself"). Within the shared Tier 1 texts, Deuteronomy 23:15 to 16 already limits a master's hold, since an escaped slave is not handed back; Rashi's narrower reading of Exodus 21:21 (section 2) is a Jewish authority and is not part of the Christian corpus. For the New Testament, the classical authorities Augustine and Aquinas (Tier 2, verified) read the limits on masters and fathers as bans on excess and threats, not on moderate correction, while binding the head of a household to rule for the good of those under him, and commentators read the stripes in the parable of Luke 12:47 to 48 as a figure of divine judgement proportioned to knowledge (not checked in a Tier 2 source). On owning people, outright repudiation comes from Tier 2 and later authorities and differs by branch: Gregory of Nyssa (a Father, verified through scholarship only, whom scholars describe as alone among the Fathers in this) condemns slaveholding as such; Chrysostom limits and reforms it (one or two slaves at most, and purchased slaves to be trained and freed) without repudiating ownership, and condemns wife-beating; Augustine explains slavery as a result of sin without rejecting it; for Catholics, Catechism 2414 (verified) forbids enslavement and the trade in people, and Vatican II and later popes (outside the corpus as listed) call slavery an infamy. No Protestant or Orthodox doctrinal text in the corpus repudiating slaveholding was verified, and no authority of any rank was found that forbids the physical correction of children or servants. None of these reconciling authorities is a counted text, so none of them changes a label (rule 1).

### 10. Islam

**Summary.** The Qur'an and the sound (*ṣaḥīḥ*) hadith treat slavery as lawful. A man may have sexual relations with women his right hand possesses, including married women taken captive, and the texts do not mention the women's consent. The Prophet himself sold a slave. Other counted texts command kindness to slaves, encourage freeing them, forbid selling a free person and tell Muslims to release captives of war as a favour or for ransom (47:4), but none forbids holding a slave. Those with power over others are told not to torment people and not to insult or overburden servants. They must feed and clothe slaves as they feed and clothe themselves, not strike the face, free a slave they have slapped or beaten without cause, and pay workers. Qur'an 4:34 allows a husband, as the last of three steps, to strike a wife whose ill conduct he fears. The verse itself states no limit, and counted hadith limit the strike to one that is not severe. Two hadith graded Hasan Sahih by al-Albani, which count for Sunni Islam under the decision of 13 October 2026, forbid striking a wife's face (Abū Dāwūd 2142) and tell parents to strike children of ten who neglect prayer (Abū Dāwūd 495). The Prophet is reported never to have struck a woman or a servant, to have taught without striking, and to have said that men who beat their wives are not the best, but no counted text withdraws the permission. Sunni and Twelver Shia Muslims share the Qur'anic texts. All the verified hadith here are Sunni (Shia collections were not checked), and the small Qur'an-only minority accepts the Qur'anic texts alone.

**How the tradition reconciles these texts.** On striking wives, the first limits come from the primary texts themselves. Qur'an 4:34 ends the steps once the wife obeys, though the verse itself states no limit on the strike. The Farewell Sermon hadith (Muslim 1218a; Ibn Mājah 1851, whose grade was not reconfirmed) restrict the strike to one that is not severe, and Abū Dāwūd 2142 forbids striking the face. The Prophet's example (Muslim 2328a), his saying that men who beat their wives are not the best (Abū Dāwūd 2146) and Bukhārī's chapter disapproving of beating women (5204) express a clear preference for not striking. These texts limit and discourage the permission but do not suspend it. The classical commentator Ibn Kathīr, a Sunni authority used for reconciliation, adds that the strike must not be violent and must not touch the face (his wording was matched only in a secondary reproduction). The gloss attributed to al-Ṭabarī that would make the strike symbolic (a tooth-stick, reported at Jāmiʿ al-Bayān 8:314) is unverified, and modern fatwa bodies and reformist readings were not checked. On slavery, the primary texts limit abuse (no beating without an offence, no slapping or striking the face, no forced prostitution, no selling a free person) and encourage freeing slaves. Qur'an 24:33 tells owners to grant a self-purchase contract to slaves who ask for one (verified), and the verses that praise freeing a slave or make it an expiation (IS-5) are unverified. Qur'an 47:4 (verified) tells Muslims to release captives of war as a favour or for ransom; commentators differ on whether it limits the ruler's options or was abrogated (not verified here). None of these texts forbids holding a slave, and classical law treated ownership as lawful. The repudiation of slavery found here comes from modern sources. The 2014 open letter of more than 120 scholars declares slavery abolished by universal consensus and forbids its reintroduction. It rests on a claim of consensus (*ijmāʿ*), a recognised source in Islamic legal theory, rather than on a Qur'anic or *ṣaḥīḥ* text forbidding ownership. Sunni and Shia Muslim representatives are also reported to have signed the 2014 Joint Declaration of Religious Leaders against Modern Slavery (names not confirmed; UNVERIFIED: [Anglican News](https://anglicannews.org/news/2014/12/archbishop-of-canterbury-joins-world-faith-leaders-in-pledge-to-end-slavery.aspx)). Apart from that modern consensus, no reconciling authority was found for the permission of sexual relations with captive and slave women.

**Result for each wording.**
- L1 (no cruelty): **Unclear**. The counted texts teach it explicitly (Muslim 2613, Bukhārī 30, Qur'an 4:19), and Muslim 1657b, 1658d and 1659c and Qur'an 4:36 limit harm to slaves, but Qur'an 23:5 to 6 and 4:24, with Muslim 1456 and Bukhārī 4138, permit sexual relations with captive and slave women without stating their consent, a hard case that the L1 wording does not plainly cover (rule 6). Qur'an 4:34 permits a strike, which the hadith (Muslim 1218a) limit to one that is not severe, so it is tested under L3 (rule 9); no counted text was found that leaves a severe injury to a dependant unpunished in the way Exodus 21:20 to 21 does.
- L2 (no one owned): **Contradicted**. Qur'an 23:5 to 6 and 4:24 (Tier 1, all branches) do more than presuppose slavery (rule 4): they say that men are not blamed for relations with women their right hands possess, and the counted hadith (Muslim 1456, Bukhārī 4138, and Bukhārī 2141, where the Prophet sells a slave) treat slaves as lawfully held and traded. No counted text forbids holding a slave, and the repudiation of slavery rests on a claim of consensus (IS-6), not on a counted text.
- L3 (no hitting): **Contradicted**. Qur'an 4:34 (Tier 1, all branches) permits a husband to strike his wife as the last of three steps, and Muslim 1218a repeats the permission with the limit that the strike not be severe; the other limits and the Prophet's example (IS-7, IS-11 to IS-15) restrict the strike, but no counted text withdraws it. For Sunni Islam, Abū Dāwūd 495 (now counted) also commands striking children of ten over prayer; no counted text was found permitting the striking of pupils.

**Gaps.** Several texts are unverified: al-Ṭabarī's glosses on 4:34, including the tooth-stick gloss reported at Jāmiʿ al-Bayān 8:314; the Qur'anic verses on freeing slaves (IS-5); the commentators' views on whether 47:4 limits the ruler's options or was abrogated; the names of the Muslim signatories of the 2014 Joint Declaration; and further texts left out of the table (Qur'an 2:231, 4:3, 6:151, 17:31, 70:29 to 30; Bukhārī 6038 and 893; Nahj al-Balāgha Letter 53; Abū Dāwūd 5156, the Prophet's last words, telling Muslims to fear God regarding those whom their right hands possess; Qur'an 38:44 with the commentary on Job's oath; and the reported occasion of revelation of 4:34, the case of Habiba bint Zayd in al-Wāḥidī, reports that Shia exegetes such as al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī find problematic). Ibn Kathīr's wording on 4:34 was matched only in a secondary reproduction, and the second check could not reconfirm Darussalam's grade for Ibn Mājah 1851. Several VERIFIED rows rest on the researcher's search without the verifier's re-check. These were not searched: Shia hadith (al-Kāfī), classical rulings on a captive's consent and on limits to striking children, modern fatwa bodies on striking wives and children, and the Cairo Declaration (1990), Article 11. Under the decision of 13 October 2026, hadith from the other Sunni collections graded sound or good (IS-9, IS-11, IS-12, IS-17) count for Sunni Islam; the L3 result rests on Qur'an 4:34 in any case.

### 11. Sikhism

**Summary.** For Sikhism, only the Guru Granth Sahib counts, and no passage found in it permits owning people, hitting dependants or treating them cruelly. It uses slavery mainly as a picture of devotion to God, and it grieves over women led into captivity in Babur's invasion, but it never states that no one may be held as property. It gives no rule addressed to masters of servants or workers, or to husbands, parents or teachers. Its support comes through wider teachings: no harsh word to anyone and no heart broken (verified), no bullying of the poor and weak (verified only on a secondary site), no contempt for women, and no religious merit in widow-burning. Under the labelling rules these wider teachings carry L1, but they cannot carry L2 or L3 on their own (rule 7), and no counted text addresses owning people or hitting directly, so L2 and L3 are silent. On children and pupils, only two texts were found, and neither is verified as a reference: a father who scolds his child, then embraces him and forgives his past faults (the content is partly confirmed, but not the ang), and the story of Prahlad, whose father threatens him in a rage when his teachers report him and is destroyed as a tyrant. Neither is a rule about hitting. All Sikh groups accept the Guru Granth Sahib, so these findings hold across branches. The Sikh Rehat Maryada gives explicit household rules (do not kill daughters, take no money for a child's marriage), but it is Tier 2 and is the mainstream Khalsa code, which not every Sikh group may accept in full (general knowledge, not verified), so it is reported beside the labels and decides nothing (rule 1).

**How the tradition reconciles these texts.** No counted text was found that permits, commands or approves what any of the three wordings forbids, so the tradition has no hard text here to limit, suspend or repudiate. Its reconciling work is about interpretation. Within the tradition, the scripture's slave imagery (SI-6, SI-7) is understood as describing the devotee's bond with God, not as permission for one person to own another. Sikh readers take the Babarvani (SI-5) as a protest against tyranny, not as resigned acceptance (no named authority was cited for this reading). Commentary (for example gurbani.org, a secondary source) reads ang 787 (SI-9) as a critique of widow-burning and says that Guru Amar Das campaigned against it. The Sikh Rehat Maryada, the code of conduct approved by the SGPC in 1945 and published in 1950, is a modern body's authority. It turns the scripture's wider teaching into explicit rules for the household (SI-13, SI-14). It is the official code for mainstream Khalsa Sikhs, but it is Tier 2 and decides nothing on its own (rule 1). No statement by the Akal Takht or the SGPC on slavery, domestic violence or the physical punishment of children was found.

### 12. Bahá'í Faith

**Summary.** Bahá'u'lláh's Kitáb-i-Aqdas forbids trading in slaves and buying another person, and the same paragraph closes by saying that no one should exalt himself above another, since all are bondslaves before God, which bears on holding a person as well as trading one. His answer on household service treats a maid as a free, paid worker who may marry when she chooses. Shoghi Effendi's authorised summary reads the Aqdas as prohibiting slavery itself. Tier 1 also forbids striking another person, and any act that saddens hearts, with no exception for the household. It also tells people not to wish for others what they would not wish for themselves. 'Abdu'l-Bahá, whose writings the tradition treats as near-primary, says it is not permissible to strike or verbally abuse a child. A verified letter of 24 January 1993 on behalf of the Universal House of Justice says a husband must never beat his wife or treat her cruelly; the same letter accepts physical discipline of children as part of their education when it is given gently, patiently and with loving care, and condemns child abuse. Letters on behalf of Shoghi Effendi call discipline of some sort, which may be physical, moral or intellectual, indispensable (verified), and read 'Abdu'l-Bahá's ban as aimed at beating, leaving the matter to parents' discretion (not verified). These letters are Tier 2 and do not count on their own, but they are the tradition's authoritative reading, so the result for L3 is unclear rather than taught. Every Bahá'í group accepts the writings of Bahá'u'lláh and 'Abdu'l-Bahá. Small groups that reject the present House of Justice would not be bound by its letters, and their positions were not researched. No text on pupils was found, and the treatment of hired workers was not searched.

**How the tradition reconciles these texts.** The tradition reads its texts through a fixed line of authority. Bahá'u'lláh's writings are scripture. 'Abdu'l-Bahá is the authorised interpreter, and his writings are treated as near-primary. Shoghi Effendi is the next authorised interpreter, and the Universal House of Justice is the elected body that explains and legislates where the texts leave matters open. On slavery, ¶72 itself forbids the trade in people and their purchase, and closes by setting no one above another (BA-1); Shoghi Effendi's authorised summary (BA-4, verified) reads it as a ban on slavery itself. The House of Justice's Synopsis (BA-5, unverified) is reported to name only "slave trading", and none of these texts allows anyone to be held. On children, the House of Justice's letter of 24 January 1993 (BA-12, verified, binding on the mainstream branch) accepts physical discipline of children as part of their education when it is given gently, patiently and with loving care, far from angry or violent beating, and condemns child abuse. A verified 1939 letter written on behalf of Shoghi Effendi (BA-10) calls discipline of some sort, physical, moral or intellectual, indispensable, without defining "physical". A further letter on his behalf (BA-11, unverified) says 'Abdu'l-Bahá's ban (BA-9) does not necessarily cover every form of corporal punishment and leaves the matter, for now, to parents' wise discretion. These letters limit how far the counted ban reaches; they do not repudiate it, and they keep the ban on beating and abuse. On spouses, the same 1993 letter (BA-13) turns the general ban on striking (¶148) into an explicit rule against wife-beating. Small groups that reject the present House would not be bound by its letters on either point: for them the rule on spouses would rest on ¶148 and on the equality of women and men. Because letters on behalf of Shoghi Effendi carry the authority of the Guardian, the narrowing in BA-11, if confirmed, would bind every group that accepts him, not only the mainstream.

### 13. Secular humanism

**Summary.** Humanism has no scripture. Its counted texts are short consensus declarations by modern humanist bodies, written after slavery had been abolished in law in most countries, and none of them names slavery, servants, spouses, or the physical punishment of children or pupils. Instead they set out wider rules that cover every person: "helping and not harming" and a duty of care to all of humanity, the inherent worth and dignity of each person, a world free of cruelty, and each individual's right to the greatest possible freedom compatible with the rights of others (Declaration of Modern Humanism 2022; Humanist Manifesto III; Amsterdam Declarations 2002 and 1952). The wider duty not to harm teaches L1. The freedom and autonomy clauses of the three Amsterdam Declarations teach L2 by principle, but only on the borderline, because slavery is not named; the UN human rights instruments that ban slavery by name are outside the corpus and are context only. No counted text bears on L3 except by implication. Explicit opposition to slavery and to the corporal punishment of children appears only in Humanists International policy and UN statements (outside the corpus) and in Humanists UK material (Tier 2). Nothing specific was found on spouses or on servants and workers in any tier. **How the tradition reconciles these texts.** No humanist text found in any tier permits slavery, cruelty to dependants or physical punishment, so on these points there is nothing for the tradition to reconcile. Humanism has no scripture and no classical commentary. Its texts are revisable consensus statements by modern bodies, and later versions replace earlier ones: the 2022 Declaration replaced the 2002 text, and the American Humanist Association has declared Manifestos I and II historic and superseded by Manifesto III. The principle that decides L2 runs through each replacement: the 1952, 2002 and 2022 Declarations each state the individual's right to the greatest possible freedom (in 1952, freedom of development) compatible with the rights of others (HU-14, HU-13, HU-5). The bodies that apply the declarations' general principles to named practices are modern ones: the Humanists International General Assembly (the 2008 policy against corporal punishment of children, still listed as pending review, and the 2009 UN statement against slavery) and Humanists UK (campaign releases and UN statements). These do not limit, suspend or repudiate any counted text; they apply the declarations' general principles explicitly to particular practices. Under sources/corpus.md they are outside the corpus or Tier 2, so they may explain but cannot decide a label on their own (rule 1). The UN human rights instruments that the declarations uphold (UDHR Article 4, ICCPR Article 8) ban slavery by name, but they too are outside the corpus and are context only. The 1996 Minimum Statement is the one text every member organisation of Humanists International must accept, which makes it the broadest common text in the tradition, but it speaks only in general terms.

**Result for each wording.**
- L1 (no cruelty): **Not contradicted, taught**. The 2022 Declaration's "helping and not harming" (HU-1), Manifesto III's inherent worth and dignity of each person and hope for a world free of cruelty (HU-2), and the 2002 Declaration's duty of care to all of humanity (HU-13) are Clear teaching of a wider duty not to harm that reaches everyone, which counts for L1 (rule 7); no counted text names households, servants or people in one's power, and none permits cruelty to them.
- L2 (no one owned): **Not contradicted, taught (by principle, and borderline)**. The freedom and autonomy clauses of the 2022, 2002 and 1952 Declarations (HU-5, HU-13, HU-14) give every person the greatest possible freedom compatible with the rights of others, which plainly excludes being owned, so they are rated Clear teaching, while the Sikh text on the common origin of all people (ang 1349) is only Implied. It is borderline because no counted text names slavery: if the lead requires the practice to be named, these texts fall to Implied and no counted text teaches L2, since the UN instruments are context only and the explicit condemnations (HU-9, HU-10) are not counted.
- L3 (no hitting): **Not contradicted, silent**. No counted text permits or forbids physical punishment of children, spouses, servants or pupils: Manifesto III's hope that differences be resolved "without resorting to violence" (HU-2) and Manifesto II's extension of democracy to the family and the school (HU-4) are Implied only, and the wider duty not to harm does not count for L3 (rule 7). The explicit positions, the Humanists International 2008 policy (HU-11, outside the corpus) and Humanists UK (HU-12, Tier 2), are not counted and cover only children and, through teachers, pupils; nothing was found in any tier on spouses or servants.

**Gaps.** The 1980 Secular Humanist Declaration (Tier 1 for the Council for Secular Humanism branch) and Humanist Manifesto I (1933) were not read in full for these topics, and the second independent check could not confirm either the 1980 Declaration's section on moral education (HU-8) or the Manifesto II passage on extending democracy (HU-4). One further item remains UNVERIFIED and is left out of the table: Manifesto II's passages on abused children and on equal rights for women and men, recalled from memory. Humanist Manifesto 2000 was searched but its text was not returned, Kurtz's writings and any Humanists International resolutions on violence against women were not searched, the current standing of the 2008 policy was not checked, and the finding that no humanist text permits slavery or hitting rests on 22 searches, so it is a negative result from limited searching, not proof of absence. The L2 result rests on reading general freedom and autonomy clauses as plainly excluding ownership; whether that reading meets the counting standard is the project lead's decision.

## Gaps that apply to every section

- **How the checks were made.** Every citation was checked through web search results, not by reading the source page in full, because the reference sites could not be opened from the working environment. Some rows rest on a single search result or on a scholarly description of a text rather than the text itself; the Verified column says so.
- **Modern authoritative bodies** were searched unevenly. For several traditions (for example Judaism, Orthodox and Protestant Christianity, Zoroastrianism and Sikhism) no statement by a present-day authoritative body on slavery, domestic violence or the physical punishment of children was checked. Their absence here is a gap, not evidence that none exists.
- **Hard texts that need reading in the original with the tradition's own commentators** before any verdict is final: Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad 6.4.7 with Śaṅkara; Ṛg Veda 8.56.3 and the standing of the Vālakhilya hymns; Exodus 21:20 to 21 with the Mekhilta; Babylonian Talmud Gittin 38b; the Vinaya ordination rules and Jātaka 252 in other Buddhist canons; Tattvārtha Sūtra 7.24 (Śvetāmbara) = 7.29 (Digambara) on the meaning of *dāsa*; Seneca, *De Ira* 3.32; Qur'an 4:34 and 23:5 to 6 with the classical commentators.
- **Branches searched lightly or not at all:** Karaite Judaism; Digambara texts other than the Tattvārtha Sūtra; the Chinese and Tibetan Vinayas; Shia hadith collections; Sikh codes other than the Sikh Rehat Maryada; reformist Zoroastrian statements on slavery.
