# Article I: Do Not Harm Others in Ways You Would Not Accept

> **Status: draft, not yet part of the Code (version 0.4).** Two things keep it out for now. First, one tradition, Zoroastrianism, teaches this only in books that some of its own followers do not accept. Under the project's rules that is not enough, and choosing the wording below will not change this. Second, the traditions' books disagree about how people in your power may be treated. Some of these books accept owning people or hitting those in your care, and texts in four traditions accept treatment that this article would count as cruel. The person leading the project has still to choose how the article should word this. The details are under "Decisions" at the end and in the separate [page on households and slavery](article-01-annex-households.md).

**The Article**

Do not harm anyone in a way you would not want to be harmed yourself.

**Why**

Everyone knows what it is like to be hurt, cheated, frightened or made to feel small. This article asks you to use that knowledge as a test: if you would not want to be harmed in a certain way, do not harm someone else in that way. Most traditions teach this by asking you to put yourself in the other person's place; it is often called the Golden Rule. Others simply teach that you should harm no one, which includes this and more. The test matters most where one person has power over another, at home or at work, because that is where it is easiest to forget.

**The floor**

What this requires: In your own dealings with other people, do not harm them in ways you would not want to be harmed. Pain, fear, loss and being made to feel small hurt everyone, so "I wouldn't mind" is no excuse. Use the same standard for everyone, whoever they are, and make no exception for yourself. A simple way to keep it: imagine being on the receiving end.

This applies at home and at work too: to your family and household, to anyone who works for you, and to anyone who depends on you or is under your authority. Being in charge of a home or a workplace does not make you one of the "lawful public authorities" left aside below. But how far the rule above reaches in homes and workplaces is not yet decided, so the words about pain and fear above do not yet settle it. Every wording being considered would at least ask you not to be cruel to anyone in your power, though texts in four traditions do not agree even with this (see section E). Whether it also rules out owning people, or hitting those in your care, is still to be decided.

**Households and slavery: wording still to be decided.** The person leading the project has decided that this article covers slavery and how people in charge of a home treat those in it. The exact words will be chosen after reading the evidence. In homes, and wherever one person has power over another, the article could rule out up to three different things. The traditions' books treat each one differently:
- **No cruelty:** do not injure, torment, starve, overwork or humiliate anyone in your household or in your power.
- **No one owned:** no one may be held or traded as property.
- **No hitting:** no physical punishment of children, spouses, servants or pupils.

The evidence for each is in the [annex on households and slavery](article-01-annex-households.md) and is summarised in section E of the counter-evidence below.

What this does not require, or does not settle (each is left to each tradition and to conscience):
- **Doing good for everyone.** It does not require you to do for others everything you would want for yourself. Many traditions ask for that and more; how much active help to give is a separate question.
- **Courts, rulers, war and self-defence.** It does not settle what courts, governments and other lawful public authorities may do, such as punishing crime or going to war. Nor does it settle when a person may use force to defend themselves or others. Each tradition answers these in its own way. Being in charge of a home or a workplace is not covered by this exception (see above).
- **The beginning and end of life, and animals.** It does not settle who counts at the beginning and end of life, or how animals should be treated. These are separate questions, in the [list of principles still to be tested](../../candidates.md).
- **Whom you keep company with.** It does not require you to keep company with everyone. But it still applies to how you treat the people you do not keep company with.
- **What you feel.** It does not tell anyone what to feel. Many traditions also ask for love or goodwill towards all, and remain free to.
- **The fuller rule.** "Do not treat anyone in *any* way you would not want to be treated" is not yet in the Code. It is an [open question](../../register/q-001-golden-rule.md).

**How the evidence is counted**

1. A passage counts if it teaches the test itself for harm: put yourself in the other person's place, or take your own dislike of harm as the measure for others.
2. A passage also counts if it teaches a wider duty not to harm others, because whoever keeps that duty keeps this floor. Such rows are marked "wider rule". The wider duty must reach everyone, not only some people. (Confirmed by the project lead on 2026-10-13, decision D3; now a general rule in `METHOD.md`, section 3.)
3. Passages in the positive form ("treat others as you would want to be treated") count when they concern the manner of treating people, since treating someone as you would want to be treated rules out harming them in ways you would not accept.
4. Strength is rated against this article's floor. The tier rule and branch rule in [`sources/corpus.md`](../../sources/corpus.md) apply.
5. "VERIFIED (search)" means the reference and its content were confirmed through web search results from the linked page, not by reading the page in full (`METHOD.md`, section 3). Translations are listed in [`sources/translations.md`](../../sources/translations.md).

**Notes on the evidence**

1. *How each tradition meets the floor in personal dealings.* (Households and slavery, which the floor now also covers, are summarised in section E and decision D2.) Hinduism, Judaism, Buddhism, Jainism, Confucianism, Stoicism, Christianity, Islam and the Bahá'í Faith teach the test itself, putting yourself in the other's place. Sikhism and secular humanism meet the floor through a wider rule, "harm no one", which includes it; their primary texts do not state the test itself (for humanism it appears in Tier 2 explanatory material, row 13d). Taoism teaches taking other people's hearts as one's own and not harming them; this is borderline (note 6). For Digambara Jains, whose branch does not accept rows 5a and 5b, the floor is met through a wider rule (row 5c; note 5).
2. *Hinduism.* The Tier 1 evidence is one verse (1a), which describes the highest yogi's way of seeing; its conduct meaning comes from the commentators. The explicit rule is in the Mahabharata (Tier 2), twice. Row 1c's critical-edition verse was confirmed on a verse-level site that is not on the project's list, and a second check could not find the line on the linked Ganguli page; it should be re-checked by direct reading.
3. *Judaism.* Rabbinic tradition largely reads the "stranger" verses (2a, 2b) as about the convert (Mekhilta on Exodus 22:20; Babylonian Talmud Bava Metzia 59b), though Rashi glosses "stranger" more widely as anyone not born in that land. So the claim that the rule reaches everyone rests, for Judaism, mainly on Hillel's answer to a non-Jew (2c), on Ben Azzai's teaching that "This is the book of the generations of Adam" (Genesis 5:1) is a greater principle than Leviticus 19:18, because it covers all human beings (Jerusalem Talmud, Nedarim 9:4), and on Rabbi Akiva's "beloved is every human being, created in the image" (Mishnah Avot 3:14), which goes on to speak of Israel's particular status.
4. *Buddhism.* Rows 4a to 4c are from the Pali Canon (Theravāda). Mahāyāna Buddhists receive the same early discourses in Chinese and Tibetan recensions (parallels not yet checked), and row 4d gives Mahāyāna's own authoritative statement.
5. *Jainism.* Rows 5a and 5b are from the Śvetāmbara canon, which Digambara Jains do not accept. Row 5c, from the Tattvārtha Sūtra accepted by all branches, meets this floor for Digambaras as a wider rule of non-injury. Row 5d states the test itself and was approved by all four main sects, but it is a modern anthology whose source has not been traced.
6. *Taoism.* Neither the Tao Te Ching nor the Zhuangzi states a rule as a formula; Taoism teaches through the example of the sage, who is the model for everyone. Read together, chapters 49 and 60 teach that the sage takes other people's hearts as his own and harms no one, which includes this floor. This is rated Clear teaching but is borderline: it rests on two short chapters about the sage, often the ruler, and on the counting rule in D3. Row 7c states the test itself, but as a later Tier 2 text it only supports.
7. *Positive and negative forms.* Rows 5b, 8a, 9a and 10c are in the positive form. They count on the "manner of treatment" reading (counting rule 3). The same traditions also have negatively worded rows (5a, 9b, 10d).

**Counter-evidence considered**

The passages below seem to contradict or limit the floor. For each, the tradition's own reconciliation is given, with its authority. Passages marked "not yet verified" could not be confirmed in this session and do not count either way until they are.

*A. Punishment, war, and the objection that the rule forbids all punishment.* Read literally, any rule of this kind would forbid every penalty, because no wrongdoer wants to be punished (a well-known objection, made for example by Kant). Every tradition permits or requires some acts that the person on the receiving end would not want: courts and penalties (Romans 13:4; Manusmṛti 7.14 to 22, not yet verified; Kitáb-i-Aqdas ¶62 and ¶45), defensive force (Guru Granth Sahib ang 1105; Qur'an 2:194), and war (the Bhagavad Gita's call to Arjuna; the Zafarnama in the contested Dasam Granth, not yet verified). Each tradition presents the rule as a summary that sits inside a wider body of justice: "the rest is commentary" (Shabbat 31a); "this is the Law and the Prophets" (Matthew 7:12); "this, in brief, is dharma" (Mahabharata 13.114.8). Aquinas holds that public authority may punish for the common good (Summa Theologiae II-II q.64 a.2); the Bahá'í Notes to the Kitáb-i-Aqdas (notes 86 and 87) place penalties with institutions, not individuals; the Jain householder's partial vow allows defensive harm while still counting it as harm to be kept to a minimum (Tattvārtha Sūtra 7.2; the fourfold division of harm into intentional, domestic, occupational and defensive, as set out in modern Jain scholarship such as V. A. Sangave's *Jaina Path of Ahimsa*; its classical source is not yet verified). **Resolution:** the article covers personal conduct and leaves lawful authority, self-defence and war to each tradition ("What this does not settle").

*B. Revenge.* The Book of Rites (Liji, Tangong I; Confucian Tier 2) has Confucius tell a son to be ready to kill his father's murderer on sight; Numbers 35:19 to 21 lets the "avenger of blood" kill a deliberate murderer; Cicero, presenting Stoic ethics, makes the first rule of justice to harm no one "unless provoked by wrong" (De Officiis 1.20). **Resolution within each tradition:** the Gongyang commentary allows vengeance only where the father was not justly executed and warns that revenge for a lawful punishment starts an endless cycle (Gongyang Zhuan, Duke Ding, year 4); Analects 14.34 answers injury with uprightness, not kindness and not spite; Numbers 35:24 to 25 itself puts the case before the congregation to judge and provides cities of refuge, and rabbinic law gives such cases to a court; Leviticus 19:18 forbids taking vengeance and Proverbs 24:29 forbids repaying like for like; Musonius Rufus would never prosecute anyone for a personal injury (Lecture 10), and Marcus Aurelius says the best revenge is not to become like the wrongdoer (Meditations 6.6). The question whether revenge is ever right is a separate candidate (C19).

*C. Violence against enemies, dissenters and those who leave the faith.* Hebrew Bible commands to destroy the seven nations and Amalek (Deuteronomy 20:16 to 17; 1 Samuel 15:3), shared by Judaism and Christianity, and the law against one who entices others to other gods (Deuteronomy 13:7 to 12 in Hebrew numbering, 13:6 to 11 in English); death for leaving Islam (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 6922); the second half of Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 1844a (force against a rival who challenges a pledged ruler); Aquinas on executing obstinate heretics (Summa Theologiae II-II q.11 a.3) and Augustine on compelling the Donatists (Letters 93 and 185); Pahlavi rulings on putting to death those guilty of the gravest sins (Dadestan-i Denig, Question 76; ch. LXXVII in West's translation; reports that this chapter concerns apostates could not be confirmed); a brahmin's licence to strike enemies with spells (Manusmṛti 11.31 to 33) and the Atharvaveda's hostile charms (Tier 1); a Mahāyāna sūtra's teaching that killing those who slander the Dharma carries little weight (Mahāparinirvāṇa Sūtra; not yet verified). **Resolution within each tradition:** rabbinic law holds that the ancient nations can no longer be identified and that peace must be offered before any war (Maimonides, Hilkhot Melakhim 6:1), and called a court that executes once in seven years "destructive" (Mishnah Makkot 1:10, with a recorded dissent); Aquinas holds that the judicial precepts of the Old Law ceased with Christ (Summa Theologiae I-II q.104 a.3), the Reformed confessions say the same of Israel's judicial laws (Westminster Confession 19.4), Augustine asked that the Donatists be corrected, not killed (Letter 100), and the Catholic Church now teaches religious freedom (Dignitatis Humanae 2); Islamic law treats apostasy and rebellion as matters for a court, after a call to repent, never for individuals (the chapter heading of Bukhārī 6922 is "seeking the repentance of apostates"); the Dadestan-i Denig allows such a killing only after three disputations with the person, on the decision of those learned in the religion and at the command of the king; Mīmāṃsā holds that Vedic rites for harming an enemy are described but are not right conduct, because injury is forbidden (not yet verified); East Asian Buddhist readers take the Mahāparinirvāṇa passage as hyperbole about slandering the Dharma, and Theravāda does not accept the text (not yet verified). These passages concern war, courts and religious law, which the article leaves aside (section A). Manusmṛti 11.31 to 33 and the Atharvaveda charms are the hardest cases, because they concern individuals: Hindu tradition ranks the Mahabharata's statement of the rule as the summary of dharma, and Manusmṛti 10.63 itself makes non-injury the first of the duties common to all classes.

*D. Laws that treat insiders and outsiders differently.* Penalties graded by birth group (Manusmṛti 8.270 to 272); damages between Israelite and gentile oxen (Mishnah Bava Kamma 4:3); interest from foreigners (Deuteronomy 23:20 to 21); not rescuing certain people from a pit (Babylonian Talmud Avodah Zarah 26a to b); greetings and right of way for Jews and Christians (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2167); no retaliation against a Muslim for killing a non-Muslim, in the majority classical view (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 6915); the jizya paid "while they are humbled" (Qur'an 9:29); "firm against the disbelievers" (Qur'an 48:29). **Resolution within each tradition:** Hindu tradition ranks the revealed texts above the law books, and the Bhagavad Gita says the wise see the same in a learned brahmin and an outcaste (5.18); the Talmud records an objection to the Bava Kamma rule and its own justification (Bava Kamma 38a), and later authorities confine such distinctions (Menachem Meiri; not yet verified); the Chazon Ish confines the Avodah Zarah ruling to an age long past (not yet verified); in Islam, Qur'an 5:8 forbids letting hatred of a people make you unjust, 60:8 permits kindness and fairness to those who do not fight you, Bukhārī 3166 makes killing a protected non-Muslim a grave sin, the Hanafi school does apply retaliation for killing a protected non-Muslim (al-Marghīnānī, *al-Hidāya*, as reported in modern scholarship), al-Adab al-Mufrad 1102 places the greeting instruction on the eve of a conflict, and al-Nawawī's commentary on Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim limits the "narrow side of the road" so that no one is pushed into a ditch or against a wall. These reconciliations are not all equally strong: several rest on later authorities rather than on the primary texts. None of the texts tells an individual to harm another person outside the setting of law or war, so they are not treated as contradicting this floor, but the wider question of equal treatment in law is not settled here (candidate C06b).

*E. Slavery and authority within the household.* Since decision D2, the texts on slavery and household authority are evidence for or against the floor itself, not only limits on it. They are set out tradition by tradition in the [annex on households and slavery](article-01-annex-households.md), which tests three wordings (no cruelty, no one owned, no hitting) under nine written labelling rules, applied to every tradition alike. In brief:
- **No cruelty** is taught, directly or through a wider duty not to harm, in seven traditions. It is contradicted by a counted text in four: Hinduism (Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad 6.4.7, where, in a rite for begetting a child, a husband is told to hit a wife who refuses him, a reading Śaṅkara's commentary follows); Judaism and Christianity (Exodus 21:20 to 21, which leaves a master unpunished if a slave he has beaten survives a day or two, and in the rabbinic reading even if the slave then dies; for Catholics and Orthodox also Sirach 33 and 42:5); and Stoicism (Seneca, *De Ira* 3.32, which treats the lash and the breaking of a slave's legs as within a master's power, faulting only haste and anger). It is unclear in Islam (counted texts permit sexual relations with captive and slave women without stating consent; the Torah's captive-woman law, Deuteronomy 21:10 to 14, raises the same question for Judaism and Christianity) and in Zoroastrianism (Yasna 33.2 and 47.4; see section G).
- **No one owned** is taught in counted texts by two traditions (the Bahá'í Faith; secular humanism, by principle and on borderline evidence). It is contradicted in five traditions and in one branch of two more; for Hinduism the contradiction rests on one verse in a Vālakhilya hymn of disputed standing. It is unclear for Jainism and silent in three.
- **No hitting** is not clearly taught by any tradition's counted texts (the Bahá'í and Jain texts come nearest). It is contradicted in five traditions and in one branch of a sixth.

Some of these texts have reconciliations that are themselves counted texts (the Mekhilta and the Talmud on Exodus 21:20; the farewell sermon in Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 1218a, which limits the strike in Qur'an 4:34 to one that is not severe), but they limit the texts rather than remove the contradiction. Many later authorities reject slavery outright (for example the Catechism of the Catholic Church 2414, Shoghi Effendi, and a 2014 open letter of Muslim scholars resting on a claim of consensus), but these are not counted texts. No reconciliation of Bṛhadāraṇyaka 6.4.7 or of *De Ira* 3.32 was found. **This is not resolved.** It is decision D2.

*F. The beginning of life.* Seneca gives drowning weak or deformed newborns as an example of reason acting without anger (De Ira 1.15), though his fellow Stoic Musonius Rufus argues against exposing infants (Lecture 15). Humanist Manifesto II affirms a right to abortion, while several other traditions treat the unborn as a person. **Resolution:** Stoicism has no later authority to settle this; it is recorded as a disagreement within the school. Who counts at the beginning and end of life is left to the separate candidate on killing (C02).

*G. Harm or contempt towards the wicked.* In the Gathas, which every Zoroastrian branch accepts, Yasna 33.2 commends whoever harms the follower of the Lie (*drəgvant*) "by word, or with thought (and plan)" and with both hands (Mills, SBE 31; verified in the first check, though the second could not reproduce the wording by search); Yasna 47.4 tells people of little or much means to favour the truthful and set themselves against the follower of the Lie (verified in two modern Zoroastrian translations on zarathushtra.com, a site not on the project's list, as rejection or contempt; the second check could see only the verse's first half); Yasna 46.6 says that whoever is "very good to" a follower of the Lie is one himself (every translation found has this wording; the dispute is over whether it means general kindness or patronage); and in Yasna 43.8 Zarathushtra calls himself a true foe of the follower of the Lie (verified in F. Azargoshasb's translation on zarathushtra.com); Psalm 139:21 to 22 speaks of hating God's enemies (Judaism and Christianity); Kabir's saloks compare the faithless person (*sākat*) unfavourably with a pig (Guru Granth Sahib ang 1372); the Mahāvaṃsa (ch. 25, outside the Buddhist canon) tells a king that the enemies he killed hardly counted as human; a Mahāyāna sūtra (*Skill in Means*) praises a bodhisattva who kills a man about to murder five hundred; Tao Te Ching 36 describes giving in order to take, and 65 describes keeping the people simple. **Resolution within each tradition:** reformist Zoroastrians read the follower of the Lie as a moral choice that anyone may make or leave (resting on Yasna 30.2; not yet verified); a Zoroastrian study reading on avesta.org takes Yasna 33.1 to 2 to mean that the Prophet acts equally towards the followers of truth and untruth and that the righteous should guide the corrupt (Weekly Zoroastrian Scripture Extract 219; verified); and the Zarathushtrian Assembly's translation (A. A. Jafarey's) renders 33.2 as "foils the wrongful" and 47.4 as a statement of what one can do. But modern Zoroastrian translators do not agree (D. J. Irani makes the object of 33.2 "Evil" but keeps rejection of the wicked at 47.4; F. Azargoshasb keeps fighting the wicked at 33.2 and contempt at 47.4). Traditionalists read the Gathas through the Pahlavi books, which state the self-as-measure rule (rows 3a and 3b), though how the Pahlavi commentary (*Zand*), their own key authority here, glosses 33.2 and 47.4 has not been checked. Yasna 31.18, addressed to all hearers, tells them not to listen to the follower of the Lie, who ruins house, village and land, and to resist him "with the weapon": literal and traditionalist readings take this as armed defence, which the article leaves aside (section A), while reformist readers (Dina McIntyre; the avesta.org study series, Weekly Zoroastrian Scripture Extracts 255 and 256) take the weapon as good thinking and truth. Yasna 53.8 to 9 (death and bloodshed, through good rulers, for evildoers who attack the villages) concerns rulers and the defence of the community, which the article leaves aside (section A); Matthew 5:44 commands loving enemies, and Exodus 23:4 to 5 requires helping an enemy's animal (not yet verified); in Sikh teaching *sākat* names a spiritual condition, not a community, and Farid's saloks in the same scripture forbid harsh words to anyone (Sikh commentators not yet verified); Theravāda places the chronicle below the canon, which forbids even inner hatred of bandits (Majjhima Nikāya 21) and says a soldier who dies fighting with the wish to kill goes to a bad rebirth, not to heaven (Saṃyutta Nikāya 42.3); the *Skill in Means* case is a narrow act to protect victims, framed as compassion for both sides, and is rejected by Theravāda; Wang Bi reads chapter 36 as a way of dealing with the violent and oppressive, and chapter 65's "simple" as unspoiled genuineness (not yet verified).

*H. Using your own tastes as the measure.* Zhuangzi ch. 7 (Inner Chapters) tells how two emperors bored seven openings into their host Hundun to repay his kindness, and he died; ch. 18 (Outer Chapters) tells of a marquis who fed a seabird as he would feed himself, and it died. G. B. Shaw's objection that "their tastes may not be the same" is discussed in Humanists UK's explanatory material. **Resolution:** in Zhuangzi's own terms the lesson is to nourish a bird as a bird would be nourished, that is, to attend to the other's nature and not impose on them; that supports restraint, which is the direction of this floor. Both stories end in death, a harm everyone would refuse. The objection falls mainly on the positive form; how far it affects the negative form is part of the open question (Q-001, Part 9).

*I. Taoist criticism of benevolence.* Tao Te Ching 5 says Heaven and Earth, and the sage, are "not benevolent" and treat people "as straw dogs"; chapter 19 says "abandon benevolence, discard righteousness"; Zhuangzi ch. 24 (Miscellaneous Chapters) says "to love the people is the beginning of harming them". **Resolution:** these reject partial, showy or imposed kindness, not care for people. Chapter 19 itself says the people will then return to kindness, and the oldest manuscript (Guodian, c. 300 BCE) reads "abandon artifice and scheming" instead. Chapter 5 is read as impartiality: Wang Bi and Heshang Gong read 芻狗 as "grass and dogs", things Heaven and Earth leave to their own course rather than favouring (as reported in R. Wagner, *A Chinese Reading of the Daodejing*, 2003); and the same book says the sage is good to all (49) and harms no one (60). Wang Bi's reading of chapter 36 and Guo Xiang's of Zhuangzi 7 and 18 are not yet verified, so sections G and H remain partly provisional.

*J. Stoic indifference.* Epictetus (Enchiridion 1 and 5) and Marcus Aurelius (2.1) teach that others cannot truly harm you, which might make "what you would not want" a weak test. **Resolution:** the same passage of Marcus draws the opposite conclusion: we are made to work together, and to act against one another is contrary to nature. Seneca: it is more wretched to harm than to be harmed (Letter 95.52).

*K. Shunning and exclusion.* The Bahá'í Faith requires believers to "shun and avoid entirely" Covenant-breakers ('Abdu'l-Bahá, Will and Testament); the Sikh Rehat Maryada imposes boycott, including in social dealings, on certain schismatic groups; the New Testament tells believers not to receive or greet a false teacher (2 John 10 to 11) and not to eat with certain wrongdoers (1 Corinthians 5:11 to 13); Shayast-ne-Shayast 13.29 lists keeping away from "vile" people. Being shunned is something no one would want. **Resolution within each tradition:** in each case the separation is presented as protecting the community, and decided by its institutions, not by individuals at will; the same Will commands "sincere kindliness unto all the peoples… be they friends or strangers"; the same Rehat Maryada fixes a daily prayer for the good of all (*sarbat da bhala*); Paul adds "count him not as an enemy, but admonish him as a brother" (2 Thessalonians 3:14 to 15) and leaves those outside the community to God's judgement (1 Corinthians 5:12 to 13). Withholding company is not itself harm in this floor's sense, and the article does not require anyone to keep company with everyone; but how far exclusion may go is part of the open question (Q-001, Part 8).

*L. The Báb's earlier laws (Bahá'í Faith).* The Bayán, a Tier 1 text with a flag, laid down severe ordinances, such as destroying books other than those defending the Cause. **Resolution:** Bahá'u'lláh released believers from the ordinance on books (Kitáb-i-Aqdas ¶77) and declared the law of holy war "blotted out from the Book" (Lawḥ-i-Bishárát, first Glad-Tidings), and 'Abdu'l-Bahá states that the Bayán is superseded except where the Aqdas confirms it.

*M. Humanist reluctance to fix rules.* Humanist Manifesto II calls ethics "autonomous and situational", and humanist texts describe themselves as consensus statements, not creeds. **Resolution:** the same texts lay down stable principles (the harm limit, moral equality, commitment to all humankind). This limits how a principle is held, not whether it is held.

**Decisions**

*D1. When a principle is stated only in Tier 2 texts that one major branch accepts. Decided 2026-10-13.* The project lead left this to the project's judgement. The tier and branch rules were kept as written; the reasons are recorded in [`sources/corpus.md`](../../sources/corpus.md). Zoroastrianism was therefore re-tested in the texts that every branch accepts (the Gathas and the Yasna Haptanghaiti), under the confirmed counting rule for wider duties, by one researcher and one independent verifier. **Result: not met.** The Gathas condemn fury and cruelty (row 3f), but no verse teaches a duty not to harm anyone at the Explicit or Clear teaching level, and in Mills's translation (SBE 31) and the Bartholomae English on avesta.org, Yasna 33.2 and 47.4 point the other way for the follower of the Lie (section G). The Younger Avesta, which only traditionalists accept, adds nothing above Implied for the floor as a whole; the Vendidad's graded penalties for assaulting any person (Fargard 4), which traditionalists accept and many reformists do not, are penal law and support the floor for that branch only (annex, row ZO-3). Zoroastrianism meets the floor in the Tier 2 Pahlavi texts that the traditionalist branch treats as authoritative (rows 3a and 3b). So, under the rules as written, the article cannot be admitted, whatever is decided on D2.

Four things could change this: a verified Gathic passage teaching non-harm to all (Insler and Humbach have not been read directly); a reading of the Pahlavi commentary (*Zand*) on Yasna 33.2, 46.6 and 47.4, the traditionalists' own key authority; a different reading of those verses established to the project's standard; or a general rule change by the project lead, decided on its merits for every tradition. The change considered was to let a Tier 2 text that one major branch treats as primary count for the whole tradition, provided the other branch's primary texts do not contradict it. Under that rule the outcome would itself turn on how Yasna 33.2 and 47.4 are read.

*Is Zoroastrianism held to a stricter standard than other traditions?* The second independent check asked this, and three comparisons answer it. **Judaism:** the claim that the rule reaches everyone rests partly on rabbinic texts that Karaite Jews reject (note 3). For Karaites the evidence is the Tanakh's own verses (rows 2a and 2b, Clear teaching), whose reach depends on how "neighbour" and "stranger" are read. Karaite readings were not checked, and whether Karaites count as a major branch has not been decided; this is recorded as a gap of the same kind. **Buddhism:** Mahāyāna Buddhists accept the early discourses in Chinese and Tibetan versions and Śāntideva (row 4d); the gap there is that parallels have not been checked, not that a branch rejects the texts. **Taoism:** Tao Te Ching 49 and 60 are counted as Clear teaching (borderline) although they speak of the sage, while the Gathic verses on fury and cruelty are rated Implied. The difference is scope, not teaching style: the same Gathas also commend harming, or opposing, the follower of the Lie (Yasna 33.2 and 47.4), so their condemnation of cruelty cannot be read as a duty owed to everyone.

*D2. Slavery and authority within households. Decided 2026-10-13: they are included; the wording is still to be decided.* The project lead decided that the floor covers slavery and authority within households, and that the wording will be chosen after reading what the texts say. The evidence is in the [annex](article-01-annex-households.md); section E above summarises it. Under the project's rules (every tradition must clearly teach the floor, and nothing may contradict its core), this is what it means for each possible wording (called L1, L2 and L3 in the annex):
- **(a) No cruelty only (L1).** Taught in seven traditions. Contradicted by counted texts in four traditions (Hinduism, Judaism, Stoicism, Christianity) and unclear in two (Islam; and Zoroastrianism, where it is taught only in texts the traditionalist branch accepts, the same gap as D1). Not admissible as the texts stand, but the nearest of the three: its contradictions are confined to a few named texts.
- **(b) No cruelty and no one owned (L1 and L2).** Only two traditions teach L2 in counted texts, and it is contradicted in five and in one branch of two more. Not admissible.
- **(c) All three (L1, L2 and L3).** No tradition's counted texts clearly teach L3, and it is contradicted in five and in one branch of a sixth. Not admissible.

*How much turns on the word "injure".* The annex reads "injure" in L1 as harm beyond moderate physical correction, leaving correction to L3. If "injure" includes any physical punishment, every counted text against L3 also counts against L1: L1 is then contradicted in five traditions (Islam is added, through Qur'an 4:34) and in one branch of Buddhism, and is hardly nearer to admission than L3. The project lead may want to settle this reading when choosing the wording.

*A lower floor was tried*, as the method requires before a question goes to the Register: "do not kill, maim, starve or torment anyone in your power". On the evidence already gathered it still fails: Seneca's *De Ira* 3.32 (breaking a slave's legs), Exodus 21:20 to 21 in the rabbinic reading (no death penalty for a master whose beaten slave dies after a day) and, for Catholics and Orthodox, Sirach 33, the passage on servants ("tortures and torments for an evil servant"; verse number not confirmed) contradict it, and it is unclear for Hinduism, Islam and Zoroastrianism. This step was taken from the annex's evidence without new searches and should itself be checked.

*Next step.* Whichever wording is chosen, the method sends a wording that fails to the Register. The proposed entry is Q-002, "May a person hold another as property, physically punish those in their care, or have sexual relations with a person in their power without that person's consent?". It would separate the parts of the disagreement: textual (for example the meaning of *dāsa* in the Tattvārtha Sūtra, of the verb in Bṛhadāraṇyaka 6.4.7, of "avenged" in Exodus 21:20, and of the verb in Qur'an 4:34), logical (whether each tradition's own wider duty not to harm implies the wording, as several of its later authorities have argued; and, for Sikhism, the argument that a scripture forbidding even a harsh word forbids a blow), and possibly axiomatic (whether a fixed order of rank within the household is itself held as a value). It also lists general questions of method for the project lead: whether a Tier 2 text that a major historical branch treated as primary should count for that branch (the Five Classics in Han to Tang Confucianism); whether an authentic hadith outside the main collections counts; whether Karaite Jews count as a major branch; and how much weight a tradition's later authoritative conclusions should carry when its primary texts are not withdrawn. The entry has not been opened; it waits for the project lead's choice of wording.

*D3. Whether a wider duty not to harm counts. Decided 2026-10-13: yes.* A passage that teaches "harm no one" counts as teaching this floor, provided it reaches everyone (counting rule 2). This is now a general rule in [`METHOD.md`](../../METHOD.md), section 3. Sikhism, Taoism, secular humanism and, for its Digambara branch, Jainism therefore meet the floor through their wider rules (notes 1 and 5).
