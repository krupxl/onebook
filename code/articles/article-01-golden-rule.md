# Article I: The Golden Rule

> **Status: DRAFT, NOT ADMITTED.** Twelve of the thirteen traditions meet this article's floor under the project's rules. Zoroastrianism states the rule explicitly, but only in Tier 2 texts whose authority one major branch of the tradition does not accept. Under the rules as written, that is not enough. The article is held until the project lead decides how the tier rule applies in this case (see "Decision needed" at the end). The fuller form of the rule is recorded as an open question in [`register/q-001-golden-rule.md`](../../register/q-001-golden-rule.md).

**The Article**

Do not harm anyone in a way you would not want to be harmed yourself.

**Why**

Everyone knows what it is like to be hurt, cheated, frightened or made to feel small. This article asks you to use that knowledge as a test before you act: if you would not want it done to you, do not do it to someone else. The rule asks nothing of you that you do not already ask of other people when you are the one on the receiving end.

**The floor**

What this requires: Before you do something to another person, imagine being on the receiving end. If you would not want to be harmed in that way, do not harm them in that way. Use the same test for everyone, whoever they are, and make no exception for yourself.

What this does not require:
- It does not require you to do for others everything you would want for yourself. Many traditions ask for that and more; how much active help to give is left to each tradition and to conscience.
- It does not decide whether, when or how punishment, self-defence, war or the correction of wrongdoing can be right. Each tradition answers those questions in its own way.
- It does not settle how animals and other living beings should be treated. That is a separate candidate (C15).
- It does not tell anyone what to feel. Many traditions also ask for love or goodwill towards all, and remain free to.
- It does not yet cover every way of treating people, only harm. The fuller rule ("do not treat anyone in any way you would not want to be treated") is not yet in the Code; see Register entry Q-001.

**Notes on the evidence**

1. *Positive and negative forms.* Several traditions state the rule in its positive form ("treat others as you would want to be treated": rows 5b, 8a, 9a, 10c). A passage that tells you to treat others as you would want to be treated rules out harming them in ways you would not want, so these count for this floor.
2. *Tier 2 evidence.* Where the strongest statement is Tier 2 (Hinduism, Judaism, Islam), each tradition also has Tier 1 evidence at Clear teaching or above, so the tier rule is met. Islam also treats the authentic Sunnah as primary (`sources/corpus.md`). Zoroastrianism is the exception: see "Decision needed".
3. *Jainism and branches.* Rows 5a and 5b are from the Śvetāmbara canon, which Digambara Jains do not accept. Row 5c is from the Tattvārtha Sūtra, which all branches accept, so the floor is met for both. It was verified through a digitised published book rather than a site on the project's list.
4. *Taoism.* Neither the Tao Te Ching nor the Zhuangzi states the rule as a formula; Taoism teaches through the example of the sage. Tao Te Ching 49 teaches unmistakably that the sage takes other people's hearts as his own and is good even to those who are not good, which rules out harming them in ways he would not accept. This is rated Clear teaching, but it is the thinnest Tier 1 evidence in the table and rests on one chapter. The Ganying Pian (row 7c) shows that the tradition itself reads its ethics this way, but as a Tier 2 text it only supports.
5. *Universal scope.* Every tradition has at least one verified passage that applies the teaching to people outside its own community (for example rows 2a, 4a, 6a, 8a, 9a, 10c, 11d, 13b).

**Counter-evidence considered**

*Punishment, self-defence and war (all traditions).* Every tradition permits or requires some acts that the person on the receiving end would not want: courts and punishment (Romans 13:4; Manusmṛti 7; Kitáb-i-Aqdas ¶62 and ¶45), defensive force (Guru Granth Sahib ang 1105; the Bhagavad Gita's call to Arjuna; Qur'an 2:194), and war. Each tradition presents the rule as a summary that sits inside a wider body of justice, not as a rule that cancels it ("the rest is commentary", Shabbat 31a; "this is the Law and the Prophets", Matthew 7:12; "this, in brief, is dharma", Mahabharata 13.114.8). Aquinas (Summa Theologiae II-II q.64 a.2), Seneca and Marcus Aurelius (punishment only to correct, without anger), and the Bahá'í Notes to the Kitáb-i-Aqdas (penalties belong to institutions, not individuals) make the same distinction. **Resolution:** the article does not decide these questions ("What this does not require"). They belong to other candidates, chiefly C02 (do not kill the innocent) and C19 (do not take revenge).

*The positive form imposes your own tastes (Taoism, secular humanism).* Zhuangzi ch. 18 tells of a marquis who "nourished a bird with what nourishes himself" and killed it; G. B. Shaw wrote "do not do unto others as you would that they should do unto you; their tastes may not be the same". **Resolution:** both objections target the positive form. This article's floor is negative: it forbids inflicting harm you would not accept. Harms such as pain, fear, loss and humiliation are the things people least differ about (Dhammapada 129; Ācārāṅga I.2.3).

*Scope: one's own community or everyone?* Some of the strongest Tier 1 statements are framed for one's own people: Leviticus 19:18 ("the children of your people"); the hadith "for his brother" (Bukhārī 13); the Fravarane creed (Yasna 12.2 to 3). **Resolution:** each tradition's own texts extend the rule beyond the community: Leviticus 19:34 and Exodus 23:9 (the foreigner); Luke 10:29 to 37 (the Good Samaritan); Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 1844a ("the people") and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 45 ("his neighbour"); Qur'an 5:8 (do not let hatred of a people make you unjust); Yasht 10.2 (keep faith with those of another faith); Seneca, Letter 47 (slaves); Guru Granth Sahib ang 1299 ("no one is a stranger"); Kitáb-i-Aqdas ¶144 (associate with all religions in friendship).

*Laws that treat insiders and outsiders differently.* Several traditions' legal texts set different penalties, rights or courtesies by birth group or religion: Manusmṛti 8.270 to 272 (penalties graded by *varṇa*); Mishnah Bava Kamma 4:3 (damages between Israelite and gentile oxen); Deuteronomy 23:20 to 21 (interest); Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2167 (greetings and right of way); Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 6915 (retaliation); Aquinas, Summa Theologiae II-II q.11 a.3 (heretics). **Resolution within each tradition:** Hindu tradition ranks the revealed texts above the law books, and Manu itself sets a common duty of non-injury for all classes; the Talmud itself questions the Bava Kamma rule (Bava Kamma 38a) and later authorities confine it; Qur'an 5:8 and 60:8 and Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 3166 forbid injustice and harm to non-Muslims; the Catholic Church now teaches religious freedom (Dignitatis Humanae 2) and calls the death penalty inadmissible (Catechism 2267, 2018). None of these texts is read by its tradition as permission for an individual to harm another in a way they would not accept themselves, so none is a core contradiction of this floor. The wider question of equal treatment in law across groups is not settled here; it belongs to candidate C06b and may need its own Register entry.

*Harm to the wicked or the enemy.* Yasna 33.2 commends the one who does harm to the follower of the Lie, and Yasna 46.6 is often read as forbidding kindness to such people (Zoroastrianism); Psalm 139:21 to 22 speaks of hating God's enemies; the Mahāvaṃsa (ch. 25, outside the Buddhist canon) consoles a king that the enemies he killed hardly counted as human; a Mahāyāna sūtra (*Skill in Means*) praises a bodhisattva who kills a man about to murder five hundred. **Resolution:** the Zoroastrian tradition reads the Gathic passages as resistance to active wrongdoers, a moral category anyone can join or leave, and its Pahlavi texts state the rule without restriction; Jewish tradition forbids private retaliation (Proverbs 24:29) and requires helping an enemy; Theravāda places the chronicle below the canon, which forbids even inner hatred of bandits (Majjhima Nikāya 21) and says a soldier killing in battle is not rewarded (Saṃyutta Nikāya 42.3); the *Skill in Means* case is a narrow act to protect victims, framed as compassion for both sides and rejected by Theravāda. None permits harming people as such.

*Taoist criticism of benevolence.* Tao Te Ching 5 says Heaven and Earth, and the sage, are "not benevolent" and treat people "as straw dogs"; chapter 19 says "abandon benevolence, discard righteousness"; Zhuangzi ch. 24 says "to love the people is the beginning of harming them". **Resolution:** the tradition reads these as rejecting partial, showy or imposed kindness, not as permission to harm. Chapter 19 itself says the people will then return to kindness, and the oldest manuscript (Guodian, c. 300 BCE) reads "abandon artifice and scheming" instead. Chapter 5 is read as impartiality; the same book says the sage is good to all (49) and harms no one (60). The traditional commentaries that state this reading (Wang Bi, Heshang Gong) could not yet be verified.

*Stoic indifference.* Epictetus (Enchiridion 1 and 5) and Marcus Aurelius (2.1) teach that others cannot truly harm you, which might make "what you would not want" a weak test. **Resolution:** the same passage of Marcus draws the opposite conclusion: we are made to work together, and to act against one another is contrary to nature. Seneca: it is more wretched to harm than to be harmed (Letter 95.52).

*Shunning and boycott.* The Bahá'í Faith requires believers to shun Covenant-breakers ('Abdu'l-Bahá, Will and Testament); the Sikh Rehat Maryada imposes boycott on certain schismatic groups; Shayast-ne-Shayast 13.29 lists "keeping away from vile people". **Resolution:** these restrict religious association, not treatment. The same Will commands kindliness to "friends or strangers"; the same Rehat Maryada fixes a daily prayer for the good of all (*sarbat da bhala*); the same Shayast passage puts the rule itself first.

*Humanist reluctance to fix rules.* Humanist Manifesto II calls ethics "autonomous and situational", and humanist texts describe themselves as consensus statements, not creeds. **Resolution:** the same texts lay down stable principles (the harm limit, moral equality, commitment to all humankind). This limits how a principle is held, not whether it is held.
