# Article I: Do Not Harm Others in Ways You Would Not Accept

> **Status: DRAFT, NOT ADMITTED.** Under the counting rule set out below, twelve of the thirteen traditions meet this article's floor in texts that count. Zoroastrianism states the rule explicitly, but only in Tier 2 texts that one major branch does not accept as binding, so under the project's rules as written the article cannot yet be admitted. Three things need the project lead's decision before it can go further (see "Decisions needed" at the end): how the tier rule treats that kind of split (D1); whether the floor leaves aside the old questions of slavery and authority within households, as it leaves aside courts and war (D2); and whether a wider duty not to harm anyone counts as teaching this floor (D3). The evidence for Taoism is borderline. The fuller form of the rule is recorded as an open question in [`register/q-001-golden-rule.md`](../../register/q-001-golden-rule.md).

**The Article**

Do not harm anyone in a way you would not want to be harmed yourself.

**Why**

Everyone knows what it is like to be hurt, cheated, frightened or made to feel small. This article asks you to use that knowledge as a test: if you would not want to be harmed in a certain way, do not harm someone else in that way. Most traditions teach this by asking you to put yourself in the other person's place; it is often called the Golden Rule. Others simply teach that you should harm no one, which includes this and more.

**The floor**

What this requires: In your own dealings with other people, do not harm them in ways you would not want to be harmed. Pain, fear, loss and being made to feel small hurt everyone, so "I wouldn't mind" is no excuse. Use the same standard for everyone, whoever they are, and make no exception for yourself. A simple way to keep it: imagine being on the receiving end.

What this does not require, or does not settle (each is left to each tradition and to conscience):
- **Doing good for everyone.** It does not require you to do for others everything you would want for yourself. Many traditions ask for that and more; how much active help to give is a separate question.
- **Courts, rulers and war.** It does not settle what lawful authorities may do: punishment, self-defence and war. Each tradition answers these in its own way.
- **Households and slavery.** It does not settle the old questions about authority within households (over children, and in some ancient texts over wives and servants) or about slavery. Several ancient texts accepted these relationships while limiting how people in them may be treated. These questions are to be studied separately (see decision D2).
- **The beginning and end of life, and animals.** It does not settle who counts at the beginning and end of life, or how animals should be treated. These are separate questions in the [list of candidates](../../candidates.md).
- **Whom you keep company with.** It does not require you to keep company with everyone. But it still applies to how you treat the people you do not keep company with.
- **What you feel.** It does not tell anyone what to feel. Many traditions also ask for love or goodwill towards all, and remain free to.
- **The fuller rule.** "Do not treat anyone in *any* way you would not want to be treated" is not yet in the Code. It is an [open question](../../register/q-001-golden-rule.md).

**How the evidence is counted**

1. A passage counts if it teaches the test itself for harm: put yourself in the other person's place, or take your own dislike of harm as the measure for others.
2. A passage also counts if it teaches a wider duty not to harm others, because whoever keeps that duty keeps this floor. Such rows are marked "wider rule". (Decision D3 asks the project lead to confirm this.)
3. Passages in the positive form ("treat others as you would want to be treated") count when they concern the manner of treating people, since treating someone as you would want to be treated rules out harming them in ways you would not accept.
4. Strength is rated against this article's floor. The tier rule and branch rule in [`sources/corpus.md`](../../sources/corpus.md) apply.
5. "VERIFIED (search)" means the reference and its content were confirmed through web search results from the linked page, not by reading the page in full (`METHOD.md`, section 3). Translations are listed in [`sources/translations.md`](../../sources/translations.md).

**Notes on the evidence**

1. *How each tradition meets the floor.* Hinduism, Judaism, Buddhism, Jainism, Confucianism, Stoicism, Christianity, Islam and the Bahá'í Faith teach the test itself, putting yourself in the other's place. Sikhism and secular humanism meet the floor through a wider rule, "harm no one", which includes it; their primary texts do not state the test itself (for humanism it appears in Tier 2 explanatory material, row 13d). Taoism teaches taking other people's hearts as one's own and not harming them; this is borderline (note 6).
2. *Hinduism.* The Tier 1 evidence is one verse (1a), which describes the highest yogi's way of seeing; its conduct meaning comes from the commentators. The explicit rule is in the Mahabharata (Tier 2), twice. Row 1c's critical-edition verse was confirmed on a verse-level site that is not on the project's list, and a second check could not find the line on the linked Ganguli page; it should be re-checked by direct reading.
3. *Judaism.* Rabbinic tradition largely reads the "stranger" verses (2a, 2b) as about the convert (Mekhilta on Exodus 22:20; Babylonian Talmud Bava Metzia 59b), though Rashi glosses "stranger" more widely as anyone not born in that land. So the claim that the rule reaches everyone rests, for Judaism, mainly on Hillel's answer to a non-Jew (2c), on Ben Azzai's teaching that "This is the book of the generations of Adam" (Genesis 5:1) is a greater principle than Leviticus 19:18, because it covers all human beings (Jerusalem Talmud, Nedarim 9:4), and on Rabbi Akiva's "beloved is every human being, created in the image" (Mishnah Avot 3:14), which goes on to speak of Israel's particular status.
4. *Buddhism.* Rows 4a to 4c are from the Pali Canon (Theravāda). Mahāyāna Buddhists receive the same early discourses in Chinese and Tibetan recensions (parallels not yet checked), and row 4d gives Mahāyāna's own authoritative statement.
5. *Jainism.* Rows 5a and 5b are from the Śvetāmbara canon, which Digambara Jains do not accept. Row 5c, from the Tattvārtha Sūtra accepted by all branches, meets this floor for Digambaras as a wider rule of non-injury. Row 5d states the test itself and was approved by all four main sects, but it is a modern anthology whose source has not been traced.
6. *Taoism.* Neither the Tao Te Ching nor the Zhuangzi states a rule as a formula; Taoism teaches through the example of the sage, who is the model for everyone. Read together, chapters 49 and 60 teach that the sage takes other people's hearts as his own and harms no one, which includes this floor. This is rated Clear teaching but is borderline: it rests on two short chapters about the sage, often the ruler, and on the counting rule in D3. Row 7c states the test itself, but as a later Tier 2 text it only supports.
7. *Positive and negative forms.* Rows 5b, 8a, 9a and 10c are in the positive form. They count on the "manner of treatment" reading (counting rule 3). The same traditions also have negatively worded rows (5a, 9b, 10d).

**Counter-evidence considered**

The passages below seem to contradict or limit the floor. For each, the tradition's own reconciliation is given, with its authority. Passages marked "not yet verified" could not be confirmed in this session and do not count either way until they are.

*A. Punishment, war, and the objection that the rule forbids all punishment.* Read literally, any rule of this kind would forbid every penalty, because no wrongdoer wants to be punished (a well-known objection, made for example by Kant). Every tradition permits or requires some acts that the person on the receiving end would not want: courts and penalties (Romans 13:4; Manusmṛti 7.14 to 22, not yet verified; Kitáb-i-Aqdas ¶62 and ¶45), defensive force (Guru Granth Sahib ang 1105; Qur'an 2:194), and war (the Bhagavad Gita's call to Arjuna; the Zafarnama in the contested Dasam Granth, not yet verified). Each tradition presents the rule as a summary that sits inside a wider body of justice: "the rest is commentary" (Shabbat 31a); "this is the Law and the Prophets" (Matthew 7:12); "this, in brief, is dharma" (Mahabharata 13.114.8). Aquinas holds that public authority may punish for the common good (Summa Theologiae II-II q.64 a.2); the Bahá'í Notes to the Kitáb-i-Aqdas (notes 86 and 87) place penalties with institutions, not individuals; the Jain householder's partial vow allows defensive harm while still counting it as harm to be kept to a minimum (Tattvārtha Sūtra 7.2; the fourfold classification of harm in the lay manuals, not yet verified). **Resolution:** the article covers personal conduct and leaves lawful authority and war to each tradition ("What this does not settle").

*B. Revenge.* The Book of Rites (Liji, Tangong I; Confucian Tier 2) has Confucius tell a son to be ready to kill his father's murderer on sight; Numbers 35:19 to 21 lets the "avenger of blood" kill a deliberate murderer; Cicero, presenting Stoic ethics, makes the first rule of justice to harm no one "unless provoked by wrong" (De Officiis 1.20). **Resolution within each tradition:** the Gongyang commentary allows vengeance only where the father was not justly executed and warns that revenge for a lawful punishment starts an endless cycle (Gongyang Zhuan, Duke Ding, year 4); Analects 14.34 answers injury with uprightness, not kindness and not spite; Numbers 35:24 to 25 itself puts the case before the congregation to judge and provides cities of refuge, and rabbinic law gives such cases to a court; Leviticus 19:18 forbids taking vengeance and Proverbs 24:29 forbids repaying like for like; Musonius Rufus would never prosecute anyone for a personal injury (Lecture 10), and Marcus Aurelius says the best revenge is not to become like the wrongdoer (Meditations 6.6). The question whether revenge is ever right is a separate candidate (C19).

*C. Violence against enemies, dissenters and those who leave the faith.* Hebrew Bible commands to destroy the seven nations and Amalek (Deuteronomy 20:16 to 17; 1 Samuel 15:3), shared by Judaism and Christianity, and the law against one who entices others to other gods (Deuteronomy 13:7 to 12 in Hebrew numbering, 13:6 to 11 in English); death for leaving Islam (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 6922); the second half of Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 1844a (force against a rival who challenges a pledged ruler); Aquinas on executing obstinate heretics (Summa Theologiae II-II q.11 a.3) and Augustine on compelling the Donatists (Letters 93 and 185); the Pahlavi ruling that an apostate is "worthy of death" (Dadestan-i Denig, ch. 76 in West's numbering); a brahmin's licence to strike enemies with spells (Manusmṛti 11.31 to 33) and the Atharvaveda's hostile charms (Tier 1); a Mahāyāna sūtra's teaching that killing those who slander the Dharma carries little weight (Mahāparinirvāṇa Sūtra; not yet verified). **Resolution within each tradition:** rabbinic law holds that the ancient nations can no longer be identified and that peace must be offered before any war (Maimonides, Hilkhot Melakhim 6:1), and called a court that executes once in seven years "destructive" (Mishnah Makkot 1:10, with a recorded dissent); Aquinas holds that the judicial precepts of the Old Law ceased with Christ (Summa Theologiae I-II q.104 a.3), the Reformed confessions say the same of Israel's judicial laws (Westminster Confession 19.4), Augustine asked that the Donatists be corrected, not killed (Letter 100), and the Catholic Church now teaches religious freedom (Dignitatis Humanae 2); Islamic law treats apostasy and rebellion as matters for a court, after a call to repent, never for individuals (the chapter heading of Bukhārī 6922 is "seeking the repentance of apostates"); the Dadestan-i Denig says an apostate is "worthy of death" only by the decision of judges and the command of kings; Mīmāṃsā holds that Vedic rites for harming an enemy are described but are not right conduct, because injury is forbidden (not yet verified); East Asian Buddhist readers take the Mahāparinirvāṇa passage as hyperbole about slandering the Dharma, and Theravāda does not accept the text (not yet verified). These passages concern war, courts and religious law, which the article leaves aside (section A). Manusmṛti 11.31 to 33 and the Atharvaveda charms are the hardest cases, because they concern individuals: Hindu tradition ranks the Mahabharata's statement of the rule as the summary of dharma, and Manusmṛti 10.63 itself makes non-injury a duty common to all classes (not yet verified).

*D. Laws that treat insiders and outsiders differently.* Penalties graded by birth group (Manusmṛti 8.270 to 272); damages between Israelite and gentile oxen (Mishnah Bava Kamma 4:3); interest from foreigners (Deuteronomy 23:20 to 21); not rescuing certain people from a pit (Babylonian Talmud Avodah Zarah 26a to b); greetings and right of way for Jews and Christians (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2167); no retaliation against a Muslim for killing a non-Muslim, in the majority classical view (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 6915); the jizya paid "while they are humbled" (Qur'an 9:29); "firm against the disbelievers" (Qur'an 48:29). **Resolution within each tradition:** Hindu tradition ranks the revealed texts above the law books, and the Bhagavad Gita says the wise see the same in a brahmin and an outcaste (5.18; not yet verified); the Talmud records an objection to the Bava Kamma rule and its own justification (Bava Kamma 38a), and later authorities confine such distinctions (Menachem Meiri; not yet verified); the Chazon Ish confines the Avodah Zarah ruling to an age long past (not yet verified); in Islam, Qur'an 5:8 forbids letting hatred of a people make you unjust, 60:8 permits kindness and fairness to those who do not fight you, Bukhārī 3166 makes killing a protected non-Muslim a grave sin, the Hanafi school does apply retaliation for killing a protected non-Muslim (not yet verified), and al-Adab al-Mufrad 1102 places the greeting instruction on the eve of a conflict. These reconciliations are not all equally strong: several rest on later authorities rather than on the primary texts. None of the texts tells an individual to harm another person outside the setting of law or war, so they are not treated as contradicting this floor, but the wider question of equal treatment in law is not settled here (candidate C06b).

*E. Slavery and authority within the household.* Several ancient texts accept slavery: foreign slaves held permanently, while fellow Israelites are not to be ruled "with rigour" (Leviticus 25:39 to 46); "slaves, obey your masters" (Ephesians 6:5); Seneca and Epictetus regulate the treatment of slaves without opposing slavery itself (rows 8a and 8c). Several permit physical discipline within the household: a husband's striking of a wife as a last resort (Qur'an 4:34); beating a wife, son, slave, pupil or younger brother with a rope or cane, on the back only (Manusmṛti 8.299 to 300; not yet verified); striking a slave, with punishment only if the slave dies under the master's hand (Exodus 21:20 to 21); correcting a child with the rod (Proverbs 23:13 to 14). **Resolution within each tradition:** each text limits how people in these relationships may be treated, often in Golden Rule terms. "Did not He who made me in the womb make him?" (Job 31:13 to 15); a slave who loses an eye or a tooth goes free (Exodus 21:26 to 27); masters must stop threatening, for both have the same Master (Ephesians 6:9), Paul asks that a runaway slave be received "as a beloved brother" (Philemon 16), and the Catechism of the Catholic Church forbids anything that leads to enslaving human beings (2414); "your slaves are your brothers… feed them of what you eat and clothe them of what you wear" (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 30; not yet verified), freeing a slave is praised (Qur'an 90:13), and striking is limited to "not severely" (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 1218a), "without injury" (Ibn Mājah 1851) and "those who beat their wives are not the best of you" (Abū Dāwūd 2146); Seneca and Epictetus forbid cruelty and contempt towards slaves; the Bahá'í Faith forbids trading in slaves (Kitáb-i-Aqdas ¶72; not yet verified); the Buddha tells lay followers not to trade in human beings (Aṅguttara Nikāya 5.177; not yet verified). But the institutions themselves were accepted, and this floor, read today, would condemn them. **This is not fully resolved.** The draft leaves these institutions aside, as it leaves aside courts and war, and records the question for separate study. Whether that is the right reading of the floor is decision D2.

*F. The beginning of life.* Seneca gives drowning weak or deformed newborns as an example of reason acting without anger (De Ira 1.15), though his fellow Stoic Musonius Rufus argues against exposing infants (Lecture 15). Humanist Manifesto II affirms a right to abortion, while several other traditions treat the unborn as a person. **Resolution:** Stoicism has no later authority to settle this; it is recorded as a disagreement within the school. Who counts at the beginning and end of life is left to the separate candidate on killing (C02).

*G. Harm or contempt towards the wicked.* Yasna 33.2 commends the one who does harm to the follower of the Lie, and Yasna 46.6 is rendered either as forbidding kindness to such people or as forbidding shielding them (Zoroastrianism); Psalm 139:21 to 22 speaks of hating God's enemies (Judaism and Christianity); Kabir's saloks compare the faithless person (*sākat*) unfavourably with a pig (Guru Granth Sahib ang 1372); the Mahāvaṃsa (ch. 25, outside the Buddhist canon) tells a king that the enemies he killed hardly counted as human; a Mahāyāna sūtra (*Skill in Means*) praises a bodhisattva who kills a man about to murder five hundred; Tao Te Ching 36 describes giving in order to take, and 65 describes keeping the people simple. **Resolution within each tradition:** the reformist reading of Yasna 46.6 (Taraporewala, as quoted on avesta.org; secondary) and of the Gathic "followers of the Lie" as a moral type anyone may join or leave (not yet verified to the project's standard); Matthew 5:44 commands loving enemies, and Exodus 23:4 to 5 requires helping an enemy's animal (not yet verified); in Sikh teaching *sākat* names a spiritual condition, not a community, and Farid's saloks in the same scripture forbid harsh words to anyone (Sikh commentators not yet verified); Theravāda places the chronicle below the canon, which forbids even inner hatred of bandits (Majjhima Nikāya 21) and says a soldier who dies fighting with the wish to kill goes to a bad rebirth, not to heaven (Saṃyutta Nikāya 42.3); the *Skill in Means* case is a narrow act to protect victims, framed as compassion for both sides, and is rejected by Theravāda; Wang Bi reads chapter 36 as a way of dealing with the violent and oppressive, and chapter 65's "simple" as unspoiled genuineness (not yet verified).

*H. Using your own tastes as the measure.* Zhuangzi ch. 7 (Inner Chapters) tells how two emperors bored seven openings into their host Hundun to repay his kindness, and he died; ch. 18 (Outer Chapters) tells of a marquis who fed a seabird as he would feed himself, and it died. G. B. Shaw's objection that "their tastes may not be the same" is discussed in Humanists UK's explanatory material. **Resolution:** in Zhuangzi's own terms the lesson is to nourish a bird as a bird would be nourished, that is, to attend to the other's nature and not impose on them; that supports restraint, which is the direction of this floor. Both stories end in death, a harm everyone would refuse. The objection falls mainly on the positive form; how far it affects the negative form is part of the open question (Q-001, Part 9).

*I. Taoist criticism of benevolence.* Tao Te Ching 5 says Heaven and Earth, and the sage, are "not benevolent" and treat people "as straw dogs"; chapter 19 says "abandon benevolence, discard righteousness"; Zhuangzi ch. 24 (Miscellaneous Chapters) says "to love the people is the beginning of harming them". **Resolution:** these reject partial, showy or imposed kindness, not care for people. Chapter 19 itself says the people will then return to kindness, and the oldest manuscript (Guodian, c. 300 BCE) reads "abandon artifice and scheming" instead. Chapter 5 is read as impartiality; the same book says the sage is good to all (49) and harms no one (60). The traditional commentaries that state this reading (Wang Bi, Heshang Gong) are not yet verified, so this resolution is provisional.

*J. Stoic indifference.* Epictetus (Enchiridion 1 and 5) and Marcus Aurelius (2.1) teach that others cannot truly harm you, which might make "what you would not want" a weak test. **Resolution:** the same passage of Marcus draws the opposite conclusion: we are made to work together, and to act against one another is contrary to nature. Seneca: it is more wretched to harm than to be harmed (Letter 95.52).

*K. Shunning and exclusion.* The Bahá'í Faith requires believers to "shun and avoid entirely" Covenant-breakers ('Abdu'l-Bahá, Will and Testament); the Sikh Rehat Maryada imposes boycott, including in social dealings, on certain schismatic groups; the New Testament tells believers not to receive or greet a false teacher (2 John 10 to 11) and not to eat with certain wrongdoers (1 Corinthians 5:11 to 13); Shayast-ne-Shayast 13.29 lists keeping away from "vile" people. Being shunned is something no one would want. **Resolution within each tradition:** in each case the separation is presented as protecting the community, and decided by its institutions, not by individuals at will; the same Will commands "sincere kindliness unto all the peoples… be they friends or strangers"; the same Rehat Maryada fixes a daily prayer for the good of all (*sarbat da bhala*); Paul adds "count him not as an enemy, but admonish him as a brother" (2 Thessalonians 3:14 to 15) and leaves those outside the community to God's judgement (1 Corinthians 5:12 to 13). Withholding company is not itself harm in this floor's sense, and the article does not require anyone to keep company with everyone; but how far exclusion may go is part of the open question (Q-001, Part 8).

*L. The Báb's earlier laws (Bahá'í Faith).* The Bayán, a Tier 1 text with a flag, laid down severe ordinances, such as destroying books other than those defending the Cause. **Resolution:** Bahá'u'lláh released believers from the ordinance on books (Kitáb-i-Aqdas ¶77) and declared the law of holy war "blotted out from the Book" (Lawḥ-i-Bishárát, first Glad-Tidings), and 'Abdu'l-Bahá states that the Bayán is superseded except where the Aqdas confirms it.

*M. Humanist reluctance to fix rules.* Humanist Manifesto II calls ethics "autonomous and situational", and humanist texts describe themselves as consensus statements, not creeds. **Resolution:** the same texts lay down stable principles (the harm limit, moral equality, commitment to all humankind). This limits how a principle is held, not whether it is held.

**Decisions needed**

*D1. When a principle is stated only in Tier 2 texts that one major branch accepts.* The rule is stated explicitly in Zoroastrian Pahlavi texts: the same maxim in the Denkard and the Dadestan-i Denig (row 3a) and in the Andarz of Adurbad (row 3b), with a third, unverified, report in the Shayast-ne-Shayast (row 3c). No passage of the Avesta teaches it above the Implied level: Yasna 43.1 reads as reciprocity only in reformist and Iranian priestly renderings, and Yasna 33.2 and 46.6 seem to point the other way. Traditionalists, Parsi and Iranian, treat the Pahlavi books as the authoritative interpretation of scripture; reformists ("Gathas-only") set them aside. Under the tier rule and branch rule as written, this does not count, so the article is held. Because `sources/corpus.md` forbids changing a rule to rescue a particular article, any change must be a general rule, decided on its merits for every tradition and every later article. The same question already arises for the Digambara branch of Jainism on the fuller rule (Q-001). Options: (a) keep the rule as written; or (b) adopt a general rule that a Tier 2 text accepted as authoritative by a major branch may count when the other branches' primary texts do not contradict it, recording the branch caveat.

*D2. Slavery and authority within households.* Several traditions' primary texts accept slavery and some physical discipline within households while limiting how people in them may be treated (section E). Options: (a) the floor concerns personal conduct and, like courts and war, leaves these institutions aside for separate study (the draft's current wording); or (b) the floor covers them, in which case several traditions' primary texts contradict it and the article cannot be admitted at this floor.

*D3. Whether a wider duty not to harm counts.* The draft counts a passage that teaches "harm no one" as teaching this floor, because it includes it (counting rule 2). If the project lead requires the test itself, Sikhism, Taoism and secular humanism do not meet the floor in their primary texts, and the article could not be admitted.
