# Open Question 001: Should a person never treat anyone in a way they would not want to be treated themselves?

This is the Golden Rule in its full negative form: not only "do not harm others in ways you would not want to be harmed" (the draft Article I), but "do not treat anyone in *any* way you would not want to be treated".

**Why this is in the Register.** No tradition's texts reject this rule. Eleven of the thirteen traditions teach it clearly, and in most it is stated in so many words. Two do not meet the project's test, for different reasons:

- **Secular humanism:** the founding declarations teach equal rights, equal dignity, compassion and not harming others, but none uses one's own wishes as the measure for how to treat people. The rule itself appears only in explanatory material (Tier 2).
- **Zoroastrianism:** the rule is stated explicitly in Pahlavi texts (Tier 2) that one major branch does not accept as binding. The Avesta (Tier 1) does not teach it above the Implied level.

The question is therefore one of **silence and the standing of texts**, not of opposed teachings. That matters for the project's working hypothesis (see "Axiomatic residue" below).

**Fair statement of the two positions**

*Zoroastrianism.* A traditionalist Zoroastrian would say: the ethical teaching of our faith has always been carried by the Pahlavi books, which preserve the priests' interpretation of the Avesta; "do not do to others what is not good for oneself" is one of the five accomplishments owed to religion (Shayast-ne-Shayast 13.29) and the very definition of a good nature (Denkard 6). A reformist would say: the Gathas, Zarathushtra's own words, are our only binding scripture; they call us to good thoughts, good words and good deeds and to stand against the Lie, and they do not contradict this rule, but they do not state it either. Both would deny that their faith permits doing to others what one would not want done to oneself.

*Secular humanism.* A humanist would say: humanist ethics rests on reason, empathy and compassion, not on fixed rules; our declarations commit us to the equal worth and dignity of every person, to freedom limited by the equal rights of others, and to "helping and not harming"; many humanists live by the Golden Rule and humanist education presents it, but our founding texts express these values through rights and dignity rather than through a single maxim.

**Axiomatic residue**

None identified. No tradition's texts reject the rule, and no foundational value has been found that requires treating others in ways one would not accept. The remaining gaps are evidential (what a text says or does not say) and procedural (how the project counts texts whose standing differs between branches).

**Candidate floor (if any)**

"Do not harm anyone in a way you would not want to be harmed yourself." This is drafted as [Article I](../code/articles/article-01-golden-rule.md). Under the rules as written, twelve traditions meet it and Zoroastrianism does not, for the reason in Parts 1 to 3. If the project lead decides Part 3 in favour of counting a branch-accepted Tier 2 text where no text opposes it, the draft article can be admitted, and this entry stays open for the full form.
