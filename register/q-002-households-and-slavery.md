# Open Question 002: How far may authority over the people in one's household or power go?

This entry covers four connected questions, about children, spouses, servants, slaves and pupils:

1. **Harm beyond correction.** May anyone in one's power ever be injured beyond moderate physical correction, or tormented, starved, overworked or humiliated? (In the annex this is wording L1, *no cruelty*. Moderate physical correction belongs to question 3.)
2. **Ownership.** May a person be held or traded as property? (L2, *no one owned*.)
3. **Physical punishment.** May children, spouses, servants or pupils be physically punished? (L3, *no hitting*.)
4. **Consent.** May someone have sexual relations with a person in their power without that person's consent?

*In short: on the first question most traditions' counted texts agree, and a few named texts in four traditions differ. On the second the texts differ widely, though several traditions' later authorities now reject owning people. On the third the texts also differ widely; most later authorities found accept moderate physical correction of children, and some reject striking a wife. The fourth arises from a small number of texts in four traditions. Nothing here judges any tradition.*

**Where this came from.** The project lead decided that the floor of [Article I](../code/articles/article-01-golden-rule.md) must cover households and slavery (decision D2), and then left the choice of wording to the project. The article now asks for *no cruelty* only ([`METHOD.md`](../METHOD.md), section 9, decision 10). Even that is contradicted by counted texts in four traditions. A lower floor ("do not kill, maim, starve or torment anyone in your power") was tried and also failed. So the method sends the whole question here.

The result for question 1 depends on reading "injure" as harm *beyond* moderate physical correction (the annex's rule 9). If any physical punishment counted as injury, *no cruelty* would be contradicted in five traditions, unclear in four, and taught in only four (annex, "What the summary shows").

**Evidence.** All the evidence is in the [annex on households and slavery](../code/articles/article-01-annex-households.md). For every passage it records the tier, the translation, what the passage says, how it bears on each wording, and whether it was verified. Row numbers such as HI-4 or JU-3 point to that annex. No new passages were searched for in writing this entry; it restates the annex's evidence and sets out what is still open. No citation here has a stronger check than the annex gives it. At best a citation is "VERIFIED (search)", some are UNVERIFIED, and none has yet been read directly (`METHOD.md`, section 3).

**Words used.**
- *Counted texts* are Tier 1 texts, and Tier 2 texts that [`sources/corpus.md`](../sources/corpus.md) says the tradition treats as primary.
- *Later authorities* are commentators, councils and modern bodies. They show how a tradition reads its texts and are the main evidence for the Logical parts below. They never outweigh a counted text (`METHOD.md`, section 9, decision 7).
- A Logical part may be marked resolved for a tradition or branch when an authority that it treats as binding has reasoned from its own principles to the conclusion (`METHOD.md`, section 5). That does not admit anything to the Code: texts remain the test of agreement.
- A *branch* is a living division of a tradition, with its own accepted texts, that standard reference works treat as one of its main divisions (decision 3).
- To *presuppose* a practice is to take it for granted without saying it is right.

On the fourth question (consent), the counted texts that bear on it are few. They are Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad 6.4.7 (Hinduism); the Torah's laws on women taken captive in war and on a daughter sold into service (Judaism and Christianity); and the Qur'anic verses and hadith on captive and slave women (Islam). They are set out under those traditions below and in Part 8.

Each tradition's position follows in two parts: what its counted texts say, and the position in the tradition's own terms, including how it reads its hard texts. The second part is written so that the tradition's own adherents could recognise it as fair; it is not the project's view.

### 1. Hinduism

*What the counted texts say.* No counted text commands slavery, and none was found on hitting children, pupils or servants. But two verses of revealed scripture bear against the floor. Ṛg Veda 8.56.3 praises a patron's gift of a hundred *dāsas*, counted with asses and sheep (HI-1). Griffith and Wilson render *dāsas* "slaves" and one modern rendering "servants"; on either reading, persons are given away as a gift. The verse is in a Vālakhilya hymn, one of a group whose standing is reported, though not yet verified, to be disputed. Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad 6.4.7, in a rite for begetting a child, tells a husband to win a wife who refuses him with gifts and, if she still refuses, to strike her with a stick or his hand and overcome her (HI-4). Śaṅkara's classical commentary follows this plain reading (HI-5, verified only on a site not on the project's list). Otherwise the counted texts teach non-harm through a duty to every being (Bhagavad Gita 6.32, HI-6, borderline).

*In the tradition's own terms.* Right conduct (*dharma*) is known first from revealed scripture (*śruti*). Where a remembered text (*smṛti*), such as the law books, conflicts with it, the remembered text gives way (Śaṅkara citing Mīmāṃsā Sūtra 1.3.3, HI-13). The Mahabharata, a remembered text of very high weight, sums up dharma as never doing to another what you regard as harmful to yourself, with no exception for dependants (HI-7). The law books, whose authority is itself contested within the tradition, regulate slavery and allow limited corrective beating of a son, pupil, wife or slave (HI-8, HI-11). They also limit both: light instruments only and on the back, with a thief's guilt for striking otherwise; a pupil as a rule not to be struck; ways out of slavery; and no sale of children (HI-9 to HI-12). Manusmṛti 4.164 forbids striking anyone in anger, but makes an exception for a son or pupil, who may be beaten for correction. Hinduism has no single body that rules for the whole tradition, so each reading binds only the schools that accept it. No classical reading was found that limits the two hard verses. Modern Hindu writers dispute the sense of the verb in Bṛhadāraṇyaka 6.4.7, but they are outside the corpus. Madhva's commentary on the verse, and the Viśiṣṭādvaita commentary of Raṅgarāmānuja (Rāmānuja himself wrote none on the Upanishads), have not yet been read. A widely followed teacher signed the 2014 joint declaration of religious leaders against modern slavery (HI-14), as one teacher's pledge, not a ruling.

### 2. Judaism

*What the counted texts say.* The Tanakh, which every branch accepts, lets slaves bought from other nations be held as inheritable property (Leviticus 25:44 to 46, JU-1). It leaves a master unpunished if a slave he beats with a rod survives a day or two, "for he is his money" (Exodus 21:20 to 21, JU-3). Proverbs commends the rod for a child (23:13 to 14, JU-10). The Torah lets a soldier take a woman captured in war as his wife after a month of mourning, without mentioning her consent; if he no longer wants her, he must let her go and may not sell her (Deuteronomy 21:10 to 14, JU-16). After the war with Midian, girls who had not lain with a man were kept alive for the Israelites (Numbers 31:18, JU-17; the war itself is outside the floor). A father may sell a young daughter as a maidservant, whom the master may take as a wife (Exodus 21:7 to 11, JU-2). For Rabbinic Judaism the Talmud is also counted. It treats a father's striking of a son he is teaching as fulfilling a commandment, and allows a teacher a sandal strap (Makkot 8a to 8b; Bava Batra 21a, JU-13). Shmuel rules that freeing a non-Israelite slave breaks a positive command (Gittin 38b, JU-8). No counted text was found that permits a husband to strike his wife.

*In the tradition's own terms.* The Torah regulates slavery rather than abolishing it, and limits a master's power from within. A slave who loses an eye or a tooth goes free; slaves share the Sabbath rest "as well as thou"; kidnapping a person is a capital crime; an escaped slave is not handed back; and Job grounds a servant's claim in the same Maker who made his master (JU-2, JU-4 to JU-7). Rabbinic law reads two of these narrowly: the death penalty for kidnapping applies to kidnapping a Jew (Mishnah Sanhedrin 11:1), and the escaped-slave law is read narrowly (Gittin 45a) (JU-5). Israelites, as God's servants brought out of Egypt, may not be sold as bondmen or ruled harshly (Leviticus 25:39 to 43, JU-1). Following the Mekhilta, rabbinic reading makes a master who kills a slave outright liable to death, but keeps the exemption when the slave lingers a day (Rashi, JU-4). The Talmud limits striking to a son who is being taught, never a grown son, and asks a husband to love his wife as his own body and honour her more than himself (Yevamot 62b, JU-14). The Talmud is reported to read the law of the captive woman as a concession to human weakness, which Rashi hedges with restrictions (Kiddushin 21b to 22a, JU-16, unverified). Maimonides, in a code that is not counted here, rules that by strict law a master may work a non-Israelite slave harshly, but urges mercy as the way of piety and wisdom (JU-9). The later codes are reported to divide on whether a husband may ever strike his wife (JU-15, unverified). Karaites accept only the Tanakh, and Reform and Reconstructionist Judaism treat the rabbinic texts as valued but not binding. Two rabbis signed the 2014 joint declaration against modern slavery as individuals, not for any branch (JU-18). Karaite readings and the statements of modern bodies have not yet been researched.

### 3. Zoroastrianism

*What the counted texts say.* The Gathas, the only texts every branch accepts, were not found to speak of slavery or of physical discipline in the household. Their one household passage has a bride promise to honour her father and her husband, and asks bride and groom to cherish each other with *asha* (righteousness) (Yasna 53.4 to 5, ZO-1). Yasna 33.2 commends one who wrongs the follower of the Lie by word or thought or with both hands (ZO-14, in Mills's translation); Yasna 47.4, which tells people to set themselves against him, has no link recorded and is unverified. Whether 33.2 reaches anyone in one's household or power is unresolved, which leaves *no cruelty* unclear. Apart from that stanza, the Gathas teach *no cruelty* only at the Implied level (ZO-1; Yasna 48.7, ZO-2, is unverified). Traditionalists also count the Vendidad, where striking any person with evil intent is a graded offence (Fargard 4, ZO-3). The Pahlavi books are not counted for either branch, because traditionalists hold them as the authoritative interpretation of scripture, not as scripture (`METHOD.md`, section 9, decision 4). Among them, the Denkard's summary of a lost Avestan legal book lists rules on selling slaves with beasts of burden and cattle (Denkard, Book 8, ZO-9). If it were counted, *no one owned* would be contradicted for traditionalists.

*In the tradition's own terms.* Traditionalists, Parsi and Iranian, read the Avesta through the Pahlavi books. They hold the maxim that a good nature does not do to another what is not good for itself as a rule for all conduct (ZO-4, ZO-5). No traditionalist authority was found applying it to slaves, reconciling it with the Denkard's rules on sales, or calling for slaves' release. Reformists hold only the Gathas binding, so for them the question is decided by the Gathas alone, which appear silent on owning people and on physical punishment. The difference within the tradition is about which texts bind, not about how they are read. No Zoroastrian authority's reading of Yasna 33.2 or 47.4 has yet been found, in the Pahlavi commentary or among reformists. No present-day statement by either branch has been searched for.

### 4. Buddhism

*What the counted texts say.* The Pali texts teach the measure of oneself: all tremble at the rod and love life, so do not strike or kill (Dhammapada 129 to 130, BU-13). Their parallels in the East Asian and Tibetan canons have not been checked. The Sigālovāda Sutta sets out a master's duties to slaves or servants and workers (work suited to their strength, food and wages, care in sickness, time off) and a husband's to his wife (Dīgha Nikāya 31, BU-6, BU-8). Lay followers must not trade in beings (Aṅguttara Nikāya 5.177, BU-4). The Pali Vinaya, the monastic code of the Theravāda branch, bars a slave from becoming a monk out of deference to his owners' claim (Mahāvagga I.47, BU-1). The closing verse of Jātaka 252, in the Theravāda canon, calls restraining someone with a stick instruction, not hostility (BU-10). The verse itself names no relationship. Its link to a teacher and pupil comes from the prose story, which is commentary and not counted, so the result on question 3 is unclear (Part 22). A Jain verse that treats a teacher's blows in a similar way (Uttarādhyayana 1.38, JA-10) is rated Implied.

*In the tradition's own terms.* Household relations are a web of mutual duties, and a master's power is limited by them. The same ordination procedure that bars slaves also bars debtors and men in royal service (BU-2). So the bar can be read as keeping the Order from being used to escape legal claims, rather than as approving them; no Buddhist authority taking either reading has yet been searched for. The monastic rules forbid a monk to hit another monk in anger, or to raise a hand in anger against anyone (BU-12); whether hitting a novice in anger is itself an offence has not been checked. A novice who harms the monks may be punished only by being barred from the place where he lives or usually goes, never from the whole monastery, and never by having food withheld (Mahāvagga I.57, BU-15). The tradition's commentary tells Jātaka 252 to a monk quick to anger, as a lesson to accept correction without resentment; in its story of the past it identifies the teacher who had his pupil beaten with the Bodhisatta and presents him as in the right (BU-11). In East Asian Mahāyāna a bodhisattva must not sell free people or slaves (Brahmajāla Sūtra, light precept 12, BU-5, still to be re-checked). No authority was found that reconciles the ordination rule or the Jātaka verse with Dhammapada 129 to 130. The Chinese and Tibetan monastic codes have not been checked, so "one branch" means only that the other branches' codes are not yet known. Two Buddhist teachers signed the 2014 joint declaration against modern slavery as individuals (BU-14).

### 5. Jainism

*What the counted texts say.* The Tattvārtha Sūtra, the one text both main branches accept, lists binding, beating, mutilating, overloading and starving living beings as lapses of the householder's vow of non-injury (7.20 in Śvetāmbara numbering, 7.25 in Digambara numbering, JA-1). The Śvetāmbara canon adds that no living being may be tormented (Ācārāṅga I.4.1), because pain is unpleasant to all as it is to oneself (I.4.2) (JA-2). The same sūtra lists female and male *dāsas* among the holdings a householder must keep within a self-set limit (7.24 = 7.29, JA-7). The translators checked, and the Digambara commentator Pūjyapāda, read "servants", though the word can also mean slaves. On the "servants" reading, a limit on how many servants one keeps need not mean they are owned, so the result is unclear. (The same test makes the Hindu result contradicted: a gift of persons treats them as things that can be given away, on either reading.)

*In the tradition's own terms.* Jain teaching rests on non-injury (*ahiṃsā*): harm is injury done through carelessness or passion (7.8 = 7.13, JA-12), and the householder keeps a partial form of the ascetic's full vows. Possession is attachment, to be limited by householders and given up by ascetics (JA-8). So *dāsas*, like land and cattle, are to be limited and ideally renounced; Pūjyapāda places the fault in exceeding one's limit out of greed (JA-9). The Śvetāmbara canon calls a man who punishes his wife, children, slave or servant harshly for small faults hateful (Sūtrakṛtāṅga II.2, JA-3, JA-4; the second check could not reach the passage). Śvetāmbara commentators, known here only through a modern summary, read the lapse of beating as cruel or merciless beating and allow restrained correction of those in one's care (JA-11, not counted). No Digambara reading on this point has yet been checked. No Jain authority was found that either rejects the holding of *dāsas* or names moderate correction of children or a spouse.

### 6. Confucianism

*What the counted texts say.* The Four Books teach anyone with authority to use themselves as the measure: what you dislike from those above you, do not use on those below you (Great Learning, commentary ch. 10, CO-1), and do not impose on others what you would not want yourself (Analects 12.2, CO-2). They do not rule on owning people or on physical correction. The Mencius mentions the beating of a boy only in passing (3B.6, CO-13), and advises fathers to hand their sons' teaching to others so that anger does not hurt the bond (4A.18, CO-10).

*In the tradition's own terms.* The household is a ranked order in which those below owe respect and those above owe care. The wider classical canon, which every historical school held canonical but which this project counts as Tier 2, takes for granted a bought concubine, bondservants and the school rod (CO-5, CO-7, CO-12). It also tells a child to bear without resentment even a beating that draws blood (CO-9; the second check could not display this clause). The tradition reads these texts as calling for restraint and kindness within that order. Heads of households "did not dare to slight" even their servants (Classic of Filial Piety, ch. 8, CO-7). Analects 17.25 says that women and "small people" are hard to deal with; an earlier commentator, Xing Bing, reads it as disparaging both, while Zhu Xi, whose commentary was the examination standard from 1313 to 1905, reads the small people as servants and makes dignity and kindness towards them a duty (CO-8). Rulers of the Han and Xin dynasties cited the saying that human beings are the most precious of all beings when limiting slavery, and the Han scholar Dong Zhongshu proposed removing slaves, all without ending slavery (CO-6, outside the corpus). There is no body with authority to rule for the tradition. The *silent* results on questions 2 and 3 depend on the Five Classics staying uncounted (`METHOD.md`, section 9, decision 4); if they were counted, both would probably be contradicted.

### 7. Taoism

*What the counted texts say.* The Tao Te Ching and the Zhuangzi speak to rulers. The sage does not harm the people (Tao Te Ching 60, TA-1, confirmed only on sites not on the project's list); deals with them, in Legge's reading, as his children (49, TA-2); and the texts blame rulers whose heavy taxes starve them (75, TA-3). The Zhuangzi's horse-tamer who brands, starves and whips his horses is an image of harmful rule (ch. 9, TA-4). They say nothing directly about slaves, servants, wives or the discipline of children and pupils.

*In the tradition's own terms.* Whether the sage-ruler's duty not to harm binds every head of a household is not settled by the core texts. The classical commentators (Heshang Gong, Wang Bi, Guo Xiang) have not yet been read on it, which is why *no cruelty* is "taught (borderline)". Teaching on the household comes in later works, chiefly the Treatise on Response and Retribution (Ganying Pian, Tier 2). It counts as wicked deeds cruelty to those beneath you, misconduct towards wife and children, and forcing free people into servile status, while assuming that a servile class exists and setting unequal duties on husband and wife (TA-8 to TA-11). The religious branches (Zhengyi, Lingbao, Quanzhen) also teach through precept texts and monastic codes. These are reported to limit keeping servants and, for Quanzhen, may set physical penalties for monastics; none of this is yet verified (TA-12, TA-14). No modern Taoist body's statement has been searched for.

### 8. Stoicism

*What the counted texts say.* Seneca accepts slavery as a legal institution: a slave's body is subject and assigned to a master (De Beneficiis 3.20, ST-5), and a master who rules his slaves with moderation is praised (De Clementia 1.18, ST-4). Good parents sometimes correct children with blows (De Clementia 1.14.1, ST-9). In De Ira 3.32 the lash and the breaking of a slave's legs are treated as within a master's power, faulting only haste and anger (ST-8). The second check could not display these De Ira and De Clementia passages. No counted text was found on striking a spouse.

*In the tradition's own terms.* Every human being shares in reason and is kin to every other: Epictetus calls a slave a brother by nature and tells the master not to play the tyrant over him (Discourses 1.13, ST-6). Legal slavery reaches only the body; the mind remains its own master (ST-5). So the moral task lies in how a master treats those below him: do not starve, torment or humiliate them, ask what natural justice allows rather than what the law permits, and live with those below you as you would want those above you to live with you (Letter 47.11, ST-1; ST-4). Seneca's chief concern with punishment is anger. He praises correcting slaves with words only (Letter 47.19, ST-3), and condemns a father who flogged his son to death and fathers who beat their children for the slightest faults (De Clementia 1.15.1 and 1.16.2 to 3, ST-9). These limit hitting but do not forbid it. No Stoic text was found that limits De Ira 3.32 beyond asking the master to wait until his anger has passed; whether Seneca there approves the punishments or names them only to argue against anger is Part 3. No counted text calls for slaves to be freed. Stoicism has no living authority to settle its texts, so its differences are between ancient teachers. The sharpest found, between Seneca and Musonius Rufus on whether every newborn must be raised, is left to candidate C02. Musonius (Tier 2, unverified) is also reported to hold husbands to the sexual standard they demand of wives, and to call marriage a partnership of mutual care (ST-12). Diogenes Laertius reports the early Stoics as calling an owner's lordship bad, though what this means is debated (7.122, ST-13, Tier 2).

### 9. Christianity

*What the counted texts say.* The Old Testament, which every branch counts, contains the laws described under Judaism. Bought foreign slaves may be held as inheritable property (Leviticus 25:44 to 46); a master is left unpunished if a beaten slave survives a day or two (Exodus 21:20 to 21); and the rod is commended for a child (Proverbs 23:13 to 14) (all CH-1). The same books let women and children be taken as spoil in war (Deuteronomy 20:14, which has no link recorded and is unverified), keep alive for the Israelites the girls who had not lain with a man (Numbers 31:17 to 18), and let a soldier take a captive woman as his wife after a month of mourning, without mentioning her consent; if he no longer wants her, he must let her go and may not sell her (Deuteronomy 21:10 to 14) (CH-1). Catholics and Orthodox also count Sirach; Protestants do not. Sirach approves torments and fetters for a disobedient servant while warning "be not excessive" (Sirach 33, verified at chapter level only), and lists "much correction of children" and making an evil servant's side bleed among things not to be ashamed of (42:5) (CH-9). The New Testament tells slaves to obey and honour their masters without asking masters to free them (1 Timothy 6:1 to 2; 1 Peter 2:18, CH-3), which presupposes slaveholding without saying that it is right. No counted text permits striking a wife.

*In the tradition's own terms.* The whole law is summed up in loving your neighbour as yourself, because "love worketh no ill to his neighbour" (Romans 13:9 to 10, CH-2). Master and servant were made by one Creator and answer to one Master who shows no partiality (Job 31:13 to 15; Ephesians 6:9). Within the shared texts, an escaped slave must not be handed back (Deuteronomy 23:15 to 16, CH-1). The New Testament limits household power: masters must give up threatening and give what is just and fair (Ephesians 6:9; Colossians 4:1, CH-4); fathers must not provoke their children (Colossians 3:21, CH-7); husbands must love their wives as their own bodies (Ephesians 5:28 to 29, CH-6); and "menstealers" are condemned (1 Timothy 1:10, CH-5; the scope of the Greek word is Part 15). Catholic teaching, through Aquinas, holds that the judicial laws of the Old Testament, these household laws among them, were annulled by the coming of Christ, though keeping them is not deadly (CH-11). The Reformed Westminster Confession is reported to hold that they expired with the state of Israel (19.4, unverified), and no Orthodox reading has yet been checked. Either way, this ends their binding force without saying that what they allowed was wrong. It does not reach Proverbs or Sirach, which are limited only by their own words. Augustine and Aquinas accept moderate correction of children and slaves, while binding the head of a household to rule for the good of all (CH-10). Rejection of owning people comes from later authority and differs by branch. Gregory of Nyssa condemns it and Chrysostom limits slaveholding (both known here only through modern scholarship); Chrysostom also condemns wife-beating (CH-12). For Catholics, the Catechism forbids enslavement and trading in people (2414, CH-13). No authority in the corpus was found that forbids the physical correction of children. Statements by present-day churches have not yet been searched for; a reviewer's search points to some (for example a 2013 World Council of Churches Assembly statement on corporal punishment), which are leads still to be checked.

### 10. Islam

*What the counted texts say.* The Qur'an, which every branch accepts, permits a man relations with women his right hand possesses, without mentioning their consent, and exempts "those whom your right hands possess" from the ban on married women (23:5 to 6; 4:24, IS-1). It permits a husband, as the last of three steps, to strike a wife whose ill conduct he fears (4:34, IS-10). For Sunni Islam the hadith treated as primary apply the exemption in 4:24 to married women taken captive: they record that relations with women taken captive became lawful once a waiting period had passed (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 1456; Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 4138, IS-2). They also record the Prophet selling a slave (Bukhārī 2141, IS-3); limit the strike in 4:34 to one that is "not severe" (Muslim 1218a, IS-11); and tell parents to strike children of ten who neglect prayer (Abū Dāwūd 495, IS-9). The same texts forbid cruelty. Wives are to be lived with in kindness even when disliked (4:19, IS-14); those under one's hand are to be fed and clothed as oneself and not insulted (Bukhārī 30, IS-8); and God will torment those who torment people (Muslim 2613, IS-8).

*In the tradition's own terms.* Those with power over others answer to God for how they use it. A master who beats a slave without cause must free him (Muslim 1657b, IS-7). Slaves are among those to whom good must be done (4:36, IS-4); owners should grant a contract of self-purchase to slaves who ask, and slave women may not be forced into prostitution (24:33, IS-4); and selling a free person is forbidden (Bukhārī 2227, IS-5). Captives of war are to be released as a favour or for ransom (47:4, IS-16, a text on war and so context only). On striking a wife, the Prophet first forbade it, allowed it when ʿUmar complained that wives had grown bold, and then said that men who beat their wives "are not the best of you" (Abū Dāwūd 2146, IS-12, which counts for Sunni Islam against question 3). The tradition reads these limits, with the Prophet's example of never striking a woman or a servant (IS-15), as a strong preference for not striking, though no counted text withdraws the permission. Ibn Kathīr reports that the strike must not be violent or touch the face (IS-13). In a 2014 open letter, more than 120 scholars, apparently mainly Sunni, state that slavery was abolished by universal consensus (*ijmāʿ*), a recognised source of Islamic law, and that bringing it back is forbidden (IS-6, not counted). Sunni and Twelver Shia Muslims share the Qur'anic texts. Shia hadith and commentators have not yet been checked, nor have the classical rulings on a captive's consent.

### 11. Sikhism

*What the counted texts say.* The Guru Granth Sahib is the only counted text, and no passage found in it permits owning people, hitting dependants or cruelty. It has been searched by targeted queries, not read in full. It teaches non-harm in wide terms: speak no harsh word and break no heart, because the True Lord abides in all (ang 1384, SI-1); its ideal person frightens no one (ang 1427, SI-2). It does not address owning people or physical punishment directly.

*In the tradition's own terms.* The scripture teaches through broad principles rather than rules addressed to husbands, parents or masters. It rebukes contempt for women, "from whom kings are born" (ang 473, SI-8). It teaches that the whole world came from one Light, so "who is good, and who is bad?" (ang 1349, SI-7), a call not to judge some people better than others. It uses slavery mainly as an image of complete devotion to God ("I am Your purchased slave", ang 396, SI-6), an image that neither permits nor condemns owning anyone. The Sikh Rehat Maryada, the mainstream Khalsa code of conduct approved by the SGPC (the elected body that manages the historic gurdwaras of Punjab), turns this teaching into household rules, such as not killing daughters and not taking money for a child's marriage (SI-13, SI-14); it is Tier 2 and does not count on its own. Because no passage found in the scripture permits these practices, no text has been found that needs reconciling. Kabir's harsher verses on women have not been checked, and the Vaaran Bhai Gurdas and the Dasam Granth have not been searched. The open question is whether the scripture's principles reach owning people and physical punishment.

### 12. Bahá'í Faith

*What the counted texts say.* Bahá'u'lláh forbade trading in slaves and forbade any servant of God to buy another. He closed the same paragraph by saying that no one should exalt himself above another, since all are bondslaves before the Lord (Kitáb-i-Aqdas ¶72, BA-1). Because that closing sentence stands in a paragraph about slavery, the annex reads it as reaching the holding of people too (Clear teaching); this is why the result is "taught" and not "taught in part" (Part 10 asks whether this line is drawn consistently). A household maid is a free, paid worker, free to choose a husband when she pleases (Questions and Answers no. 30, BA-3). Bahá'u'lláh forbade striking another or doing anything that saddens hearts (¶148, BA-6). 'Abdu'l-Bahá, whose writings the tradition treats as primary, says it is not permissible to strike or vilify a child (Selections no. 95, BA-9).

*In the tradition's own terms.* Bahá'ís hold that 'Abdu'l-Bahá and then Shoghi Effendi were the authorised interpreters of Bahá'u'lláh's writings, and that the Universal House of Justice explains and legislates where the texts leave matters open. Shoghi Effendi reads the Aqdas as prohibiting slavery itself (BA-4). A 1939 letter written on his behalf calls discipline of some sort, "physical, moral or intellectual", indispensable (BA-10). A 1993 letter written on behalf of the House of Justice reads the teachings as forbidding a husband ever to beat his wife and as condemning all child abuse, while accepting physical discipline of children when it is gentle, patient and loving, far from angry or violent beating (BA-12, BA-13). This is why *no hitting* is "unclear" rather than "taught"; whether a letter written on behalf of the House counts as its ruling is Part 23. Small groups that reject the present House of Justice accept the same scripture; their positions have not been researched.

### 13. Secular humanism

*What the counted texts say.* The founding declarations teach a duty not to harm that applies to everyone ("helping and not harming", 2022 Declaration, HU-1; Humanist Manifesto III, HU-2; Amsterdam Declaration 2002, HU-13). They give every person the greatest possible freedom (in 1952, freedom of development) compatible with the rights of others (HU-5, HU-13, HU-14). That rules out being owned by plain reference (`METHOD.md`, section 9, decision 9), though slavery is not named. No counted text addresses physical punishment directly; Manifesto III's hope that differences be resolved without violence is Implied only (HU-2).

*In the tradition's own terms.* Humanism has no scripture or revealed law. Its texts are revisable statements agreed by humanist bodies, rooted in human need, and later versions replace earlier ones. No humanist text in any tier was found permitting slavery, cruelty to dependants or physical punishment, so there is nothing to reconcile. The explicit stance against hitting children comes from Humanists International policy and Humanists UK, which apply the declarations' principles but are not counted (HU-11, HU-12). The 1980 Secular Humanist Declaration (Tier 1 for its branch) and Humanist Manifesto I have not yet been read in full.

## Axiomatic residue

None confirmed. Four parts are possibly axiomatic and are not yet tested:

1. **What later authority can do** (Part 17): whether a permission in a revealed or founding text can be withdrawn by later authority, or only regulated. This is a premise about sources rather than about conduct, but it decides how far the Logical parts can go.
2. **Where the wrong in a blow lies** (Part 24): whether harm is defined by the act or by the anger or passion behind it.
3. **Rank within the household** (Part 29): whether a fixed order of master and slave, husband and wife, parent or teacher and child, with a rightful power to correct, is itself a value.
4. **Freedom as a status** (Part 30): whether freedom from being owned belongs to everyone or only to some.

Each would prove reducible if the tradition's own texts and authorities treat the premise as serving some further good that every tradition shares (for example, the good of those below). Each would prove axiomatic if a tradition holds it as a good in itself, or holds that revealed permissions cannot be withdrawn. Part 29 is the candidate already listed in `HYPOTHESIS.md`.

## Candidate floor (if any)

None of the following has been tested across all thirteen traditions. They are recorded so that the next round of research has a target, with the counter-evidence already known.

1. **No cruelty** (L1), as drafted in [Article I](../code/articles/article-01-golden-rule.md). Taught in seven traditions. It turns on Parts 1 to 6 and 8, and for Zoroastrianism also on Q-001, Part 1.
2. **The lower floor** tried for Article I: "do not kill, maim, starve or torment anyone in your power". Fails on the annex's evidence (Part 7).
3. **Do not kill anyone in your power.** Some texts that permit beating still punish killing: Exodus 21:20 punishes a master whose slave dies under his hand (JU-3), and Seneca condemns a father who flogged his son to death (ST-9). But counted texts also cut against this floor. In the rabbinic reading, a master whose beaten slave lingers a day and then dies is exempt (JU-3, JU-4), which is one reason the lower floor fails (Part 7). Seneca counts drowning weak or deformed newborns as an act of reason (*De Ira* 1.15.2, ST-11). Texts on war and courts (Numbers 31:17; Deuteronomy 21:18 to 21) are outside the floor (annex, rule 5). This belongs with candidate C02 ("do not kill the innocent"), and these texts should be its first counter-evidence.
4. **Do not punish anyone in your power in anger, or out of proportion to the fault.** Suggested by Part 24. Counted texts in at least six traditions fault punishment given in anger or to excess, though several only for one branch or one relationship: Judaism (Rabbinic: JU-13), Buddhism (monks: BU-12), Jainism (JA-12; for Śvetāmbaras also JA-4), Stoicism (ST-8, ST-9), Christianity (Catholic and Orthodox: Sirach 33, CH-9; for Protestants only "do not provoke your children", CH-7) and Islam (Sunni: IS-7, IS-11, IS-17). Confucianism's Mencius 4A.18 faults anger in a father's teaching, but does not mention striking (CO-10). For Hinduism and the Bahá'í Faith the evidence found is Tier 2 (HI-11; BA-12). No text found approves punishment given in anger. The "out of proportion" half has counter-evidence to weigh: *De Ira* 3.32 treats breaking a slave's legs as within a master's power (ST-8), and Exodus 21:20 to 21 leaves a beating unpunished whatever the fault (JU-3). Whether every tradition's counted texts teach this at the Explicit or Clear teaching level has not been checked.
5. **Do not seize or sell a free person.** Taught in counted texts of several traditions, often the same texts that accept slavery: Exodus 21:16 (JU-5; rabbinic law limits the death penalty to kidnapping a Jew); Aṅguttara Nikāya 5.177 (BU-4) and, for East Asian Mahāyāna, Brahmajāla precept 12 (BU-5, to be re-checked); 1 Timothy 1:10 (CH-5; for the scope of the Greek word, see Part 15); Bukhārī 2227 (IS-5); Kitáb-i-Aqdas ¶72 (BA-1); and the humanist freedom clauses (HU-5). In Tier 2: Āpastamba on the sale of children (HI-10) and the Ganying Pian (TA-11). Counted texts also cut against it as worded. A father may sell his young daughter into service (Exodus 21:7 to 11, JU-2, for Judaism and Christianity), and captives taken in war may be kept (Numbers 31:18, JU-17; Qur'an 4:24 with Muslim 1456 and Bukhārī 4138, IS-1, IS-2). Part of the test is whether the floor can leave war to other candidates without emptying it. Not yet searched in Zoroastrianism, Jainism, Confucianism, Stoicism or Sikhism.

Floors 4 and 5 are narrower than *no cruelty* and *no one owned*. If either passed, it would not settle this question, but it would show where agreement already lies.
