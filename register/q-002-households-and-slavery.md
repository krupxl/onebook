# Open Question 002: How far may authority over the people in one's household or power go?

This entry covers four connected questions, about children, spouses, servants, slaves and pupils:

1. **Harm beyond correction.** May anyone in one's power ever be injured, tormented, starved, overworked or humiliated? (In the annex this is wording L1, *no cruelty*.)
2. **Ownership.** May a person be held or traded as property? (L2, *no one owned*.)
3. **Physical punishment.** May children, spouses, servants or pupils be physically punished? (L3, *no hitting*.)
4. **Consent.** May someone have sexual relations with a person in their power without that person's consent?

*In short: on the first question the traditions' texts are close, with a few named texts standing in the way; on the second and third they differ widely as the texts stand, though several traditions' own principles and later authorities point towards agreement; the fourth arises from a small number of texts in three traditions. Nothing here judges any tradition.*

**Where this came from.** The project lead decided that the floor of [Article I](../code/articles/article-01-golden-rule.md) must cover households and slavery (decision D2), and then left the choice of wording to the project. The article now asks for *no cruelty* only (`METHOD.md`, section 9, decision 10). Even that is contradicted by counted texts in four traditions, and a lower floor ("do not kill, maim, starve or torment anyone in your power") was tried and also failed, so the method sends the whole question here.

**Evidence.** All the evidence is in the [annex on households and slavery](../code/articles/article-01-annex-households.md), which records for every passage its tier, translation, what it says, how it bears on each wording, and its verification status. Row numbers such as HI-4 or JU-3 point to that annex. No new passages were searched for in writing this entry: it restates the annex's evidence, applies the annex's nine labelling rules, and sets out what is still open. Every citation here therefore meets the standard of the annex, which is "VERIFIED (search)" or weaker; none has yet been read directly (see `METHOD.md`, section 3).

**Words used.** *Counted texts* are Tier 1 texts, and Tier 2 texts that `sources/corpus.md` says the tradition treats as primary. *Later authorities* are commentators, councils and modern bodies: they show how a tradition reads its texts and are the main evidence for the Logical parts below, but they never outweigh a counted text (`METHOD.md`, section 9, decision 7). A *branch* is a living division of a tradition with its own accepted texts (decision 3).

On the fourth question (consent), the counted texts that bear on it are few: Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad 6.4.7 (Hinduism), the Torah's laws on women taken captive in war (Judaism and Christianity), and the Qur'anic verses and hadith on captive and slave women (Islam). They are set out under those traditions below and in Part 8.

Each tradition's position follows in two parts: what its counted texts say, and the position in the tradition's own terms, including how it reads its hard texts. The second part is written so that the tradition's own adherents could recognise it as fair; it is not the project's view.

### 1. Hinduism

*What the counted texts say.* No counted text commands slavery or physical punishment, but two verses of revealed scripture bear against the floor. Ṛg Veda 8.56.3, in a Vālakhilya hymn (one of a group of hymns whose standing is disputed), praises a patron's gift of a hundred *dāsas*, which Griffith and Wilson render "slaves", counted with asses and sheep (HI-1). Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad 6.4.7, in a rite for begetting a child, tells a husband to win a wife who refuses him with gifts and, if she still refuses, to strike her with a stick or his hand and overcome her (HI-4); Śaṅkara's classical commentary follows this plain reading (HI-5). Otherwise the counted texts teach non-harm through a duty to every being (Bhagavad Gita 6.32, HI-6, borderline). No verified counted text speaks either way about hitting children, pupils or servants.

*In the tradition's own terms.* Right conduct (*dharma*) is known first from revealed scripture (*śruti*), and where a remembered text (*smṛti*), such as the law books, conflicts with it, the remembered text gives way (Śaṅkara citing Mīmāṃsā Sūtra 1.3.3, HI-13). The Mahabharata, a remembered text of very high weight, sums up dharma as never doing to another what you regard as harmful to yourself, with no exception for dependants (HI-7). The law books, whose authority is itself contested within the tradition, regulate slavery and allow limited corrective beating of a son, pupil, wife or slave, but they also limit both: no striking in anger, light instruments only and on the back, a pupil as a rule not to be struck, ways out of slavery, and no sale of children (HI-8 to HI-12). Hinduism has no single body that rules for the whole tradition, so each reading binds only the schools that accept it. No classical reading was found that limits the two hard verses: modern Hindu writers dispute the sense of the verb in Bṛhadāraṇyaka 6.4.7, but they are outside the corpus, and the commentaries of Rāmānuja and Madhva have not yet been read. A widely followed teacher signed the 2014 joint declaration of religious leaders against modern slavery (HI-14), as one teacher's pledge, not a ruling.

### 2. Judaism

*What the counted texts say.* The Tanakh, which every branch accepts, lets slaves bought from other nations be held as inheritable property (Leviticus 25:44 to 46, JU-1), and leaves a master unpunished if a slave he beats with a rod survives a day or two, "for he is his money" (Exodus 21:20 to 21, JU-3). Proverbs commends the rod for a child (23:13 to 14, JU-10). For Rabbinic Judaism the Talmud is also counted: it treats a father's striking of a son he is teaching as fulfilling a commandment and allows a teacher a sandal strap (Makkot 8a to 8b; Bava Batra 21a, JU-13), and Shmuel rules that freeing a non-Israelite slave breaks a positive command (Gittin 38b, JU-8). No counted text was found that permits a husband to strike his wife.

*In the tradition's own terms.* The Torah regulates slavery rather than abolishing it, and limits a master's power from within: a slave who loses an eye or a tooth goes free, slaves share the Sabbath rest "as well as thou", kidnapping a person is a capital crime, an escaped slave is not handed back, and Job grounds a servant's claim in the same Maker who made his master (JU-2, JU-4 to JU-7). Israelites, as God's servants brought out of Egypt, may not be sold as bondmen or ruled harshly (Leviticus 25:39 to 43, JU-1). Rabbinic reading, following the Mekhilta, makes a master who kills a slave outright liable to death, though it keeps the exemption when the slave lingers a day (Rashi, JU-4). The Talmud limits striking to a son who is being taught, never a grown son, and asks a husband to love his wife as his own body and honour her more than himself (Yevamot 62b, JU-14). Maimonides, in a code that is not counted here, urges mercy towards a non-Israelite slave as the way of piety and wisdom (JU-9), and the later codes are reported to divide on whether a husband may ever strike his wife (JU-15, unverified). Karaites accept only the Tanakh, and Reform and Reconstructionist Judaism treat the rabbinic texts as valued but not binding. Neither Karaite readings nor any modern body's statement has yet been researched.

### 3. Zoroastrianism

*What the counted texts say.* The Gathas, the only texts every branch accepts, were not found to speak of slavery or of physical discipline in the household. Their one household passage asks bride and groom to cherish each other with *asha* (righteousness) (Yasna 53.4 to 5, ZO-1). Two Gathic stanzas commend harming, or setting oneself against, the follower of the Lie (Yasna 33.2 and 47.4, ZO-14), which leaves *no cruelty* unclear, as in Article I. Traditionalists also count the Vendidad and the Pahlavi books they treat as primary: there, striking any person with evil intent is a graded offence (Vendidad, Fargard 4, ZO-3), and a nature is good only when it does not do to another what is not good for itself (ZO-4, ZO-5); but the Denkard's summary of a lost Avestan legal book lists rules on selling slaves, with beasts of burden and cattle (Denkard, Book 8, ZO-9).

*In the tradition's own terms.* Traditionalists, Parsi and Iranian, read the Avesta through the Pahlavi books, and hold the maxim against doing to others what is not good for oneself as a rule for all conduct. No traditionalist authority was found applying it to slaves, reconciling it with the Denkard's rules on sales, or calling for slaves' release. Reformists hold only the Gathas binding, so for them the question is decided by the Gathas alone, which appear silent on owning people and on physical punishment. The difference within the tradition is about which texts bind, not about how they are read. No present-day statement by either branch has yet been searched for.

### 4. Buddhism

*What the counted texts say.* Every branch's texts teach the measure of oneself: all tremble at the rod and love life, so do not strike or kill (Dhammapada 129 to 130, BU-13). The Sigālovāda Sutta sets out a master's duties to slaves or servants and workers (work suited to their strength, food and wages, care in sickness, time off) and a husband's to his wife (Dīgha Nikāya 31, BU-6, BU-8), and lay followers must not trade in beings (Aṅguttara Nikāya 5.177, BU-4). But the Pali Vinaya, the monastic code of the Theravāda branch, bars a slave from becoming a monk out of deference to his owners' claim (Mahāvagga I.47, BU-1), and the closing verse of Jātaka 252, in the Theravāda canon, calls restraining someone with a stick instruction, not hostility (BU-10).

*In the tradition's own terms.* Household relations are a web of mutual duties, and a master's power is limited by them. The monastic rules forbid a monk to hit, or raise a hand against, anyone in anger, and allow a novice who harms the monks to be punished only by being confined to his lodging, not by withholding food (BU-12, BU-15). The tradition's own commentary reads Jātaka 252 as a lesson for a monk quick to anger to accept correction without resentment (BU-11). In East Asian Mahāyāna a bodhisattva must not sell free people or slaves (Brahmajāla Sūtra, light precept 12, BU-5, still to be re-checked). No authority was found that reconciles the ordination rule or the Jātaka verse with Dhammapada 129 to 130, and none has yet been searched for. The Chinese and Tibetan monastic codes have not been checked, so "one branch" means only that the other branches' codes are not yet known, not that they were found silent. Two Buddhist teachers signed the 2014 joint declaration against modern slavery as individuals (BU-14).

### 5. Jainism

*What the counted texts say.* The Tattvārtha Sūtra, the one text both main branches accept, lists binding, beating, mutilating, overloading and starving living beings as lapses of the householder's vow of non-injury (7.20 in Śvetāmbara numbering, 7.25 in Digambara numbering, JA-1); the Śvetāmbara canon adds that no living being may be tormented, because pain is unpleasant to all as it is to oneself (Ācārāṅga I.4.1, JA-2). The same sūtra lists female and male *dāsas* among the holdings a householder must keep within a self-set limit (7.24 = 7.29, JA-7). The translators checked, and the Digambara commentator Pūjyapāda, read "servants", but the word can also mean slaves.

*In the tradition's own terms.* Jain teaching rests on non-injury (*ahiṃsā*): harm is injury done through carelessness or passion (7.8 = 7.13, JA-12), and the householder keeps a partial form of the ascetic's full vows. Possession is attachment, to be limited by householders and given up by ascetics (JA-8), so *dāsas*, like land and cattle, are to be limited and ideally renounced; Pūjyapāda places the fault in exceeding one's limit out of greed (JA-9). The Śvetāmbara canon calls a man who punishes his wife, children, slave or servant harshly for small faults hateful (Sūtrakṛtāṅga II.2, JA-3, JA-4). Śvetāmbara commentators, known here only through a modern summary, read the lapse of beating as cruel or merciless beating and allow restrained correction of those in one's care (JA-11, not counted); no Digambara reading on this point has yet been checked. No Jain authority was found that either rejects the holding of *dāsas* or names moderate correction of children or a spouse.

### 6. Confucianism

*What the counted texts say.* The Four Books teach anyone with authority to use themselves as the measure: what you dislike from those above you, do not use on those below you (Great Learning, commentary ch. 10, CO-1), and do not impose on others what you would not want yourself (Analects 12.2, CO-2). They do not rule on owning people or on physical correction: the Mencius mentions the beating of a boy only in passing (3B.6, CO-13), and advises fathers to hand their sons' teaching to others so that anger does not hurt the bond (4A.18, CO-10).

*In the tradition's own terms.* The household is a ranked order in which those below owe respect and those above owe care. The wider classical canon, which every historical school held canonical but which this project counts as Tier 2, takes for granted a bought concubine, bondservants and the school rod, and tells a child to bear without resentment even a beating that draws blood (CO-5, CO-7, CO-9, CO-12). The tradition reads these texts as calling for restraint and kindness within that order: heads of households "did not dare to slight" even their servants (Classic of Filial Piety, ch. 8, CO-7), and Zhu Xi, whose commentary was the examination standard for six centuries, makes dignity and kindness towards servants a duty (CO-8). Han Confucians cited the saying that human beings are the most precious of all beings to limit slavery, without ending it (CO-6, outside the corpus). There is no body with authority to rule for the tradition. The *silent* results on the second and third questions depend on the Five Classics staying uncounted (`METHOD.md`, section 9, decision 4); if they were counted, both would probably be contradicted.

### 7. Taoism

*What the counted texts say.* The Tao Te Ching and the Zhuangzi speak to rulers: the sage does not harm the people (Tao Te Ching 60, TA-1), deals with them, in Legge's reading, as his children (49, TA-2), and must not starve them with heavy taxes (75, TA-3); the Zhuangzi's horse-tamer who brands, starves and whips his horses is an image of harmful rule (ch. 9, TA-4). They say nothing directly about slaves, servants, wives or the discipline of children and pupils.

*In the tradition's own terms.* Whether the sage-ruler's duty not to harm binds every head of a household is not settled by the core texts, and the classical commentators (Heshang Gong, Wang Bi, Guo Xiang) have not yet been read on it; this is why *no cruelty* is "taught (borderline)". Teaching on the household comes in later works, chiefly the Treatise on Response and Retribution (Ganying Pian, Tier 2), which counts as wicked deeds cruelty to those beneath you, misconduct towards wife and children, and forcing free people into servile status, while assuming that a servile class exists (TA-8 to TA-11). The religious branches (Zhengyi, Lingbao, Quanzhen) also teach through precept texts and monastic codes, which are reported to limit keeping servants and, for Quanzhen, may set physical penalties for monastics; none of this is yet verified (TA-12, TA-14). No modern Taoist body's statement has been searched for.

### 8. Stoicism

*What the counted texts say.* Seneca accepts slavery as a legal institution: a slave's body is subject and assigned to a master (De Beneficiis 3.20, ST-5), and a master who rules his slaves with moderation is praised (De Clementia 1.18, ST-4). Good parents sometimes correct children with blows (De Clementia 1.14.1, ST-9), and in De Ira 3.32 the lash and the breaking of a slave's legs are treated as within a master's power, faulting only haste and anger (ST-8). No counted text was found on striking a spouse.

*In the tradition's own terms.* Every human being shares in reason and is kin to every other: Epictetus calls a slave a brother by nature and tells the master not to play the tyrant over him (Discourses 1.13, ST-6). Legal slavery reaches only the body; the mind remains its own master (ST-5). So the moral task lies in how a master treats those below him: do not starve, torment or humiliate them, ask what natural justice allows rather than what the law permits, and live with those below you as you would want those above you to live with you (Letter 47.11, ST-1; ST-4). Seneca's chief concern with punishment is anger: he praises correcting slaves with words only and condemns lethal and constant beating (Letter 47.19, ST-3; ST-9). No counted text calls for slaves to be freed. Stoicism has no living authority to settle its texts. Its sharpest internal difference is with Musonius Rufus (Tier 2, unverified), who is reported to teach one sexual standard for husband and wife and marriage as a partnership of mutual care (ST-12).

### 9. Christianity

*What the counted texts say.* The Old Testament, which every branch counts, contains the laws described under Judaism: bought foreign slaves held as inheritable property (Leviticus 25:44 to 46), a master left unpunished if a beaten slave survives a day or two (Exodus 21:20 to 21), and the rod commended for a child (Proverbs 23:13 to 14) (all CH-1). Catholics and Orthodox also count Sirach, which approves torments and fetters for a disobedient servant while warning "be not excessive" (Sirach 33; 42:5, CH-9); Protestants do not. The New Testament tells slaves to obey and honour their masters without asking masters to free them (1 Timothy 6:1 to 2; 1 Peter 2:18, CH-3), which presupposes slaveholding without saying that it is right. No counted text permits striking a wife.

*In the tradition's own terms.* The whole law is summed up in loving your neighbour as yourself, because "love worketh no ill to his neighbour" (Romans 13:9 to 10, CH-2), and master and servant were made by one Creator and answer to one Master who shows no partiality (Job 31:13 to 15; Ephesians 6:9). The New Testament limits household power: masters must give up threatening and give what is just and fair (Ephesians 6:9; Colossians 4:1, CH-4), fathers must not provoke their children (Colossians 3:21, CH-7), husbands must love their wives as their own bodies (Ephesians 5:28 to 29, CH-6), and "menstealers" are condemned (1 Timothy 1:10, CH-5; the scope of the Greek word is Part 15). The tradition reads the Old Testament's household laws as judicial laws of ancient Israel that no longer bind as such (Aquinas, CH-11), which ends their force without saying that what they allowed was wrong. Augustine and Aquinas accept moderate correction of children and servants, while binding the head of a household to rule for the good of all (CH-10). Rejection of owning people comes from later authority and differs by branch: Gregory of Nyssa condemns it, Chrysostom limits slaveholding and condemns wife-beating (CH-12), and for Catholics the Catechism forbids enslavement and trading in people (2414, CH-13). No authority of any rank was found that forbids the physical correction of children.

### 10. Islam

*What the counted texts say.* The Qur'an, which every branch accepts, permits a man relations with women his right hand possesses, including married women taken captive, without mentioning their consent (23:5 to 6; 4:24, IS-1), and permits a husband, as the last of three steps, to strike a wife whose ill conduct he fears (4:34, IS-10). For Sunni Islam the hadith treated as primary show captive women taken by their captors and the Prophet selling a slave (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 1456; Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 4138 and 2141, IS-2, IS-3), limit the strike to one that is "not severe" (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 1218a, IS-11), and tell parents to strike children of ten who neglect prayer (Abū Dāwūd 495, IS-9). The same texts forbid cruelty: wives are to be lived with in kindness even when disliked (4:19, IS-14), those under one's hand are to be fed and clothed as oneself and not insulted (Bukhārī 30, IS-8), and God will torment those who torment people (Muslim 2613, IS-8).

*In the tradition's own terms.* Those with power over others answer to God for how they use it. A master who beats a slave without cause must free him (Muslim 1657b, IS-7); owners should grant a contract of self-purchase to slaves who ask, and slave women may not be forced into prostitution (24:33, IS-4); and selling a free person is forbidden (Bukhārī 2227, IS-5). The tradition reads the limits on 4:34, together with the Prophet's example of never striking a woman or a servant and his saying that men who beat their wives "are not the best of you", as a strong preference for not striking (IS-12, IS-15), though no counted text withdraws the permission; Ibn Kathīr reports that the strike must not be violent or touch the face (IS-13). Modern scholars hold that slavery has been abolished and may not be revived, resting on consensus (*ijmāʿ*), a recognised source of Islamic law (2014 open letter of more than 120 scholars, IS-6, not counted). Sunni and Twelver Shia Muslims share the Qur'anic texts; Shia hadith and commentators have not yet been checked, nor have the classical rulings on a captive's consent.

### 11. Sikhism

*What the counted texts say.* The Guru Granth Sahib is the only counted text, and no passage found in it permits owning people, hitting dependants or cruelty. It teaches non-harm in wide terms: speak no harsh word and break no heart, because the True Lord abides in all (ang 1384, SI-1); its ideal person frightens no one (ang 1427, SI-2). It does not address owning people or physical punishment directly.

*In the tradition's own terms.* The scripture teaches through broad principles rather than rules addressed to husbands, parents or masters. It rebukes contempt for women, "from whom kings are born" (ang 473, SI-8), teaches that all were made from one Light so that no one is high and no one low (ang 1349, SI-7), and uses slavery only as an image of complete devotion to God ("I am Your purchased slave", ang 396, SI-6), which the tradition does not read as permission to own anyone. The Sikh Rehat Maryada, the code of conduct approved by the SGPC (the elected body that manages the gurdwaras) for initiated Sikhs, turns this teaching into household rules, such as not killing daughters and not taking money for a child's marriage (SI-13, SI-14), but it is Tier 2 and does not count on its own. Because nothing in the scripture permits these practices, there is no hard text to reconcile; the open question is whether its principles reach owning people and physical punishment.

### 12. Bahá'í Faith

*What the counted texts say.* Bahá'u'lláh forbade trading in slaves and buying another person, since no one should exalt himself above another (Kitáb-i-Aqdas ¶72, BA-1), and made a household maid a free, paid worker (Questions and Answers no. 30, BA-3). He forbade striking another or doing anything that saddens hearts (¶148, BA-6), and 'Abdu'l-Bahá, whose writings the tradition treats as primary, says it is not permissible to strike or vilify a child (Selections no. 95, BA-9).

*In the tradition's own terms.* Bahá'ís hold that 'Abdu'l-Bahá and then Shoghi Effendi were the authorised interpreters of Bahá'u'lláh's writings, and that the Universal House of Justice explains and legislates where the texts leave matters open. Shoghi Effendi reads the Aqdas as prohibiting slavery itself (BA-4). A 1993 letter written on behalf of the House of Justice reads the teachings as forbidding a husband ever to beat his wife and as condemning all child abuse, while accepting physical discipline of children when it is gentle, patient and loving, far from angry or violent beating (BA-12, BA-13); this is why *no hitting* is "unclear" rather than "taught". Small groups that reject the present House of Justice accept the same scripture; their positions have not been researched.

### 13. Secular humanism

*What the counted texts say.* The founding declarations teach a duty not to harm that applies to everyone ("helping and not harming", 2022 Declaration, HU-1; Humanist Manifesto III, HU-2; Amsterdam Declaration 2002, HU-13), and give every person the greatest possible freedom compatible with the rights of others (HU-5, HU-13, HU-14), which rules out being owned by plain reference (`METHOD.md`, section 9, decision 9), though slavery is not named. No counted text speaks to physical punishment.

*In the tradition's own terms.* Humanism has no scripture or revealed law. Its texts are revisable statements agreed by humanist bodies, rooted in human need, and later versions replace earlier ones. No humanist text in any tier was found permitting slavery, cruelty to dependants or physical punishment, so there is nothing to reconcile. The explicit stance against hitting children comes from Humanists International policy and Humanists UK, which apply the declarations' principles but are not counted (HU-11, HU-12). The 1980 Secular Humanist Declaration has not yet been read in full.

## Decomposition

The disagreement is broken into thirty parts, grouped by question. "Type" follows the brief: **Textual** (what the texts say or mean), **Empirical** (a claim about the world), **Logical** (what follows, including whether a tradition's own principles imply something), **Axiomatic** (a foundational premise). Nothing in this table is yet resolved by direct reading of the texts; "Open" means the next step is known but not taken.

**Pattern across the parts.** Three things recur. First, most hard texts *regulate* a practice while limiting it, so much turns on where the line between approving and regulating is drawn (Part 10). Second, several traditions locate the wrong in anger or excess rather than in the blow (Part 24), which suggests a narrower common floor (see below). Third, many later authorities have moved further than their traditions' primary texts, so much of the possible reduction is Logical (Parts 16, 17 and 25).

## Axiomatic residue

None confirmed. Four parts are possibly axiomatic and are not yet tested:

1. **Rank within the household** (Part 29): whether a fixed order of master and slave, husband and wife, parent or teacher and child, with a rightful power to correct, is itself a value.
2. **Freedom as a status** (Part 30): whether freedom from being owned belongs to everyone or only to some.
3. **Where the wrong in a blow lies** (Part 24): whether harm is defined by the act or by the passion behind it.
4. **What later authority can do** (Part 17): whether a permission in a revealed or founding text can be withdrawn by later authority, or only regulated. This is a premise about sources rather than about conduct, but it decides how far the Logical parts can go.

Each would prove reducible if the tradition's own texts and authorities treat the premise as serving some further good that every tradition shares (for example, the good of those below). Each would prove axiomatic if a tradition holds it as a good in itself, or holds that revealed permissions cannot be withdrawn. Part 29 is the same candidate already listed in `HYPOTHESIS.md`.

## Candidate floor (if any)

None of the following has been tested across all thirteen traditions. They are recorded so that the next round of research has a target.

1. **No cruelty** (L1), as drafted in [Article I](../code/articles/article-01-golden-rule.md): taught in seven traditions; turns on Parts 1 to 6.
2. **The lower floor** tried for Article I: "do not kill, maim, starve or torment anyone in your power". Fails on the annex's evidence (Part 7).
3. **Do not kill anyone in your power.** Many hard texts that permit beating still punish killing (for example the rabbinic reading of Exodus 21:20, JU-4, and Seneca's condemnation of lethal beating, ST-9). This belongs with candidate C02 ("do not kill the innocent") and should be tested there.
4. **Do not punish anyone in your power in anger, or out of proportion to the fault.** Suggested by Part 24: texts in at least seven traditions fault punishment given in anger or to excess (Stoicism, Confucianism, Buddhism, Jainism, Christianity for Catholics and Orthodox, the Bahá'í Faith, and Hinduism in Tier 2), and none found approves it. Whether every tradition's counted texts teach it at the Explicit or Clear teaching level has not been checked.
5. **Do not seize or sell a free person.** Taught in counted texts of several traditions, often in the same texts that accept slavery: Exodus 21:16 (JU-5), 1 Timothy 1:10 (CH-5), Bukhārī 2227 (IS-5), Aṅguttara Nikāya 5.177 (BU-4), Kitáb-i-Aqdas ¶72 (BA-1), the humanist freedom clauses (HU-5); in Tier 2, the Ganying Pian (TA-11). Not yet searched in the other traditions.

Floors 4 and 5 are narrower than *no cruelty* and *no one owned*. If either passed, it would not settle this question, but it would show where agreement already lies.
